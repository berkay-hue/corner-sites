      How is it calculated?

      The calculator applies the simple unit economics formulas most commonly used in B2B subscription and recurring service businesses:

      1. CAC (customer acquisition cost) = sales + marketing spend for the period ÷ new customers won in the same period
      2. Monthly gross profit per customer = ARPA × gross margin
      3. Average customer lifetime (months) = 1 ÷ monthly churn
      4. LTV (customer lifetime value) = ARPA × margin ÷ churn
      5. LTV:CAC ratio = LTV ÷ CAC
      6. CAC payback period (months) = CAC ÷ (ARPA × margin)

      The default values are an illustrative, fictional example: a company that spends $90,000 in a quarter to win 12 new customers, earns $1,200 per customer per month, runs at a 70% gross margin and loses 3% of its customers each month. Its CAC is $7,500 and its monthly gross profit per customer is $840. Customers stay 33.3 months on average, LTV comes to $28,000 and the LTV:CAC ratio reaches 3.7. Paying back the cost of acquiring a customer from gross profit takes about 8.9 months.

      LTV uses gross profit rather than revenue, because the direct cost of serving the customer (hosting, support and onboarding in SaaS; the delivery team’s time in service businesses) comes out of the money available to cover acquisition cost. Skipping margin and calculating with revenue alone makes the ratio look better than it is.

      Gathering the right inputs

      What goes into CAC?

      Every cost incurred to win new customers: salaries, commissions and employer costs for the sales and marketing team, ad budget, sales and marketing tool subscriptions, event and trade show costs, content production and outside services. A “paid CAC” calculated from ad spend alone comes out lower but hides the real cost. Try to separate account management and renewal work for existing customers; that is the cost of retention, not of acquiring new customers.

      Spend and customer count should come from the same period. If your sales cycle is long, this month’s spend turns into customers a few months later; in that case, using totals for the last 3–6 months instead of a single month smooths out the result.

      ARPA, margin and churn

      ARPA is the average monthly revenue from active customers; if you sign annual contracts, divide the annual fee by 12. In service businesses you can use the monthly retainer fee or project revenue spread over the contract term. For churn, divide the customers who left during a month by the number of customers at the start of that month. If you only know your annual churn rate, convert it to a monthly figure: 30% annual churn is roughly 2.9% per month. The calculator shows the reverse too: the annual equivalent of the monthly churn you enter appears in the interpretation section.

      How to read the results

      The widely used benchmark for LTV:CAC is 3:1: if you expect gross profit of at least three times the acquisition cost from a customer, the acquisition model is considered healthy. A ratio below 1 means every new customer costs the company money; between 1 and 3, the business works but leaves no margin to fund growth. A very high ratio (say, above 5) is also a signal: you may not be investing enough in growth.

      The payback period tells the cash flow story. Even with a good ratio, if payback takes 18–24 months, every new customer ties up cash for a long time. A common rule of thumb in SaaS is to keep payback under 12 months. If the payback period is longer than the average customer lifetime, customers are leaving before they cover their cost; the calculator warns you when that happens.

      CAC and LTV for service businesses

      The formulas aren’t only for software companies. A service business that works on a monthly retainer, maintenance contract or recurring consulting fee can use the same logic. Enter the monthly contract fee per customer as ARPA and the share of contracts ending each month as churn. The main difference is gross margin: in service businesses, the cost of the work delivered to the customer is mostly the team’s time. Use the share left after delivery team salaries, outsourcing and project tools as your margin; since this rate isn’t as high as in software, the same CAC produces a lower LTV:CAC.

      If you work project by project and customers don’t come back, monthly churn loses its meaning. In that case, you can estimate lifetime by hand from how many months a customer gives you work on average and their average monthly payment, then enter 1 ÷ lifetime as a percentage in the churn field (for example, 10% for an average 10-month relationship).

      Two ways to improve the ratio

      The LTV:CAC ratio moves with two levers. The first is CAC: a better-qualified target list, dropping deals that don’t fit your ideal customer profile early and cutting the time reps spend on research and writing all win more customers for the same spend. The second is LTV: lowering churn grows the ratio directly, because cutting monthly churn from 3% to 2% extends customer lifetime from 33 to 50 months. Raising prices and improving gross margin also lift LTV. Change the values one at a time in the calculator to see which lever works best for you.

      Limits and tips

      • LTV can’t be calculated with 0% churn. Lifetime comes out as infinite; if you haven’t lost a single customer, it usually means your data covers too short a period. Calculate with a conservative floor (for example, 1%).
      • Small numbers mislead. For a team winning 3–5 customers a quarter, a single large deal can cut CAC in half. Use long-term averages.
      • Don’t mix segments. Enterprise and SMB customers have very different ARPA, churn and CAC; a single average misrepresents both. Run the calculation separately for each segment.
      • Upsells are not included. Track expansion revenue from existing customers separately; don’t add it to this simple formula, so LTV isn’t inflated.
      • Channel CAC doesn’t see shared costs. Salaries and tool costs that aren’t split across channels stay in total CAC; channel CAC is good for comparing channels, not for total cost.

      Frequently asked questions

      What should the LTV:CAC ratio be?

      The widely used benchmark is 3:1. Below 1 means every new customer loses money, between 1 and 3 calls for caution, and 3 or above points to a healthy acquisition model. If the ratio is very high, you may be underinvesting in growth.

      Which costs should go into CAC?

      All sales and marketing costs aimed at winning new customers: team salaries, commissions and employer costs, advertising, tool subscriptions, events and outside services. Keep renewal and account management costs for existing customers separate.

      Why isn't LTV calculated when churn is 0%?

      LTV is monthly gross profit divided by churn; if churn is zero, customer lifetime and LTV come out as infinite. If you haven't lost any customers, assume your data covers too short a period and calculate with a conservative value such as 1%.

      How is the CAC payback period calculated?

      Divide CAC by monthly gross profit per customer (ARPA × gross margin). For example, with a CAC of $7,500 and monthly gross profit of $840, payback takes about 8.9 months.

      Can service businesses use this calculator too?

      Yes. For consulting and service businesses that work on a monthly retainer or recurring contract, enter the monthly contract fee as ARPA, the share left after delivery team costs as gross margin, and the monthly contract termination rate as churn.

      Are the values I enter saved?

      No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser; values are not sent to a server and no sign-up is required.
