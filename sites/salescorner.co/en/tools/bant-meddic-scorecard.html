BANT / MEDDIC deal qualification scorecard

Score your deal 0–3 against BANT or MEDDIC criteria and instantly see the total score, the qualification level and the questions to ask on your next call for the two weakest criteria. Copy the summary in one click and paste it straight into your CRM notes.

Score the deal

Only added to the copied summary; never saved anywhere.

BANT looks at four criteria: budget, authority, need and timing. Maximum 12 points.

Budget

Ask: Is there a budget set aside for this (or one that can be freed up), and is the range known?

Authority

Ask: Who makes the decision, who signs, and what role does your contact play in the process?

Need

Ask: Is the business problem clear, how is it handled today and what is it costing them?

Timing

Ask: Is there a date for the decision and rollout, and what concrete event is driving that date?

MEDDIC looks at six criteria: metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, identify pain and champion. Maximum 18 points.

Metrics

Ask: Have you agreed with the customer which number will change, and by how much, if the project succeeds?

Economic Buyer

Ask: Is it clear who gives final sign-off on the spend and can veto it, and are you in contact with them?

Decision Criteria

Ask: Have the criteria for comparing the options been written down or openly discussed?

Decision Process

Ask: Are the steps to a decision, their order, the approval points and the timelines known?

Identify Pain

Ask: Are the problem forcing the customer to act and its business impact clear, in the customer’s own words?

Champion

Ask: Is there someone with internal influence who gains personally from this project and speaks up for you?

    How is the score calculated?

    Each criterion is scored from 0 to 3. BANT has four criteria, so the maximum is 12; MEDDIC has six, so the maximum is 18. The total is divided by the maximum to get a percentage, and the level is set by that percentage:

    • Weak (below 40%): The deal is missing the basics. Instead of writing a proposal at this stage, go back to discovery.
    • Developing (40–64%): Some criteria are clear, but the picture you need for a decision isn’t complete yet.
    • Strong (65–84%): Most criteria are concrete. Closing the one or two remaining gaps sets how fast the deal moves.
    • Qualified (85% and above): Almost every criterion has been confirmed by the customer. You have solid ground to move to the proposal and close plan.

    There is one more rule: if any criterion is marked None (0), the level is capped at Developing, however high the total. For example, a deal with clear budget, need and timing but a completely unknown decision-maker scores 9/12 on paper; in practice, you can’t commit to a close date without knowing who will sign. Criteria you leave unmarked count as 0 and are flagged separately in the result.

    Recommendations come from the two lowest-scoring criteria. When scores are tied, the order of the criteria in the list decides. For each criterion, two questions from a pre-written list are shown; they are not a script, but a reminder of the topics you need to open up on the call.

    Illustrative example (fictional): In BANT, Budget 2 (Partial), Authority 1 (Unclear), Need 3 (Clear) and Timing 2 (Partial) add up to 8/12, or 67%, which gives a Strong level. Since the two weakest criteria are Authority and Budget, the tool suggests asking first about the people in the decision process on your next call, then which budget line the money will come from and who has to approve it.

    What does the 0–3 scale mean?

    For the score to mean anything, everyone on the team has to use the scale the same way. These are the definitions we recommend:

    • 0 · None: The topic hasn’t come up at all, or you have no information.
    • 1 · Unclear: The topic came up, but the answer stayed vague or rests on your own guess (“budget probably won’t be an issue”).
    • 2 · Partial: There is concrete information, but it’s incomplete or relies on one person’s word and hasn’t been confirmed by another source.
    • 3 · Clear: The information came from the customer in their own words, was verified and written down. Someone else could read the note and reach the same conclusion.

    The most common mistake is optimistic scoring: marking a criterion “Clear” when it should be “Partial” just because the call went well. If you’re not sure, pick the lower score. The point of the scorecard isn’t to make the deal look good; it’s to make missing information visible.

    BANT or MEDDIC?

    BANT is a quick first filter. It’s ideal for deciding after the first discovery call whether a deal deserves a place in your pipeline, and it is often enough for lower-value sales with a short decision process. Budget, authority, need and timing questions land more naturally when you weave them into the conversation rather than asking them in order.

    MEDDIC is designed for enterprise sales that involve multiple stakeholders, have approval steps and can take months. It splits BANT’s “authority” question in two: the person who makes the decision (the economic buyer) and the person who speaks for you inside the company (the champion) are different people. By probing decision criteria and the decision process separately, it also shows you earlier why a deal is stuck.

    A practical approach: screen new deals with BANT, and track high-value deals that reach the proposal stage with MEDDIC. This tool does not include the extra MEDDPICC criteria (paper process and competition); it scores plain MEDDIC.

    How to use the scorecard with your team

    The scorecard works best when the whole team speaks the same language. In the weekly pipeline review, discussing each deal’s score and its two weakest criteria, instead of walking through every deal story, shifts the conversation from “how is this deal going?” to “what information is missing on this deal?”. The manager’s coaching question gets sharper too: if Authority shows as “Unclear”, the topic is how you’ll surface the people in the decision process on the next call.

    MEDDIC’s economic buyer and champion criteria can’t be scored properly without knowing the people in the deal. That’s why it pays to use the scorecard alongside a decision-maker map: the map shows who approves, who influences and who might push back, while the scorecard shows how well the information about those people has been verified. Add the copied summary to the deal note in your CRM and you’ll see at a glance what’s missing before the next call.

    Limits and tips

    • The score is not a forecast. A high score doesn’t guarantee the deal will be won; it only shows how much of the information you need for the sale you already have. For win probability, look at your own historical data.
    • All criteria carry equal weight. In your sales motion, some criteria may matter more than others. If so, read the weakest-criteria list through your own priorities.
    • Rescore after every important call. How the score changes over time tells you more than any single score. A score that stays flat for weeks is the earliest sign of a stalled deal.
    • Look back at lost deals. Seeing which criterion stayed low on deals you lost is the fastest way to start a lost-deal analysis.
    • Don’t confuse it with lead scoring. Lead scoring measures interest and fit before a conversation; this scorecard measures qualification after it.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between BANT and MEDDIC?

    BANT is a fast first-pass filter built on four criteria: budget, authority, need and timing. MEDDIC goes deeper with six criteria, separately probing the measurable gain, the economic buyer who approves the spend, the decision criteria, the decision process, the customer’s pain and the internal champion. BANT suits short sales cycles; MEDDIC suits enterprise deals with many stakeholders.

    What score counts as a qualified deal?

    On this scorecard, 85% and above shows as Qualified, 65–84% as Strong, 40–64% as Developing and below 40% as Weak. If any criterion is marked None, the level is capped at Developing. Compare these thresholds with your team’s past won and lost deals and interpret them for your own sales motion.

    What is the difference between Unclear and Partial?

    Unclear means the topic came up but the answer stayed vague or rests on your own guess. Partial means there is concrete information, but it is incomplete or relies on a single person’s word. Clear requires the information to come from the customer in their own words and to be verified.

    When should I fill in the scorecard?

    Fill it in right after the first discovery call and update it after every important conversation. How the score moves over the weeks tells you more than any single score; a score that stays flat for a long time is a sign the deal has stalled.

    Why MEDDIC and not MEDDPICC?

    MEDDPICC adds paper process and competition to MEDDIC. To keep things simple, this tool scores only the six MEDDIC criteria. If procurement and contract steps are decisive in your sales, you can factor them into the decision process criterion.

    Are the values I enter saved?

    No. Scoring happens entirely in your browser; the deal name and your selections are never sent to a server or stored, and no sign-up is required. Your selections are cleared when you close the page.
