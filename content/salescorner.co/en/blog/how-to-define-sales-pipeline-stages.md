Open the CRM of almost any B2B sales team and you'll see the same picture: vague stages like "Contacted," "Interested," "Hot," and "Proposal to be sent," with hundreds of opportunities that have been sitting in the same column for months. The sales manager asks for a forecast in the weekly meeting, and reps answer, "It looks like it'll close this month." The problem usually isn't the team. It's that **the pipeline stages were never clearly defined**.

In this article, we walk step by step through how to define your sales pipeline stages. We explain how to write entry and exit criteria for each stage and how to assign probability and duration values. We also share concrete examples from three scenarios, ranging from outbound SaaS sales to long-cycle enterprise services sales. The goal is to turn your CRM from a notebook into **a reliable forecasting and management tool**.

## What Is a Pipeline Stage and Why Does It Matter So Much?

Pipeline stages are the **observable and verifiable** milestones a sales opportunity passes through from first contact to close. The key word here is "verifiable." "The customer seems interested" is a feeling. "The customer attended a demo with the budget owner, and a second meeting is on the calendar" is a fact.

The sales funnel and the pipeline are often confused. A funnel is a conversion model that generally shows the total volume across marketing and sales: how many visitors, how many leads, how many opportunities, how many customers. A pipeline is an operational view that shows **where each individual opportunity stands in the sales process**. The funnel answers "how many?" The pipeline answers "which one, where, and when?"

Well-defined stages give you:

- **Reliable sales forecasts:** When each stage has a realistic close probability, your weighted pipeline value becomes meaningful.
- **Bottleneck detection:** You can see which stage opportunities pile up in or drop out of.
- **A shared language:** When someone says "in the proposal stage," everyone understands the same thing.
- **Coaching opportunities:** If a rep's progression from discovery to demo is weak, you can pinpoint the exact training need.
- **A foundation for automation:** Without clear criteria, you can't safely automate any workflow.

## The Five Components of a Well-Defined Stage

Giving a stage a name isn't enough. For each stage, you should define the following five components in writing.

### 1. Entry Criteria

**What must have happened** for an opportunity to move into this stage. The criteria should be based on an action by the buyer, not the seller. For example, instead of "We sent a proposal," use "The customer requested a proposal and confirmed the pricing scope."

### 2. Exit Criteria

**The conditions that must be met** for the opportunity to move to the next stage. Often, one stage's exit criteria are the next stage's entry criteria. Still, writing them separately clarifies what the rep needs to do in this stage.

### 3. Owner or Role

Who manages the opportunity at this stage? The SDR, the account executive, or the solutions consultant? Without clear handoff points, opportunities get lost between roles.

### 4. Close Probability

The rate at which opportunities in this stage have **historically** been won. You can start with estimated values. Once you've collected a few quarters of data, you should update these rates with actual conversions.

### 5. Maximum Time in Stage

The **reasonable upper limit** for how long an opportunity can stay in this stage. A rule such as "any opportunity in discovery for more than 14 days is automatically added to the review list" keeps the pipeline from bloating.

## A 7-Step Process for Defining Pipeline Stages

### Step 1: Review Recent Won Deals in Reverse

Start from your real data, not from theory. Take your last 15-20 won opportunities and, for each one, list the **key events** from first contact to signature: first reply, discovery call, technical evaluation, budget approval, legal review, and so on. You'll start to see common patterns.

### Step 2: Map the Buyer's Journey

Your sales process should match the buyer's purchasing process. On the buyer's side, the steps usually are: recognizing the problem, researching solution options, evaluating vendors, getting internal approval, and purchasing. Each pipeline stage should correspond to a **moment of progress** in this journey.

### Step 3: Limit the Number of Stages

In practice, **5 to 7 stages** is ideal for most B2B teams. Fewer than three doesn't give you enough visibility, and more than nine wears reps out with CRM updates. Closed Won and Closed Lost are not included in this count.

### Step 4: Write Verifiable Criteria for Each Stage

Put every criterion through this test: "Could someone else verify that this criterion has been met by looking at the CRM record?" If the answer is no, make the criterion more concrete. Look for evidence such as meeting notes, a calendar invite, a document that was sent, or the name of the decision-maker.

### Step 5: Tie Your Qualification Methodology to the Stages

If you use BANT, MEDDIC, SPICED, or another framework, build it into your stages. For example, if you use MEDDIC, "Metrics" and "Identify Pain" could be exit criteria for the discovery stage. "Economic Buyer" and "Decision Criteria" could be required to exit the solution presentation stage.

### Step 6: Assign Probability and Duration Values

Use reasonable assumptions in the first version. After a quarter, compare them with actual data. You'll see how this works in the examples below.

### Step 7: Document, Train, and Review Every 90 Days

Write up your stage definitions as a one-page "pipeline glossary" and add them to each stage's description field in your CRM. Give the team a short training session. At the end of each quarter, look at conversion rates and, if needed, merge or split stages or tighten the criteria.

## Example 1: An Outbound-Driven B2B SaaS Pipeline

Let's consider a SaaS company with a mid-range average annual contract value and a 30-60 day sales cycle. Most of its opportunities come from outbound campaigns run through cold email and LinkedIn.

### Stage 0: Target Account (Prospect)

- **Entry criteria:** The account fits the ideal customer profile (ICP), for example industry, 50-500 employees, and use of a specific technology. Contact details for at least one decision-maker have been verified.
- **Owner:** SDR or automation system.
- **Note:** This stage is often kept separate as a "prospecting list" rather than part of the pipeline. To build the right list, contact and company data can be pulled with firmographic filters and enriched from data platforms like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya).

### Stage 1: Reply Received / Meeting Booked

- **Entry criteria:** The buyer replied positively or neutrally, and a discovery meeting is on the calendar.
- **Exit criteria:** The meeting took place.
- **Probability:** 10%
- **Maximum time:** 10 days. If the meeting is postponed and not rescheduled, the opportunity is moved to a "re-engagement" sequence.

Feeding this stage depends heavily on email quality. For an in-depth look at personalized messaging, check out our [guide to writing cold emails with AI](/blog/how-to-write-cold-emails-with-ai). You can also try our [Sequence Writer tool](/sekans-yazari) to generate sequences automatically from your product sources.

### Stage 2: Discovery Completed

- **Entry criteria:** The discovery call took place. A concrete problem the buyer wants to solve and its business impact have been recorded.
- **Exit criteria:** Basic information about the budget range and decision process has been gathered. A demo date has been set.
- **Probability:** 20%
- **Owner:** The handoff from SDR to account executive happens at this stage.
- **Maximum time:** 14 days.

### Stage 3: Demo / Solution Presentation

- **Entry criteria:** The demo took place, and at least one decision-maker attended.
- **Exit criteria:** The buyer requested a proposal or pricing. If a technical evaluation is needed, its scope has been defined.
- **Probability:** 40%
- **Maximum time:** 21 days.

### Stage 4: Proposal Sent

- **Entry criteria:** A written proposal within the scope approved by the buyer has been delivered, and a proposal review meeting has been scheduled.
- **Exit criteria:** The buyer has started negotiating price and terms or has given verbal approval.
- **Probability:** 60%
- **Maximum time:** 14 days.

### Stage 5: Negotiation / Contract

- **Entry criteria:** Commercial terms are being discussed, and a draft contract has been sent to legal or procurement.
- **Exit criteria:** Signature.
- **Probability:** 80%
- **Maximum time:** 21 days.

### Closing Stages

- **Closed Won:** The contract has been signed, and payment or order confirmation has been received.
- **Closed Lost:** A loss reason must be selected as a required field, for example price, competitor, timing, couldn't reach the decision-maker, or no need.

## Example 2: Long-Cycle Enterprise Services Sales

Now let's look at a different scenario: a firm selling consulting or integration projects to large enterprises. The sales cycle lasts 4-9 months, 5-8 stakeholders are involved in the decision, and procurement and legal processes play a decisive role. Here, the stages should focus more on **stakeholder progression**.

1. **Initial Contact Made (5%):** A first meeting was held with a sponsor or potential champion at the target organization.
2. **Need Validated (15%):** The scope of the problem, the affected business units, and the cost of not solving the problem have been documented. The champion has agreed to own the process internally.
3. **Stakeholder Map Completed (25%):** The economic buyer, technical evaluator, and procurement lead have been identified. At least one meeting has been held with the economic buyer.
4. **Solution Design / Workshop (40%):** Joint working sessions were held with the customer, and the solution scope was defined together. At this stage, the parties usually agree on a "success criteria" document.
5. **Formal Proposal and Project Plan (55%):** A proposal including pricing, timeline, and resource plan has been presented. The budget approval process has started on the customer side.
6. **Procurement and Legal (75%):** Vendor onboarding, contract negotiation, and security reviews are underway.
7. **Awaiting Signature (90%):** Agreement has been reached on all terms, and the signing process has been initiated.

In this model, maximum time in stage is longer. For example, the "Procurement and Legal" stage can take 45-60 days. That's why an activity rule such as **"days since the last meaningful customer interaction"** works better than a time-in-stage rule. If there's no response from the customer for 21 days, the opportunity is added to the risk list regardless of its stage.

## Example 3: Fast-Cycle SMB and Inbound Sales

The third scenario is a team selling a low-to-mid-priced product to SMBs, with most opportunities coming from inbound forms or free trials. The sales cycle is 7-21 days. Here, too many stages kill speed.

1. **New Lead (5%):** A form was submitted or a trial account was created.
2. **Qualified (20%):** Company size and use case are a fit, and the need was confirmed via a short call or email.
3. **Active Evaluation (45%):** The defined "activation event" occurred in the trial account, for example the first integration was set up or at least three team members were invited.
4. **Pricing Discussion (70%):** Plan selection and payment terms are being discussed.
5. **Closed Won / Closed Lost**

The key point in this model is that most stage transitions depend on **in-product behavior**. This makes automation almost essential. When product data flows into the CRM, the opportunity stage can be updated without rep intervention.

## Probabilities and Weighted Pipeline: A Worked Example

Let's look at the practical benefit of assigning probabilities to stages with a simple calculation. Suppose the SaaS company in Example 1 has the following opportunities in its pipeline:

- 20 opportunities in Stage 1 × average 100,000 TL × 10% = **200,000 TL**
- 12 opportunities in Stage 2 × 100,000 TL × 20% = **240,000 TL**
- 8 opportunities in Stage 3 × 100,000 TL × 40% = **320,000 TL**
- 5 opportunities in Stage 4 × 100,000 TL × 60% = **300,000 TL**
- 3 opportunities in Stage 5 × 100,000 TL × 80% = **240,000 TL**

While the total raw pipeline appears to be 4,800,000 TL, **the weighted pipeline is 1,300,000 TL**. The forecast presented to the board should be based on this second figure, or better yet, on a version filtered to opportunities with close dates in the current quarter.

Now imagine you look at actual data a quarter later. Say only 7 of the 30 opportunities that entered Stage 3 were won. That puts the real probability at roughly 23%, not the 40% you assumed. This gap points to one of two things. Either you need to lower the probability, or the entry criteria for the demo stage are too loose and unqualified opportunities are being pushed into demos. More often than not, the right answer is to **tighten the criteria**.

## Common Mistakes When Defining Pipeline Stages

### Stages Based on Seller Actions

Stages like "Email sent," "Called," or "Preparing proposal" show what the rep did, not how far the buyer has progressed. The purpose of the pipeline is to measure buyer progress. Activities should be logged as activity records.

### Emotional or Subjective Names

Names like "Hot," "Warm," or "Hopeful" mean different things to different people. The same opportunity is hot for one rep and warm for another. If you need these kinds of labels, use them as a separate "priority" field instead of as stages.

### Allowing Stage Skipping

An opportunity sometimes genuinely moves straight from discovery to proposal. But if this becomes systematic, either the intermediate stage is unnecessary or the criteria aren't being enforced. Requiring an explanation field in the CRM whenever a stage is skipped makes this visible.

### Not Recording Loss Reasons

Lost opportunities are your pipeline's most valuable teachers. Have reps select loss reasons from a **predefined list** instead of free text. Also report on which stage each opportunity was lost from. For example, if most losses happen at the proposal stage due to "price," the problem most likely isn't the proposal stage itself, but that value wasn't established well enough during discovery.

### Never Cleaning Up the Pipeline

Opportunities that haven't moved in months inflate the weighted pipeline and distort the forecast. Run a "zombie deal" cleanup every month. Close opportunities that have exceeded their maximum time in stage and had no customer interaction in the last 30 days, or move them into a re-engagement sequence.

## Managing Pipeline Stages with AI and Automation

Clearly defined stages are where automation works best. When criteria are verifiable, the system can take over many stage transitions and follow-up tasks. A few practical examples:

- **Meeting-based stage updates:** When a discovery meeting is created in the calendar tool, the opportunity automatically moves to Stage 1. Once the meeting happens, the rep is sent a discovery notes template.
- **AI call summaries:** The call recording is transcribed. A language model extracts fields such as the problem, budget signals, decision-makers, and next steps from it and writes them to the CRM. It then notifies the rep which discovery-stage exit criteria are still missing.
- **Time-in-stage alerts:** When an opportunity exceeds its maximum time in a stage, an alert goes to the rep and their manager via Slack or email.
- **Data enrichment:** When a new opportunity is created, the company's employee count, industry, and tech stack are filled in automatically. This allows ICP fit to be checked at stage entry.
- **Post-loss re-engagement:** Opportunities lost due to "timing" are automatically enrolled in a personalized email sequence after 90 days.

## Key Pipeline Metrics to Track

Once you've defined your stages, these metrics show you the health of the system:

- **Stage-to-stage conversion rate:** What percentage of opportunities entering each stage move on to the next?
- **Average time in stage:** Which stage is slowing the process down?
- **Pipeline velocity:** Number of opportunities × win rate × average deal value ÷ sales cycle length. For example, 40 opportunities, a 20% win rate, a 100,000 TL average deal value, and a 50-day cycle give you a pipeline velocity of roughly 16,000 TL per day.
- **Pipeline coverage ratio:** How many times your period target does your pipeline cover? If your win rate is 25%, you'll need qualified pipeline of roughly 4 times your target.
- **Loss reasons by stage:** Where and why losses happen.

Review these metrics monthly at the rep level as well. If a rep's post-demo conversion is clearly below the team average, the problem most likely lies in the demo structure or in information left uncovered during discovery.

## Conclusion

Defining your sales pipeline stages isn't a CRM setting; it's **designing the backbone of your sales process**. Every stage should have entry and exit criteria based on buyer actions, a clear owner, a probability grounded in real data, and a maximum time in stage. Start with 5-7 stages depending on your business model. As you've seen with outbound SaaS, enterprise services, and SMB sales, stages can vary, but the principles stay the same: verifiability, simplicity, and regular review.

## FAQ

### How many stages should a sales pipeline have?
For most B2B teams, 5 to 7 stages is ideal, not counting Won and Lost. Fewer than three stages don't give enough visibility, and more than nine wear reps out with CRM updates.

### What should a well-defined pipeline stage include?
Every stage should have five components: an entry criterion, an exit criterion, an owner, a probability and a maximum time. The entry criterion should be based on an action taken by the buyer, not the sales rep.

### What is the difference between a sales funnel and a pipeline?
A funnel answers "how many?", while a pipeline answers "which one, where and when?". The funnel is a volume-based conversion model; the pipeline is an operational view of each individual opportunity.

### How do you set stage probabilities?
Start with estimated probabilities and compare them with actual results after a quarter. If 7 of 30 opportunities in a stage were won, the real rate is about 23%, not 40%; in that case you usually tighten the stage's criteria.
