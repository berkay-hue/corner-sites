A sales commission plan is a written set of rules that defines **who earns variable pay, for which results, how much, and when**. A good plan comes together in five steps: clarify the company goal, separate the roles, set on-target earnings (OTE) and the fixed-to-variable split, tie in the quota, and put the commission rate, threshold, accelerators and payout rules in writing. The healthiest plans are simple. A sales rep should be able to tell you what they will earn from a closed deal without reaching for a calculator.

In this guide, we walk you step by step through building a commission plan for B2B sales teams. We share sample calculations in Turkish lira for SDR, Account Executive and Account Manager roles. We also cover issues specific to Turkey, such as collections, exchange rates and inflation. The goal is to help you build a plan that steers your sales team toward the right behavior, protects company profitability and doesn't spark disputes.

## Why is a sales commission plan so important?

A commission plan is more than a payout table. It is the document that most clearly tells your sales team "what we value at this company." Reps spend their time on whatever the plan rewards. That behavior is rational.

A few typical examples:

- If the plan rewards revenue alone, reps close quickly with steep discounts, and margins erode.
- If the plan rewards signed contracts but ignores collections, you end up with a portfolio full of customers who struggle to pay.
- If SDRs are rewarded on "number of meetings" but meeting quality isn't measured, calendars fill up while the pipeline doesn't.

In short, a commission plan is a **behavior design** tool. First you define the behavior you want, then you tie the money to that behavior.

## What questions should you answer before building a commission plan?

Before getting into formulas, answer the following questions in writing as a leadership team. Plans built before these answers are clear usually get revised in the first quarter.

1. **What is this year's number one sales goal?** New customer acquisition, expansion within existing accounts, profitability, or entry into a new market?
2. **Who exactly makes the sale?** Does one person manage it end to end, or is there a handoff between SDR, AE and Account Manager?
3. **How long is your sales cycle?** A deal that closes in three weeks and an enterprise deal that closes in nine months can't be managed on the same payout schedule.
4. **How is revenue recognized?** Which do you use: annual upfront payment, monthly subscription, project-based invoicing or deferred-payment collections?
5. **Do you trust your data?** Are the opportunity amounts, close dates and opportunity owners in your CRM accurate?

The last question is often underestimated. If commission is tied to CRM data, that data now feeds into payroll. [Without clearly defined sales pipeline stages](/blog/how-to-define-sales-pipeline-stages), even the definition of a "closed opportunity" can become contentious.

## How do you build a sales commission plan, step by step?

### 1. Write the purpose of the plan in a single sentence

Boil the plan's purpose down to one sentence for each role. For example:

- **SDR:** "Increase the number of qualified meetings accepted by the sales team."
- **Account Executive:** "Win new annual contract value in the target segment, above the defined minimum margin."
- **Account Manager:** "Renew and expand existing customers."

Down the line, this sentence serves as the referee in "should we include this in commission?" debates.

### 2. Separate roles and crediting rules

If more than one person contributes to the same deal, write down in advance who gets how much credit. Common approaches include:

- The SDR receives credit the moment the meeting is **accepted by the sales team**. They can also receive an additional bonus when the deal closes.
- The AE receives credit when the contract is signed and the **first payment is collected**.
- The Account Manager receives credit for renewals and additional sales. However, expansion within the first 90 days can still be credited to the AE.

If you work by region or segment, your [lead assignment rules](/blog/lead-assignment-rules), which determine which lead goes to whom, become a direct part of your commission plan. Most "this customer was actually mine" disputes stem from unclear assignment rules.

### 3. Set on-target earnings (OTE) and the fixed-to-variable split

OTE (On-Target Earnings) is the total annual amount a rep earns when they hit exactly 100% of quota. It is the sum of base salary and target variable pay.

The fixed-to-variable split is determined by how directly the role influences sales results:

- **New-business AE (hunter):** Typically between 50/50 and 60/40
- **SDR / BDR:** Typically between 70/30 and 80/20
- **Account Manager:** Typically between 70/30 and 80/20
- **Sales engineer / pre-sales:** Typically between 80/20 and 90/10

These ranges are a starting point, not hard rules. As sales cycles get longer and deals get bigger, the fixed portion can be increased. Otherwise, reps are stuck on low income for months because of timing they can't control.

**Note for Turkey:** In a high-inflation environment, keeping base salary very low means tying a rep's basic needs to variable pay. This encourages short-term, aggressively discounted sales. Make sure the fixed portion doesn't fall below the market rate.

### 4. Set the quota and link it to OTE

A commission plan can't be considered separately from the quota. If the quota is too high, nobody earns commission, no matter how generous the plan looks. If the quota is too low, the company overpays.

Quota setting should take pipeline capacity, historical conversion rates and ramp times into account together. We covered this in detail, with formulas, in our [guide to setting sales quotas](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quota). From a commission plan perspective, the key ratio to check is:

**Quota-to-OTE ratio.** How many times OTE is an AE's annual quota? Many B2B teams use 4x to 6x as a starting point. However, the right ratio depends on your gross margin and customer lifetime value. If you sell a low-margin product, this ratio needs to be higher.

Here is a practical way to test whether your quota is realistic: if roughly 60-70% of the team can hit quota, the quota is balanced. If only a very small share of the team gets there, the problem most likely lies with the quota, not the reps.

### 5. Calculate the base commission rate

The base commission rate is found with a simple formula:

**Commission rate = Target variable pay / Quota**

Example:

- Annual target variable pay: 480,000 TL
- Annual quota: 6,000,000 TL in new contract value
- Base commission rate: 480,000 / 6,000,000 = **8%**

This formula guarantees the plan's internal consistency. A rep who hits quota receives exactly the variable pay they were promised.

### 6. Set threshold, accelerator and cap rules

A flat-rate commission is simple but has two weaknesses. It rewards reps who fall far short of quota, and it doesn't sufficiently motivate those who exceed it. That's why most plans add three mechanisms:

- **Threshold:** No commission is paid, or a lower rate applies, below a certain percentage of quota. For example, 50% of quota.
- **Accelerator:** The rate increases once quota is exceeded. For example, 1.5x for 100-130% and 2x above 130%.
- **Cap:** An upper limit on total commission. Caps are generally not recommended, because they push your best reps into "that's enough for this quarter, I'll save the rest for next period" behavior. A healthier approach is to write a separate approval rule for exceptionally large deals.

### 7. Write down payout timing and clawback rules

This is the area that causes the most disputes in B2B sales in Turkey. Here's what needs to be clarified:

- **When is commission earned?** At contract signing, at invoicing or at collection? In industries where deferred-payment sales and payment by check are common, **collection-based** payouts protect the company.
- **What amount is it calculated on?** The net amount excluding VAT? Are setup fees, hardware and third-party licenses included?
- **Exchange rates for foreign-currency contracts:** Which date's exchange rate will be used to convert commission into TL? The signing date or the collection date? If you don't write this down, currency fluctuations will spark a new argument every month.
- **Payout period:** Monthly or quarterly? Monthly payouts are common for short-cycle sales, and quarterly payouts for long-cycle enterprise sales.
- **Clawback rule:** If the customer cancels the contract or fails to pay within the first 90 days, the commission already paid is deducted from the next payout.
- **Departures:** When a rep leaves, will they receive commission on deals that haven't yet been collected?

### 8. Put the plan in writing and get it signed

A commission plan shouldn't remain a verbal agreement. Prepare a plan document for each rep that includes:

- The plan period (e.g., January 1 - December 31)
- Role, quota and OTE
- Commission rates, threshold and accelerators
- Crediting, payout and clawback rules
- Management's right to change the plan and the notice period
- At least two calculated example scenarios

For the tax and payroll treatment of variable pay, be sure to work with your accountant and, if needed, your employment law advisor. Setting it up as an addendum to the employment contract protects both parties in any future disputes.

## Which commission models can you use?

Depending on how your business is structured, you can choose one of the following models or a combination of several.

**Flat-rate commission.** A fixed percentage is paid on every sale. It's the simplest model and suits small teams and new sales functions whose quotas haven't settled yet.

**Tiered commission.** Rates increase based on percentage of quota attained. It rewards high performance and is the most common B2B model.

**Gross profit-based commission.** Commission is calculated on gross profit rather than revenue. It's ideal for companies where reps have discounting authority, cost structures vary from deal to deal, and sales are project-based. It naturally keeps reps disciplined about discounting.

**Management by objectives (MBO) bonus.** Part of the commission is tied to non-numerical goals. Example goals: winning the first 3 reference customers in a new vertical, or maintaining CRM data quality at a defined level. Keep this model limited to a small portion of total variable pay. Otherwise, the plan becomes subjective.

**Product or segment multipliers.** The commission rate is increased for strategically prioritized products or segments. For example, a 1.2x multiplier could apply to deals from companies that fully match your ideal customer profile. This approach is extremely effective [if you've clearly defined your ideal customer profile (ICP)](/blog/how-to-define-ideal-customer-profile-b2b).

## Sample plan: Tiered commission for an Account Executive

The following example is designed for an AE responsible for new customer acquisition. The figures are for illustration only.

**Key parameters:**

- Monthly base salary: 60,000 TL
- Monthly target variable pay: 40,000 TL (60/40 split)
- Monthly OTE: 100,000 TL, annual OTE: 1,200,000 TL
- Annual quota: 6,000,000 TL, quarterly quota: 1,500,000 TL
- Quarterly target variable pay: 120,000 TL
- Base rate: 8%

**Tier table:**

- Below 50% of quota: no commission
- 50% – 100%: **8%** from the first lira
- 100% – 130%: **12%** on the portion above quota (1.5x accelerator)
- Above 130%: **16%** on the excess portion (2x accelerator)

**Scenario A – Below quota (46%):** The rep brings in 700,000 TL in collected new sales for the quarter. Because they don't reach the 750,000 TL threshold, they earn no commission. If your team is newly formed or your sales cycle is long, you might consider applying a low rate (e.g., 4%) below the threshold as well.

**Scenario B – At quota (100%):** 1,500,000 TL × 8% = **120,000 TL** in commission. The rep receives exactly the target variable pay.

**Scenario C – Above quota (130%):** 1,950,000 TL in sales.

- First 1,500,000 TL × 8% = 120,000 TL
- Next 450,000 TL × 12% = 54,000 TL
- Total: **174,000 TL**

**Scenario D – High performance (150%):** 2,250,000 TL in sales.

- 1,500,000 TL × 8% = 120,000 TL
- 450,000 TL × 12% = 54,000 TL
- 300,000 TL × 16% = 48,000 TL
- Total: **222,000 TL**

Put this table into the plan document exactly as is. Reps should be able to calculate where they stand within minutes.

## How should SDR commission be structured?

Since SDRs don't close deals themselves, revenue-based commission isn't the right tool for them. Instead, choose a metric they can control that also captures quality.

**Sample SDR plan:**

- Monthly base salary: 35,000 TL
- Monthly target variable pay: 15,000 TL (70/30 split)
- Monthly target: 20 **accepted qualified meetings**
- Payout per meeting: 15,000 / 20 = **750 TL**
- Payout per meeting above target: **1,125 TL** (1.5x)
- Additional bonus: **2,000 TL** per deal when an opportunity sourced by the SDR closes

**Calculation:** The SDR books 26 accepted meetings during the month, and 2 opportunities sourced from meetings in previous periods close this month.

- 20 × 750 TL = 15,000 TL
- 6 × 1,125 TL = 6,750 TL
- 2 × 2,000 TL = 4,000 TL
- Total variable pay: **25,750 TL**

The critical point here is the definition of **"accepted."** A meeting should count once the AE confirms it meets specific criteria. These criteria might include ICP fit, decision-maker attendance and a defined need. Otherwise, there will be constant friction between SDRs and AEs. As you think about balancing SDR activity goals with outcome goals, our guide on [KPIs for measuring sales team productivity](/blog/how-to-measure-sales-team-productivity-kpis) can point you in the right direction.

Some companies prefer to outsource their outbound function rather than build it in-house. In that case, a similar outcome-based payment logic can be set up. ## What should commission look like for Account Managers?

In roles that manage existing customers, two different behaviors need to be rewarded separately: **retention** and **expansion**.

**Sample structure:**

- On renewed contract value: **2-3%**
- On add-on sales and upsells (new revenue): **7-8%**
- A 20% reduction in expansion commission if gross revenue retention falls below a defined level

This structure prevents Account Managers from neglecting renewals and focusing solely on add-on sales. It also clearly communicates that renewal is an expected outcome and that the real reward lies in growth.

## How much should commission cost?

To determine whether the commission plan is sustainable for the company, look at the **cost of sales ratio**:

**Cost of sales ratio = (Base salary + Variable pay + Benefits and employer costs) / New revenue brought in by the rep**

In our AE example:

- Annual OTE: 1,200,000 TL
- Employer costs and benefits (assume 25% for illustration): 300,000 TL
- Total cost: 1,500,000 TL
- Quota: 6,000,000 TL
- Cost of sales ratio: **25%**

Whether this ratio is acceptable depends on three factors: your gross margin, how many years customers stay, and how much they expand after the initial deal. For high-margin, long-lived subscription revenue, this ratio may be reasonable. For low-margin, one-off project sales, it could push the company into a loss.

Before approving the plan, calculate the total commission burden under three scenarios: bad, expected and good. Doing this alongside a realistic [pipeline-based sales forecast](/blog/sales-forecasting-from-pipeline) helps the finance team plan the budget accurately.

## What are the most common mistakes in commission plans?

- **Using too many metrics.** Nobody can keep a plan with more than five components in their head. Two or three metrics per role is enough.
- **Changing the plan frequently mid-year.** Every change erodes trust. If a change is necessary, make it at the start of a quarter and announce it in advance.
- **Ignoring collections.** Commission paid at signing hurts the company twice when receivables go uncollected.
- **Not controlling discounts.** In revenue-based plans with unlimited discounting authority, a rep can reduce their own commission slightly while wiping out a large share of the company's margin. A gross profit-based model, or rate reductions tied to discount tiers, solves this problem.
- **Forgetting the ramp period.** Giving a new hire full quota from day one leads to low morale in the first months. Define a graduated quota or a guaranteed minimum variable pay for the first 3 months.
- **Ignoring the impact of inflation and exchange rates.** If your prices rise during the year and the quota is in TL, reps will start exceeding quota regardless of their performance. Update quotas during price increases, or define quotas in units or in foreign currency.
- **Manual calculation.** Commission tables maintained by hand in Excel are error-prone. And every error means lost trust.

## How can you track commission automatically?

For a commission plan to work in practice, reps need to see their performance **in real time**. Seeing a surprise figure at month-end doesn't motivate anyone. Seeing "you're at 78% of quota, 330,000 TL to go until the next tier" every week does.

Here's a setup you can use to automate this:

1. **Tie closing criteria to required fields in your CRM.** When an opportunity moves to the "won" stage, don't allow the stage to change until the contract amount, net amount excluding VAT, currency, payment terms and source (SDR name) fields are filled in.
2. **Write collection data back to the CRM from your accounting or ERP system.** This way, which deals qualify for commission is determined automatically.
3. **Move tier calculations into an automation workflow.** With every collection entry, the rep's period-to-date total should update, and their tier and earned commission should be recalculated.
4. **Send reps automatic notifications.** Share their current status, the amount remaining to the next tier and pending collections via a weekly summary email or Slack message.
5. **Generate an exception report for managers.** Separately list deals discounted beyond the defined limit, customers at risk of clawback, and opportunities with missing data.

This setup reduces the finance team's month-end workload and largely eliminates "my numbers were calculated wrong" objections.

## Frequently asked questions

### What percentage should the sales commission rate be?

There's no single right rate. The commission rate is found by dividing target variable pay by quota. For example, with 480,000 TL in annual target variable pay and a 6,000,000 TL quota, the base rate is 8%. Before setting the rate, you should check your gross margin and your overall cost of sales ratio.

### Should commission be paid at contract signing or at collection?

In B2B markets where deferred-payment sales and collection risk are high, tying commission to collection protects the company. If the sales cycle is long, you can pay a small portion at signing (e.g., 30%) and the rest at collection to keep reps motivated. Whichever method you choose, it should be stated clearly in the plan document.

### Should SDRs earn commission on revenue?

Since SDRs don't close deals themselves, revenue shouldn't be their primary metric. The number of qualified meetings accepted by the sales team is typically used as the main metric. Giving them a small additional bonus when opportunities they sourced close encourages a focus on quality.

### How often should a commission plan be updated?

A comprehensive review once a year, alongside the new budget cycle, is enough. During the year, update the plan only for reasons such as price increases, new product launches or major changes in market conditions. Implement changes at the start of a quarter, with advance written notice.

## Conclusion

A good sales commission plan comes from clear decisions, not complex formulas. First, define the company's top priority for the year. Then define a single purpose for each role. Next, calculate OTE, quota and commission rate so they are consistent with one another, and put the threshold, accelerator, payout and clawback rules in writing. Finally, connect the plan to your CRM and accounting data so it can be tracked automatically. That way, your team sees clearly what is being rewarded, and your company protects its profitability.
