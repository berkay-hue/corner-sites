There is no universal number for a sales rep. That said, for an SDR doing B2B outbound, **40-60 calls and 30-80 targeted emails a day** is a common and sustainable starting range. The right number for you comes from **reverse calculation**, not a fixed rule. You start from your monthly meeting or revenue target and use your own connect, reply and conversion rates to work back to daily activity. One rep might hit target with 40 calls, while another working with poor-quality data might not book a single meeting from 100.

In this article, we walk step by step through reasonable activity ranges for different sales roles, how to calculate the right number for your own team, and how to balance calls and emails. We'll also cover how AI and automation are changing the equation and what to watch out for in the Turkish market. Our goal isn't to give you a number to memorize. It's to give you a calculation method you can reuse every quarter.

## What are reasonable activity ranges by role?

"Sales rep" isn't a single job description. Daily activity expectations vary significantly depending on where the rep sits in the sales process. The ranges below are practical expectations commonly seen in the industry and should be treated as a starting point:

- **SDR (Sales Development Representative):** Their main job is to make first contact with new prospects and book meetings. 40-60 calls, 30-80 emails and 10-20 LinkedIn touches a day is a reasonable workload. Most of their time goes to prospecting.
- **BDR (Business Development Representative):** Focuses on larger, more strategic accounts, and research takes longer. 20-40 calls and 20-40 highly personalized emails a day may be enough.
- **AE (Account Executive):** Runs meetings, prepares proposals and closes deals. New-business calls are usually around 5-20 a day. The real work is managing existing opportunities.
- **Account Manager:** Looks after existing customers. Conversation quality, renewals and cross-sell opportunities matter more than call volume.

None of these numbers means much on its own. At an enterprise software company with a high average contract value and a narrow target audience, 25 well-researched touches a day can be worth more than 80 touches at a company selling low-cost subscriptions to SMBs.

## Why is there no "one right number"?

A few key variables determine activity targets. Any target set without understanding them will either burn out your reps or waste potential.

### Average contract value (ACV)

The higher the deal value, the more research and personalization each touch requires. Sending a templated email to the CFO about an enterprise deal worth several million lira a year is a sure way to burn that account. With low-cost products, volume plays a bigger role.

### Size of the target market

If only 400 companies in a given sector in Türkiye fit your ideal customer profile, reaching 100 people a day will exhaust your list within a few weeks. Narrow markets call for depth instead of volume, meaning more decision-makers per account and higher-quality touches.

### Length of the sales cycle

In long cycles, a single touch rarely produces results. You may need 8-12 touches with the same person spread over several weeks. In that case, "how many new people a day" and "how many total touches a day" need to be treated as separate questions.

### Data quality

If half your phone numbers go to a switchboard and a significant share of your emails go to invalid addresses, your activity numbers will be artificially inflated, but the results won't follow. That's why data quality needs to be discussed before activity targets.

## How do you calculate the right activity number?

The most reliable method is to work backwards from the target. You'll need your own historical data for this calculation. If you don't have it, use the first 4-6 weeks as a measurement period to establish your rates.

1. **Set your revenue or deal target.** For example, say an AE's quarterly quota corresponds to a certain number of closed deals.
2. **Use your win rate to find the number of opportunities needed.** You need to know what share of qualified opportunities you win.
3. **Apply your meeting-to-opportunity conversion rate.** What share of first meetings that actually take place turn into qualified opportunities?
4. **Factor in your show rate.** Not every booked meeting actually happens.
5. **Add your connect-to-meeting or reply-to-meeting conversion.** What share of the people you speak with on the phone, or who reply positively, agree to a meeting?
6. **Apply your call-to-connect and email-to-reply rates.** This final step gets you to your daily activity number.

### Example calculation

The rates below are entirely **hypothetical** and are only meant to illustrate the logic. Replace them with your own data.

Let's say an SDR's monthly target is **12 completed meetings**.

- If the show rate is 80%, meetings that need to be booked: 12 / 0.80 = **15 meetings**
- Let's assume half of these meetings come from calls and half from email: 7-8 meetings from calls, 7-8 from email.

**Call side:**
- If 10% of connected conversations turn into meetings: 7.5 / 0.10 = **75 connects**
- If 8% of calls reach a decision-maker: 75 / 0.08 ≈ **940 calls/month**
- With 20 working days a month: 940 / 20 ≈ **47 calls/day**

**Email side:**
- If 40% of positive replies turn into meetings: 7.5 / 0.40 ≈ **19 positive replies**
- If 2% of people contacted reply positively: 19 / 0.02 = **950 new contacts/month**
- Daily average: 950 / 20 ≈ **47-48 new contacts**

There's an important detail here: 48 new contacts doesn't mean 48 emails. If each contact is in a multi-step sequence, the total number of emails sent per day is several times higher. We covered how to structure these sequences in detail in [our guide on how many steps an outbound email sequence should have](/blog/outbound-email-sequence-steps-timing).

The most valuable thing about this calculation is that it shows which lever has the most impact. Raising the positive reply rate from 2% to 3% cuts the number of new contacts you need by a third. Achieving that improvement is often easier than making a rep write to 30 more people a day.

## How many calls should a rep make per day?

What's usually overlooked when setting call targets is **the time math**. The real cost of a call isn't just the ringing time. It also includes finding the number, briefly researching the person, dialing, leaving a voicemail or having the conversation, and then logging notes in the CRM.

For a rep working manually, an average of 3-5 minutes per call is a realistic estimate. That means:

- **12-20 calls per hour** (manual, including CRM logging)
- Two 90-minute call blocks per day: **36-60 calls**
- With an auto-dialer and automatic logging, this number can rise considerably

That's why 40-60 calls a day is a range that's both achievable for the SDR role and leaves room for other tasks. In teams that set a 100-calls-a-day target, one of two things usually happens: either research and personalization are skipped entirely, or CRM records are left incomplete. Both are costly in the long run.

### Think in terms of "connects," not calls

The metric that really matters is the minutes spent actually talking to decision-makers. The same rep calling the same list at different times of day can see a very different connect rate. You can find practical advice on which days and times are most productive in [our guide to the best time and day for cold calling](/blog/best-time-to-cold-call-b2b).

## How many emails should a rep send per day?

On the email side, the question splits in two: **emails the rep writes manually** and **emails sent through automated sequences**.

### Manual and semi-personalized emails

For emails that open with a personalized first line referencing company news, a new hire or something the person has posted, **20-40 per rep per day** is realistic. Each one requires 3-6 minutes of research. AI-powered research tools can shorten this time.

### Automated sequences and the deliverability ceiling

With automated sequences, the bottleneck isn't the rep's time but **inbox reputation**. Sending very high volumes per day from a single mailbox used for cold email significantly increases the risk of landing in spam. In practice, many teams play it safe by keeping daily cold sends at **around 30-50 per warmed-up mailbox** and spreading volume across multiple mailboxes and domains. On a new domain, sending volume should be ramped up gradually over several weeks.

What to check before increasing volume:

- Correct configuration of **SPF, DKIM and DMARC** records
- Verifying the list before sending
- Keeping the bounce rate low
- Using a separate domain for cold outreach instead of your primary domain

Every email sent to an invalid address also hurts the deliverability of future sends. [Our B2B guide to reducing email bounce rates](/blog/reduce-email-bounce-rate) offers a step-by-step checklist on this.

## How should you balance calls and email?

The best results usually come from **multichannel cadences** that use channels together. Email on its own is easy to ignore. Calls on their own often end up in voicemail. Used together, with a LinkedIn touch in between, they reinforce each other.

A sample 3-week cadence:

1. **Day 1:** Personalized email + LinkedIn profile visit
2. **Day 2:** Call (short voicemail if no answer)
3. **Day 4:** LinkedIn connection request (no note or a short note)
4. **Day 6:** Follow-up email with a different value proposition
5. **Day 9:** Call
6. **Day 13:** Short email with a case study or industry insight
7. **Day 16:** Call + LinkedIn message
8. **Day 21:** Breakup email ("If now isn't the right time…")

In this kind of cadence, a rep's daily work is the sum of touches to people at different stages. As a result, the "calls per day" target is naturally derived from the cadence itself.

### A sample SDR day

- **08:45-09:15:** Review the day's list, identify priority accounts
- **09:15-10:45:** First call block (20-30 calls)
- **10:45-11:00:** Break
- **11:00-12:30:** Personalized email and LinkedIn touches (20-30 people)
- **12:30-13:30:** Lunch
- **13:30-14:00:** Process incoming replies, book meetings
- **14:00-15:30:** Second call block (20-30 calls)
- **15:30-16:30:** New account research and list building
- **16:30-17:30:** CRM updates, prep for the next day, team meeting

This schedule produces roughly 40-60 calls and 25-40 manual touches a day. Meanwhile, automated sequences keep running in the background.

## Which metrics matter more than activity counts?

Activity is an **input metric**. It's easy to control, but it doesn't explain success on its own. Managers need to track activity alongside these outcome and quality metrics:

- **Connect rate:** What share of calls reach a decision-maker?
- **Reply rate and positive reply rate:** Total replies can be misleading. "Take me off your list" is a reply too.
- **Meeting booking rate:** How many meetings are booked per connect or positive reply?
- **Show rate:** What share of booked meetings actually take place?
- **Qualified opportunity rate:** What share of meetings are accepted by the AE as opportunities?
- **Pipeline generated:** Ultimately, the potential revenue the rep brings to the company.

Take two SDRs as an example. The first makes 70 calls a day and books 8 meetings a month. The second makes 40 calls a day and books 12 meetings a month. The answer isn't to ask the first rep for more calls, but to understand what the second rep is doing and spread it across the team. ## Why doesn't increasing activity always work?

When results decline, the most common reflex is to say "more calls, more emails." Sometimes that's right, but more often it isn't.

- **Burnout:** Constant pressure for high activity drives up turnover in the SDR role, which is already high. Every rep who leaves means more hiring and onboarding time.
- **Burning the market:** In a narrow market, reaching the same people with careless messages damages the company's reputation. You may not be able to go back to those accounts for years.
- **Loss of deliverability:** Increasing email volume without control lowers your domain reputation. Eventually, even emails to the right people end up in spam.
- **Misdiagnosis:** The problem may lie in the message, the targeting or the data. Increasing volume doesn't fix these issues. It just exposes them to more people.

Before increasing volume, ask yourself: Are we reaching the right people? Does our message address a real problem they have? Is our data up to date?

## How do AI and automation change these numbers?

The biggest impact of automation is freeing up reps' time from "activity prep" work. In many teams, a large part of a rep's day actually goes to research, data entry and list building rather than selling.

Key tasks that can be handed off to automation:

- **Building and enriching lead lists:** Target company and contact lists can be built using filters for job title, industry and company size. They can then be enriched with verified email and phone data. B2B data platforms like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) are commonly used for this.
- **Account research:** AI can scan a company's website, recent news and job postings and give the rep a short summary and a possible opening line.
- **Personalized drafts:** Instead of writing from scratch, the rep edits and sends a draft with the right context already in place.
- **CRM logging:** Call outcomes, email replies and meeting notes are automatically logged to the relevant record.
- **Reply classification:** Incoming replies are sorted into categories like "interested," "not now," "wrong person" and "unsubscribe," then routed to the right next step.

This lets the same rep increase their number of quality touches per day without sacrificing quality. More importantly, they can devote the time saved to conversations that truly need a human touch. Automation won't fix poor targeting or a weak message. It just scales them faster. That's why you need to clarify your process and target audience first, and then automate.

## What should you watch out for in the Turkish market?

Activity models recommended in global content may not translate directly to Türkiye. A few local factors need to be taken into account:

- **Phone culture:** In Türkiye, phone calls and face-to-face meetings still play a strong role in B2B relationships. In many sectors, especially manufacturing and traditional services, a call-heavy cadence can outperform an email-heavy one.
- **Switchboard and gatekeeper barriers:** When you can't reach someone's mobile directly, calls get stuck at the switchboard or with an assistant. This lowers the connect rate and means daily call targets need to be adjusted accordingly.
- **Legal framework:** The rules on commercial electronic messages, İYS (the Turkish Message Management System) and KVKK (Türkiye's Personal Data Protection Law) directly affect cold email and messaging volume. Pushing volume beyond legal limits to hit a target creates serious risks.
- **Calendar effects:** Religious holidays, the summer months and the year-end budget season noticeably change how productive activity is. Monthly targets should be adjusted to account for this seasonality.

## How should managers set activity targets?

Activity targets exist not to control reps, but to map out a predictable path to results. For a healthy approach, we recommend the following steps:

1. **Make the first 4-6 weeks a measurement period.** Don't set targets before you see your current rates.
2. **Derive targets from outcomes.** Activity targets should be tied to meeting and pipeline targets. The quota itself should also be grounded in reality. For this, take a look at [our formula guide on how to set a sales quota](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quota).
3. **Set a minimum threshold, not a ceiling.** For example, set a floor like "at least 40 calls a day." But don't penalize a rep who's producing results because of a number.
4. **Review weekly, not daily.** A single bad day doesn't mean much. Look at weekly trends.
5. **Invest in improving rates.** Message testing, listening to call recordings together and targeting improvements deliver more lasting results than increasing volume.
6. **Recalculate every quarter.** As the market, product and team change, so do your rates.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many calls should a new SDR make per day?

Since the first two weeks are spent learning the product and the market, 20-30 calls a day is a reasonable start. From the third week onward, with a gradual ramp-up, the goal can be to reach the team's standard range, usually 40-60 calls, by the end of the first month. During this period, the focus should be on conversation quality and feedback rather than numbers.

### Does sending 100 emails a day create a spam risk?

Sending 100 cold emails a day from a single mailbox is considered risky for deliverability. It's safer to spread volume across multiple warmed-up mailboxes and, ideally, a separate sending domain. Verifying the list in advance and setting up SPF, DKIM and DMARC are also essential.

### Which is more effective, calls or email?

It depends on the industry, the target job title and the market. In Türkiye, the phone tends to deliver faster results in many traditional sectors, while email and LinkedIn may be better received at technology companies. The most consistent results usually come from multichannel cadences that use both together.

### What should you do with a rep who hits activity targets but doesn't book meetings?

Start by reviewing targeting, data quality and messaging. Listening to call recordings together and analyzing email replies usually reveals the source of the problem quickly. Asking for more activity is only the right fix if the rates are healthy.

## Conclusion

The short answer to "How many calls and emails should a sales rep make a day?" is roughly 40-60 calls and 30-80 targeted emails for SDRs. The real answer, however, lies in working backwards from your own targets and conversion rates. Activity count is an important input, but it won't produce results on its own if your data quality, targeting, messaging and deliverability aren't solid. AI and automation, meanwhile, free reps from research and data entry so they can make higher-quality touches in the same amount of time.
