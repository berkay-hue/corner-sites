The right response to a price objection in B2B sales is not to offer a discount straight away. First, use questions to pin down the real source of the objection, then rebuild the value case using the customer's own numbers. If you are going to show flexibility, present it as a trade in which you get something in return, not as a free discount. "It's too expensive" is usually not a "no." What the buyer is really saying is, "I'm not convinced enough to defend this price internally."

In this article, we look at how to tell the different types of price objections apart and walk through a six-step response framework you can use during a sales conversation. We also cover example phrases for each objection type, the real impact of discounting on profitability, and where AI and automation help in this process. The goal is for your sales team to see the price question not as a threat but as a door to deepening the opportunity.

## What does a price objection actually mean?

The same sentence can stem from different needs. "It's over our budget," "Your competitor is cheaper," and "How am I going to explain this number to management?" each require a different response. The price objections you encounter most often in the field fall into five groups:

- **Genuine budget constraint:** The amount the company has set aside for this line item really is limited. In this case, the solution usually lies in restructuring the scope or the timing.
- **Lack of perceived value:** The buyer hasn't fully understood what they're getting, or can't see the benefit in numbers. This is the most common type, and also the most solvable.
- **Comparison objection:** There's another offer on the table, and the buyer is comparing the two solutions on price alone. More often than not, they're comparing apples and oranges.
- **Negotiation reflex:** The procurement department or the decision-maker has made a habit of always pushing back on the first offer. In Türkiye, this reflex is especially common in corporate procurement processes.
- **Hidden objection:** The real issue isn't price; it's timing, the internal approval process, trust, or another priority. Price comes to the fore simply because it's the easiest excuse to give.

Any response given without making this distinction is based on a guess. If you give a discount to a buyer who lacks a sense of value, you leave money on the table. If you give a discount to a buyer with a hidden objection, you leave money on the table and still don't close the deal.

## Why is responding to a price objection with an instant discount a mistake?

A sales rep's first reflex to "It's too expensive" is often "We can do something special for you." This response creates three separate problems.

First, **it signals that your price isn't real.** A price that can drop 15 percent at the first objection looks, in the buyer's eyes, like it was inflated to begin with. This perception invites a second and third round of negotiation.

Second, **it erodes profitability disproportionately.** Let's look at a simple calculation: Say your gross margin is 40 percent. On a sale of 100 units, you make 40 units of profit. When you give a 10 percent discount, the price drops to 90 and the profit to 30. To maintain the same gross profit, you need roughly **33 percent more sales volume**. In other words, a "small" 10 percent discount wipes out a quarter of your profit.

Third, **it doesn't solve the real problem.** If the buyer doesn't see the value, they won't see the value of something cheaper either. Even if the deal closes, customer relationships that start with a low perception of value usually cause problems at renewal time.

That's why a clear discount policy and a matching incentive structure are essential at the team level. In a system where reps are paid commission on revenue alone, discounting becomes tempting. [When building your sales commission plan](/blog/how-to-build-sales-commission-plan), adding margin or discount rate as a multiplier directly changes this behavior.

## How do you respond to a price objection? A 6-step framework

The framework below can be applied both in live conversations and in email exchanges. The order of the steps matters. Value shouldn't be discussed before a diagnosis is made, and flexibility shouldn't be discussed before value is established.

### 1. Pause, acknowledge, and clarify

When you hear the objection, don't get defensive, and don't throw out a number right away either. A short pause and an acknowledging sentence lower the tension on the other side:

> "I understand, this is a significant investment and it's completely natural that you want to evaluate it properly. When you say expensive, what are you comparing it to? Could you tell me a bit more?"

This sentence neither validates nor rejects the objection. It simply keeps the conversation going and gives you information.

### 2. Diagnose the source of the objection

Ask short, open-ended questions to figure out which of the five types above the price objection falls into:

- "Is there a set budget range for this project, or has that not been finalized yet?"
- "Are there other alternatives you're currently evaluating? Are they similar in scope?"
- "Setting price aside, does the solution meet your needs?"
- "Who else will need to approve this decision, and what do you think they'll focus on most?"

The third question is especially critical. If the answer is "Yes, it's exactly what we need," you're having a value and budget conversation. If the answer is "Actually, we're not sure about a few things," the price objection is a smokescreen and the real objection lies behind it.

### 3. Rebuild the value using the customer's own numbers

Buyers don't believe your numbers; they believe their own. That's why you should build the value calculation with them, using the information they provide. The following example is built entirely on hypothetical figures:

Say the annual quote for a sales automation solution is 240,000 TL. The customer says it's "too expensive." In the discovery call, you gathered the following information:

- There are 6 reps on the sales team.
- Each spends about 1.5 hours a day on manual list building, data entry, and follow-up emails.
- Based on 22 working days a month, that comes to 33 hours per person per month, or **198 hours** across the team.
- If automation saves 60 percent of this time, roughly **119 hours** are recovered each month.
- Assuming a fully loaded hourly cost of 400 TL per rep, that frees up time worth about **47,500 TL** per month, or about **570,000 TL** per year.

This calculation shows that the 240,000 TL investment pays back more than twice over on time savings alone. And the additional revenue generated when those freed-up hours turn into new conversations isn't even included in this calculation yet.

The key point is this: Don't state these numbers yourself; ask the customer and fill them in together on screen. Buyers rarely dispute numbers they provided themselves. Adding this calculation to the proposal document also makes it easier for the buyer to defend the price internally. [When preparing sales proposals with AI](/blog/ai-sales-proposal-step-by-step), automatically generating a customer-specific ROI table makes this step scalable.

### 4. Move the comparison onto the right footing

When faced with "Your competitor is 30 percent cheaper," badmouthing the competitor is the worst possible response. Instead, level the scope of the comparison:

> "Great, that's a reasonable comparison. So we can compare accurately, let me ask: Does their offer include setup, integration, training, and the first three months of support? Our offer includes all of these. Shall we put them side by side?"

The goal here isn't to justify the price but to make the total cost of ownership visible. Items such as setup time, hours spent by the internal team, additional license needs, and migration risk can be far more decisive than the initial quoted price.

### 5. Offer flexibility as a trade

If there's still a budget issue after value has been established, you can show flexibility. But always do it in exchange for something. This approach is known as the "give-get" principle. Example trades:

- **Longer commitment:** "If you go with a two-year contract instead of one, we can reduce the annual price by 8 percent."
- **Upfront payment:** "If the annual fee is paid upfront, we can offer a 5 percent benefit."
- **Narrower scope:** "If the budget is capped at 180,000 TL, we can start with two modules in the first phase and expand in the third month."
- **Reference and case study:** "If you agree to turn your results into a case study with us at the end of the project, we can reflect that in the price."
- **Quick decision:** "If the contract is signed by the end of the month, we can waive the setup fee." (This should only be used if there's a genuinely valid reason; artificial urgency damages trust.)

The trade-off logic protects two things: the credibility of your price and the value you receive. A discount with nothing in return signals to the buyer to "ask for more." A trade, on the other hand, carries the message that "everything has a price."

### 6. Lock in the next step

Once the price conversation is over, don't leave things open-ended:

> "If this structure works for you, I'll send the revised proposal by noon tomorrow. Could we set up a 30-minute call on Thursday that your finance director can also join?"

Price conversations without a scheduled next step turn into opportunities that sit in the pipeline for weeks. To catch such opportunities early, you need to regularly apply methods for [identifying stalled opportunities in the pipeline](/blog/how-to-spot-stalled-deals-in-pipeline).

## Which phrase works for which objection?

The examples below aren't scripts to memorize but frameworks you can adapt to your own voice.

**"It's over our budget."**
> "I understand. If you can share your budget range, let's work out together the scope that will have the greatest impact within that range. It might make more sense to keep the first phase narrower and expand once you see the results."

**"Your competitor is much cheaper."**
> "That may be the case; different approaches lead to different prices. Which criterion will matter most in your decision? Could you tell me two things besides price that are critical for you?"

**"I can't sell this price to management."**
> "That's really valuable to know, thank you. What numbers does your management usually look at when making decisions? Payback period, cost savings, or revenue impact? Let me prepare a one-page summary for you in that language."

**"Give us a discount, or it's a no."** (Negotiation reflex)
> "We set our prices to be fair to every customer, so rather than a straight discount, I'd prefer to talk about the structure. Would flexibility on the commitment period or payment plan be meaningful for you?"

**"It's expensive for now, maybe next quarter."** (Possible hidden objection)
> "Of course. What do you expect to change by next quarter? Is it the budget cycle, or is there something else that needs to be settled internally?"

The last example is especially important. A "next quarter" response is often not about price but about priorities or internal approval. Sending follow-up emails without uncovering the real issue usually goes nowhere.

## How do you reduce price objections before the conversation?

The best objection handling is reducing the intensity of the objection before the conversation even happens. Four areas stand out for this.

**1. Talk to the right customers.** A significant share of price objections comes from conversations with companies that don't need your solution or can't afford it. An [ideal customer profile](/blog/how-to-define-ideal-customer-profile-b2b) defined by criteria such as company size, team structure, and the severity of the problem helps you focus from the outset on companies whose problems are big enough to justify your price. **2. Talk about price early and as a range.** Saving price for the final meeting amplifies the surprise, and therefore the objection. Asking in the first or second meeting, "For similar companies, the investment usually falls within this range. Is that a range worth discussing for you?" weeds out poor-fit opportunities early.

**3. Gather numbers in the discovery call.** To build the value calculation, you need to learn the cost of the problem in quantitative terms during discovery. Questions such as "How many hours a week does this process cost your team?" and "What's the average value of a missed opportunity?" provide the material you'll later use against price objections.

**4. Involve all decision-makers early.** A proposal that has convinced the technical team but that finance has never seen comes back with a price objection in the final stage. Decision-makers and the approval process should be mapped during the discovery stage.

## When should you give a discount, and how much?

Discounts shouldn't be banned outright, but they should be governed by rules. A structure that works in practice might look like this:

- **0–5%:** The rep can grant it on their own initiative, but only in exchange for a trade.
- **5–10%:** Requires sales manager approval, and the rationale is recorded in the CRM.
- **Over 10%:** Requires executive approval. The narrower-scope alternative must be tried first.

These thresholds vary from company to company; what matters is that a threshold exists. In addition, if the rationale for every discount is recorded, after a few quarters it becomes clear which segment requests discounts, against which competitor, and at which stage.

An often-overlooked point about discounts is their effect on renewals. It's hard to apply the list price in year two to a customer who started with a 20 percent discount in year one. If a discount is given, the contract should state explicitly that it applies "to the first year only."

## How do AI and automation help with price objection handling?

The most powerful weapon against price objections is context-specific information. AI and automation make it easier to gather and organize this information and put it in front of the rep at the right moment.

**Objection analysis from call recordings:** Call transcripts can be tagged automatically with AI: At what stage did the price objection come up, how was it phrased, how did the rep respond, and what was the outcome? [Automating sales call notes with AI](/blog/automate-sales-call-notes-with-ai) allows this data to accumulate without depending on manual note-taking.

**Ready-made response library:** Objection responses from top-performing reps can be compiled and categorized by objection type. When a new rep encounters the "competitor is cheaper" objection, they can see the responses that have worked best for the team against it.

**Automated ROI calculation:** When the numbers gathered in the discovery call (team size, hours spent, opportunity value) are entered into a template, a customer-specific value calculation can be prepared within minutes. This calculation can then be automatically added to the proposal email.

**Post-pricing follow-up sequences:** A short email sequence can be set up for buyers who go quiet after the pricing conversation. For example, a value summary on day 2, an anonymized results example from a similar company on day 5, and a clear "Shall we move forward?" question on day 10. This sequence hands off to the system the follow-ups the rep would otherwise have to remember manually.

**Competitor pricing intelligence:** Competitors' pricing pages, package structures, and public positioning can be monitored and summarized regularly. That way, when faced with the "competitor is cheaper" objection, the rep already knows what the comparison leaves out.

## How do you measure objection handling at the team level?

A skill that isn't measured doesn't improve. The following metrics can be used to track price objection handling:

- **Win rate after a price objection:** What percentage of opportunities that received a price objection were won?
- **Average discount rate:** How much discount is given per rep, segment, and competitor?
- **No-trade discount rate:** What percentage of discounts were given without getting anything in return?
- **Opportunities lost at the pricing stage:** In lost deals, was "price" really the main reason, or was it a cover for other reasons?

The last item is especially important. Selecting "price" as the loss reason in the CRM is the easiest option, but it often doesn't reflect reality. When lost opportunities are reviewed regularly, it turns out that some of the losses recorded as price were actually caused by value messaging, timing, or poor targeting. Adding [lost opportunity analysis](/blog/lost-deal-analysis-guide) to your monthly routine is the most reliable way to see the true weight of price objections.

A 15-minute "objection practice" in weekly team meetings is also highly effective. One rep plays the buyer, another responds, and the team gives feedback based on the six-step framework. These short role-plays quickly reduce the habit of reflexively offering discounts in the field.

## Frequently asked questions

### What's the first thing you should say when a price objection comes up?

The first response should be a question that acknowledges and clarifies the objection. For example, "I understand. When you say expensive, what are you comparing it to?" works well. This response keeps the conversation going without getting defensive or giving a discount, and helps you understand the real source of the objection.

### Is giving a discount in B2B sales always wrong?

No, but discounts should be governed by rules and given in exchange for something. Trades such as a longer commitment, upfront payment, a reference, or a quick decision turn the discount into a negotiation tool that protects profitability. A discount given with nothing in return, on the other hand, signals that your price isn't real.

### How do you respond to "Your competitor is cheaper"?

Instead of criticizing the competitor, focus on leveling the scope of the comparison. Make the total cost of ownership visible by putting items such as setup, integration, training, support, and internal team time side by side. Then ask the buyer what their two most critical decision criteria are besides price.

### Is it possible to reduce price objections before the conversation?

Yes. Talking to companies that fit your ideal customer profile, sharing price early as a range, and learning the cost of the problem in numbers during the discovery call significantly reduce the intensity of objections. Involving all decision-makers early in the process also prevents surprise objections at the final stage.

## Conclusion

In B2B sales, a price objection isn't the end of the deal; it's a sign that the buyer needs more reassurance. The right response consists of acknowledging and clarifying the objection, diagnosing its source with questions, building value with the customer's own numbers, moving the comparison onto fair ground, offering flexibility only in exchange for a trade, and putting the next step on the calendar. Once this discipline becomes a team habit, both win rate and profit margin are protected together.
