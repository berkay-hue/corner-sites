In B2B sales, a decision-maker map is a structured diagram that shows who makes the purchasing decision at a target company, who influences it and who could block it, based on **role, level of influence and attitude toward you**. To build one, you start by listing everyone involved in the account. You then assign each person a role (such as budget owner, champion, technical evaluator, user or blocker), score their influence and level of support, identify missing relationships and create a separate engagement plan for each person. A well-built map largely eliminates the familiar problem of "talking to just one person and never understanding why the deal stalled."

In enterprise sales, decisions are never made by a single person. Even at a mid-sized company, the purchase of software, services or equipment typically involves 4 to 10 people: the user team, the department head, IT, finance, procurement and sometimes legal. When a sales rep reaches only one of these people, the process either drags on or ends at the final stage when someone they have never met says "not now." In this article, we walk you step by step through how to build a decision-maker map from scratch, which tools can support it and how to put it to work using a concrete example.

## What is a decision-maker map, and why does it matter so much?

A decision-maker map (also known in English-language sources as a "stakeholder map," "buying committee map" or "power map") makes the human dynamics of a sales opportunity visible. What sets it apart from a traditional org chart is that it shows not just who reports to whom, but **who actually has how much say**. Someone with the title "specialist" may hold veto power in the technical evaluation, while someone with the title "director" may barely be involved at all.

The benefits of the map for a sales team can be summarized as follows:

- **It reduces single-threading risk.** If your contact leaves the company or their priorities change, the opportunity doesn't reset to zero.
- **It personalizes your message.** You talk to the CFO about payback period and cost. With the IT manager, you discuss integration and security, and with the user team, you explain how their daily workload will shrink.
- **It improves forecast accuracy.** Reporting an opportunity as "in the proposal stage" when you've never reached the budget owner makes the pipeline look better than it really is.
- **It surfaces hidden blockers early.** You spot the executive with close ties to the incumbent vendor in the first week, not the last.
- **It shortens the sales cycle.** When you answer each stakeholder's questions individually and on time, you cut down on "let's also loop in this other team" delays.

## What roles exist in a buying committee?

Before you build the map, you need to know which roles you're looking for. Titles vary from company to company, but roles remain largely the same.

### Budget owner (economic decision-maker)

This is the person who approves the spend and whose "yes" makes the purchase happen. At SMEs, it's usually the general manager or the company owner. At large companies, it's the deputy general manager of the relevant department or the CFO. You'll have few direct meetings with this person, but every one of them is critical.

### Champion (internal advocate)

This is the person who advocates for your solution inside the company and speaks on your behalf in meetings. A good champion both believes in your solution and has influence within the organization. Someone who likes you but whom nobody listens to isn't a champion; they're just a "friend."

### Technical evaluator

This is the person in IT, engineering or operations who assesses whether the solution is technically fit. They usually don't have the authority to say "yes," but they do have the authority to say "no."

### Users

These are the team members who will use the product or service every day. They could be the sales team, the accounting team or production shift supervisors. If they push back, even the best offer will collapse during the pilot.

### Procurement and legal

These are the functions that manage contract terms, payment terms and vendor onboarding. Most sales teams only meet these people in the final week and are hit with "surprise" payment-term or discount requests. To prepare for the negotiation scenarios that commonly arise at this stage, check out our guide on [how to respond to price objections in B2B sales](/blog/b2b-sales-price-objections).

### Blocker

This is the person who resists change, backs the incumbent vendor or believes the project will shrink their own area of responsibility. Ignoring a blocker is one of the most expensive mistakes you can make. You need to identify them, win them over, or at the very least neutralize them.

### Influencer

These are people with no formal role whose opinion the decision-maker seeks out: an outside consultant, a board member or a trusted former employee could all fall into this group.

## How do you build a decision-maker map step by step?

The seven steps below provide an actionable framework for a single account. For strategic accounts, it's possible to produce a first version in 1-2 hours. What matters is keeping the map up to date throughout the sales process.

### 1. Clarify the account and the scope of the opportunity

A decision-maker map is built around the **opportunity**, not the company. The committee you'll face when selling HR software to a company is completely different from the one you'll face when selling sales automation to the same company. So start by answering these questions:

- What problem are we solving, and which department owns that problem?
- What is the estimated deal size? (This determines the approval layers.)
- Which systems does the solution touch? (This determines the technical evaluators.)

Checking up front whether the account truly fits your target audience saves you from mapping in vain. To do this, we recommend first clarifying [how to define your ideal customer profile (ICP)](/blog/how-to-define-ideal-customer-profile-b2b).

### 2. Sketch the skeleton of the organization

At this stage, the goal is to list the people in the relevant departments. Don't analyze them yet. Sources you can use include:

- **LinkedIn:** The employee list on the company page, title filters and recent posts. Who is talking about which project reveals their actual area of responsibility. If you'd like to strengthen your company's and executives' visibility on LinkedIn, you can get support from the [Social Media Corner team for B2B LinkedIn management](https://socialmediacorner.co).
- **Company website:** The "About Us," "Leadership" and press pages.
- **B2B data platforms:** Tools like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) save time when building contact lists by title, department and seniority and accessing verified contact information.
- **Public sources:** Annual reports, tender announcements and job postings (newly opened positions say a lot about a company's priorities).
- **Your existing contact:** This is the most valuable source. Asking "Who else weighs in when a decision like this is made?" often yields more than half an hour of research.

At this stage, it's normal to end up with a list of 8-15 people from the relevant departments. You'll narrow it down later.

### 3. Assign a role to each person

Next to each name on the list, write one of the roles above. A person can hold more than one role. For example, at SMEs, the general manager may be both the budget owner and the technical evaluator. Mark any roles you're unsure about as "assumptions" and validate them in your conversations.

When assigning roles, look at **behavior**, not titles. The question "Would a decision get made if this person weren't in the meeting?" is a better indicator than a title.

### 4. Score influence and attitude

Give each person a score from 1 to 5 on two axes:

- **Influence:** How much can they shape the decision? (1 = barely at all, 5 = has the final say)
- **Attitude:** How do they view you and your solution? (1 = active opponent, 3 = neutral, 5 = strong supporter)

When you plot these two axes on a matrix, four quadrants emerge:

1. **High influence + high support:** Your key allies. Keep them informed and equip them for internal advocacy.
2. **High influence + low support:** The most critical quadrant. Most of your time should go here.
3. **Low influence + high support:** Sources of information and potential champion candidates.
4. **Low influence + low support:** Monitor them, but don't spend too many resources.

Scoring may seem subjective, but it creates a shared language within the team. In a pipeline meeting, saying "The CFO is influence 5, attitude 2" paints a much clearer picture than "finance seems a bit hesitant."

### 5. Identify relationship and access gaps

Once you've built the map, ask yourself these questions:

- Have we spoken directly with the budget owner at least once?
- Do we have a real champion, or just someone who politely listens to us?
- Is the technical evaluator involved in the process, or will they only step in at the final stage?
- Do we know procurement's process and timeline?
- Is there anyone who could be a blocker? If so, what is their concern?

Every "no" becomes an item on your action list for the next two weeks. As a practical rule, it's a healthy discipline not to advance an opportunity to later stages until you have **active contact with people in at least three different roles**.

### 6. Create a personalized message and engagement plan

Write a short plan for each critical stakeholder:

- **What is their priority or pain point?** (For example, cash flow and predictable costs for the CFO.)
- **What evidence do they want to see?** (A payback calculation, a reference call, security documentation.)
- **Who will make the introduction?** (Through the champion if possible, otherwise with a personalized outbound message.)
- **Which channel?** (Email, phone, LinkedIn message or in-person meeting.)
- **Target date?**

The aim here is a multi-threaded approach. You move forward on the same account with parallel but mutually consistent messages. Don't neglect coordinating with your champion so that messages don't overlap and no one feels "bypassed."

### 7. Keep the map live in your CRM

A decision-maker map isn't a document you draw once and file away. It should be updated after every conversation:

- If a new name comes up, add it.
- If an attitude score changes, update it and note the reason.
- If someone changes positions or leaves the company, flag it.

The simplest solution is to add "role," "influence" and "attitude" fields to the contact record in your CRM. If you don't use a CRM, a shared spreadsheet will do. What matters is that the whole team sees the same up-to-date picture.

## A concrete example: Selling to a 250-person manufacturing company

Imagine you're selling a sales automation solution to a manufacturing company with 250 employees that sells industrial equipment domestically. The first contact came from a sales operations specialist who requested a demo through your website. The first two meetings went very well. But three weeks later, the response came back: "Management isn't prioritizing this right now." When you rebuild the map from scratch, the picture looks like this:

| Person (title) | Role | Influence (1-5) | Attitude (1-5) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales operations specialist | User / potential champion | 2 | 5 | Active contact |
| Sales director | Budget owner (department) | 4 | ? | Never met |
| General manager | Final approval | 5 | ? | Never met |
| IT manager | Technical evaluator | 4 | 2 | Has security concerns |
| Regional sales managers (3 people) | User | 3 | 3 | Unaware |
| Procurement specialist | Procurement | 2 | ? | Never met |
| Current CRM consultant (external) | Influencer / possible blocker | 3 | 2 | Defends the current system |

The conclusions from this table are quite clear:

- **Your champion has little influence.** The specialist likes you but doesn't have the clout to champion the project in front of the sales director.
- **The budget owner was never reached.** The "management isn't prioritizing this" response most likely stems from the director never having heard the details.
- **There's a silent objection in IT.** The data security concern was never raised in writing, but it's standing in the way of the process.
- **The external consultant is a likely blocker.** They earn revenue from maintaining the current system.

An action plan built on these findings might look like this:

1. Ask the champion to set up a 30-minute meeting with the sales director, and prepare a presentation for it focused on the director's KPIs (conversion by region, number of meetings per rep).
2. Send the IT manager a short technical document covering data retention, access control and integration, and request a separate meeting with the technical team.
3. Propose a two-week pilot with one regional sales manager. A concrete result on the user side will be the strongest argument in the director's eyes.
4. Look for ways to position the external consultant as an integration partner rather than a competitor.
5. Find out procurement's vendor onboarding process now, so you don't lose two weeks once approval comes through.

Without the map, the same opportunity would have stayed "stuck." With the map, it turned into five concrete actions, each with a clear owner and date.

## How can AI be used for decision-maker mapping?

The most time-consuming parts of mapping are research and keeping the map up to date. AI saves significant time in both areas:

- **Account research:** Summarizing the company's website, news, job postings and executive posts. For example, you can get an answer within minutes to a question like "Which sales roles has this company posted in the last six months, and what does that mean?"
- **Role prediction:** Suggesting a likely role based on title and department. This prediction is a starting point and must always be validated in conversations.
- **Extracting signals from meeting notes:** Automatically capturing signals from meeting transcripts such as "a new name came up," "the budget cycle was mentioned" or "an objection was raised."
- **Personalized message drafts:** Preparing different opening messages based on each stakeholder's priorities.
- **Change alerts:** Notifying the rep when someone on the map changes positions or leaves the company.

## What are the most common mistakes?

A decision-maker map looks simple, but a few common mistakes in practice undermine its value:

- **Confusing title with role.** Not everyone with "manager" in their title is a decision-maker. And some critical influencers sit fairly low on the org chart.
- **Mistaking a friend for a champion.** Someone who politely listens to you in every meeting may not be advocating for you internally. A real champion gives you inside information and puts their own reputation on the line.
- **Avoiding the blocker.** Not meeting with the person who opposes you doesn't make them go away. It just means they voice their objection in a room you're not in.
- **Drawing the map once and leaving it.** B2B sales cycles can last months. During that time, the organization changes and priorities shift. A map that isn't updated is misleading.
- **Sending everyone the same message.** Sending the CFO a product feature list or approaching a user with a payback calculation kills interest fast.
- **Leaving procurement until the last week.** Issues such as vendor onboarding, contract templates and payment terms can push your close date back by weeks.

## How does a decision-maker map affect the pipeline?

The map's real value shows up when it's reflected in pipeline management. Many teams advance opportunity stages based solely on the rep's gut feeling. The map, by contrast, adds concrete criteria to stage transitions. A sample set of rules might look like this:

- **To exit the needs analysis stage:** You must have met with people in at least two different roles.
- **To exit the solution presentation stage:** At least one direct meeting with the budget owner must have taken place, and the technical evaluator must be involved in the process.
- **To exit the proposal stage:** The procurement process and the signatory must be known, and the concerns of known blockers must have been addressed.

These criteria also make it easier to diagnose opportunities that have been stalled for a long time. If an opportunity has been sitting in the same stage for weeks, the first place to look is usually the map. Most likely, a critical role has been left unfilled. ## Which accounts should you map?

Building a detailed map for every opportunity isn't efficient. A practical breakdown might look like this:

- **Small, fast deals** (single decision-maker, short cycle): A simple checklist is enough. "Who approves it, who uses it?"
- **Mid-sized deals** (3-5 stakeholders): A simple table with roles and attitudes is enough.
- **Strategic accounts** (multiple departments, long cycle, high value): These require a full map, an influence-attitude matrix, individual engagement plans and a review every two weeks.

Making this distinction steers reps' time toward the opportunities that will deliver the greatest return.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a decision-maker map and an org chart?

An org chart shows who reports to whom. A decision-maker map, on the other hand, shows how much influence each person has over a specific purchasing decision and their attitude toward you. That's why the same company can have different maps for different opportunities.

### How many people should you engage in a B2B sale?

There's no fixed number, and it varies with deal size. As a practical rule, for mid-sized and large deals, it's recommended not to advance an opportunity to later stages until you have active contact with people in at least three different roles (budget owner, champion and technical evaluator).

### What should you do in an opportunity with no champion?

First, look among your existing contacts for someone who is both interested in your solution and listened to within the company. If there's no such person, try to create a champion through a small pilot or value demonstration targeting the users most affected by the problem. Opportunities that move forward without a champion generally have a low chance of closing.

### How often should a decision-maker map be updated?

Ideally, the map should be updated after every significant conversation. For strategic accounts, a short review every two weeks on top of that is enough to catch new names, shifting attitudes and changes in position.
