Pipeline velocity is a metric that shows how much revenue your sales pipeline generates per day. The formula is: **(Number of qualified opportunities × Win rate × Average deal size) ÷ Sales cycle length (days)**. For example, if you have 60 qualified opportunities, a 25% win rate, an average deal size of 180,000 TL and a 90-day sales cycle, your pipeline velocity is 30,000 TL per day.

This single number exposes the gap between a sales team's sense of being "really busy" and actual revenue generation. In this article, we'll walk step by step through how to calculate each component of the formula correctly, what the result tells you, and which variable delivers the biggest impact when improved. We'll show the calculations with concrete numbers. We'll also discuss why segment-level measurement is essential and cover the most common mistakes.

## What is pipeline velocity and why does it matter?

Pipeline velocity measures the pace at which opportunities in your pipeline turn into revenue. Pipeline size answers the question "What do we have?" Pipeline velocity answers the question "How fast is what we have turning into money?"

Consider two companies. Each has 10 million TL worth of open opportunities in its pipeline. The first company closes these opportunities in an average of 45 days with a 30% win rate. The second company closes the same opportunities in 180 days with a 12% win rate. On paper, their pipeline reports look identical. In terms of cash flow, growth rate and hiring capacity, however, these two companies are in completely different places.

From a management perspective, pipeline velocity offers the following benefits:

- **It combines four metrics into a single number.** Instead of tracking opportunity count, win rate, deal size and cycle length separately, you see their combined effect.
- **It shows which lever to pull.** When growth slows, you can tell whether the problem is a lack of opportunities, low conversion or a lengthening cycle.
- **It strengthens forecasting.** When you know your daily revenue generation capacity, you can see early on whether your quarter-end target is realistic. We covered this topic in detail in our guide to [building realistic sales forecasts from pipeline data](/blog/sales-forecasting-from-pipeline).
- **It's comparable over time.** Tracking the trend month over month or quarter over quarter lets you separate one-off wins from structural improvements.

## What is the pipeline velocity formula?

The standard formula is:

**Pipeline Velocity = (O × W × A) ÷ L**

- **O:** Number of qualified open opportunities
- **W:** Win rate (as a decimal, e.g., 25% = 0.25)
- **A:** Average deal size (TL)
- **L:** Average sales cycle length (days)

The result is expressed in "TL/day." The numerator gives you the expected total revenue of your pipeline. Dividing it by the cycle length shows how quickly that revenue is generated on a daily basis.

The formula is simple. The mistakes happen in how the variables are defined. So let's look at each one individually.

### How do you determine the number of qualified opportunities?

The key word here is **qualified**. Not every record in your CRM is an opportunity. Count only records where a discovery call has taken place, the need has been validated and a defined stage has been passed. Contacts from cold lists who haven't responded yet don't belong in this number.

A practical rule: Count opportunities from the same stage at which you start calculating your win rate. For example, if you measure your win rate from the "Discovery call completed" stage onward, your opportunity count should consist of open records at that stage and beyond. If your stages aren't clearly defined, we recommend first reviewing our guide to [defining sales pipeline stages](/blog/how-to-define-sales-pipeline-stages). Vague stage definitions make pipeline velocity meaningless.

### How do you calculate win rate?

**Win rate = Won opportunities ÷ (Won + Lost opportunities)**

Look at opportunities closed within a specific period (for example, the last 6 or 12 months). Don't include still-open opportunities in the denominator, because their outcomes aren't known yet. If you include them, you'll artificially lower the rate.

Example: Over the last 12 months, you won 48 opportunities and lost 144. Your win rate is 48 ÷ 192 = **25%**.

### How do you calculate average deal size?

Calculate this only from **won** deals. Quote amounts on open opportunities tend to be optimistic and drop during negotiation. If you operate on a subscription model, choose a consistent definition: annual contract value (ACV) or first-year revenue? Apply your chosen definition the same way in every calculation.

Example: If the total value of 48 won deals is 8,640,000 TL, your average deal size is 8,640,000 ÷ 48 = **180,000 TL**.

### How do you measure sales cycle length?

Take the average number of days won opportunities spent from the date they were opened as opportunities to the date they closed. The starting point should be consistent with the stage at which you count opportunities. If you use the day the first cold email was sent as the starting point, you'll make the cycle look longer than it is. Measuring the time after an opportunity becomes qualified gives a more accurate result.

If a single unusually long deal skews the average, look at the median as well. Decide which one you'll use in the formula and stay consistent.

## Step-by-step example: How do you calculate pipeline velocity?

Let's work through a hypothetical example. Imagine a B2B company based in Istanbul that sells software to mid-sized manufacturing companies.

**Step 1: Count qualified open opportunities.**
In the CRM, there are a total of **60** open opportunities in the "Discovery completed," "Demo held," "Proposal sent" and "Negotiation" stages.

**Step 2: Calculate the win rate.**
Over the last 12 months, of the opportunities that passed the discovery stage, 48 were won and 144 were lost. Win rate: 48 ÷ 192 = **0.25**

**Step 3: Find the average deal size.**
The average of the 48 won deals is **180,000 TL**.

**Step 4: Calculate the sales cycle length.**
The average time won deals took from the discovery stage to signature is **90 days**.

**Step 5: Plug the numbers into the formula.**

- Numerator: 60 × 0.25 × 180,000 = 2,700,000 TL
- Denominator: 90 days
- **Pipeline velocity: 2,700,000 ÷ 90 = 30,000 TL/day**

Under current conditions, this company's pipeline generates roughly 30,000 TL in revenue per day.

## How do you interpret the result?

On its own, 30,000 TL/day may seem like an abstract number. There are three ways to make it meaningful.

**Convert it to a period.** Multiply the daily figure by 30 and you get a monthly revenue capacity of about 900,000 TL; multiply by 90 and you get a quarterly capacity of about 2,700,000 TL. If your quarterly target is 4 million TL, you'll see at the start of the quarter that reaching it at your current pipeline velocity is mathematically difficult. That way, you won't be caught off guard in the last two weeks.

**Track it as a trend.** Calculate pipeline velocity every month using the same method. A single month's value tells you little. A six-month trend, however, clearly shows whether your sales engine is speeding up.

**Break it down into components.** If velocity drops, look at the four variables one by one. If the opportunity count stays the same while the cycle grows from 90 to 110 days, the problem isn't at the top of the funnel. There's a bottleneck in the middle of the sales process. Perhaps approval processes at the proposal stage have gotten longer, or perhaps a new layer of decision-makers has been added.

Don't use pipeline velocity as a standalone measure of success. Evaluate it alongside other metrics that measure team productivity. Our guide on [KPIs for measuring sales team productivity](/blog/how-to-measure-sales-team-productivity-kpis) is a good complement for this.

## Which variable is most effective to improve?

The beauty of the formula is that you can simulate the impact of each lever. Let's assume the company in our example improves each variable by 10% individually:

| Scenario | Calculation | Pipeline velocity | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current state | 60 × 0.25 × 180,000 ÷ 90 | 30,000 TL/day | – |
| Opportunity count +10% (66) | 66 × 0.25 × 180,000 ÷ 90 | 33,000 TL/day | +10% |
| Win rate +10% (27.5%) | 60 × 0.275 × 180,000 ÷ 90 | 33,000 TL/day | +10% |
| Deal size +10% (198,000 TL) | 60 × 0.25 × 198,000 ÷ 90 | 33,000 TL/day | +10% |
| Cycle length −10% (81 days) | 60 × 0.25 × 180,000 ÷ 81 | ~33,333 TL/day | +11.1% |
| All four combined | 66 × 0.275 × 198,000 ÷ 81 | ~44,367 TL/day | +47.9% |

Two important lessons emerge from this table:

1. **Shortening the cycle is proportionally slightly more effective.** The smaller the denominator, the greater the impact. Cutting the cycle by 10% increases velocity by more than 11%. Cutting it by 20% increases velocity by 25%.
2. **Small improvements have a multiplier effect.** A modest 10% gain in each of the four variables adds up to a jump of roughly 48% overall. Trying to achieve a 48% improvement in a single metric is far harder.

In practice, each lever differs in cost and feasibility. Increasing opportunity count is usually the most predictable, but if quality drops, it drags down the win rate. Increasing deal size requires pricing and packaging decisions. Shortening the cycle is mostly achieved through process discipline and automation.

## Why is segment-level calculation essential?

The biggest pitfall of pipeline velocity is blending different segments into a single average. Let's assume the same company sells to two segments:

**SMB segment:**
- 40 opportunities, 30% win rate, 60,000 TL average deal, 30-day cycle
- 40 × 0.30 × 60,000 ÷ 30 = **24,000 TL/day**

**Enterprise segment:**
- 20 opportunities, 15% win rate, 500,000 TL average deal, 150-day cycle
- 20 × 0.15 × 500,000 ÷ 150 = **10,000 TL/day**

The combined pipeline velocity of the two segments is **34,000 TL/day**.

Now let's merge the same data into a single pool. There are 60 opportunities in total. The weighted win rate is 25% (12 + 3 = 15 wins expected from 60 opportunities). The average value of won deals is about 148,000 TL. The simple average of the two cycles is 90 days. The calculation becomes: 60 × 0.25 × 148,000 ÷ 90 ≈ **24,667 TL/day**.

The same data yields 24,667 TL instead of 34,000 TL. That's a deviation of roughly 27%. More importantly, the combined calculation completely hides which segment is driving growth. In this example, the SMB segment generates more than twice as much daily revenue as enterprise, even with smaller deals. This insight is decisive when deciding where to allocate sales resources.

At a minimum, calculate pipeline velocity separately for these breakdowns:

- Customer segment (SMB, mid-market, enterprise)
- Lead source (outbound, inbound, partner, event)
- Product or package
- Sales rep or team

To segment correctly, you first need to be clear about who you're targeting. [Defining your ideal customer profile](/blog/how-to-define-ideal-customer-profile-b2b) lays the foundation for this segmentation.

## What are the most common mistakes when calculating pipeline velocity?

**1. Counting unqualified records as opportunities.** Counting leads you haven't spoken to yet as opportunities inflates the O value. Because these records aren't included in the win rate calculation, the two metrics become inconsistent and the result comes out far higher than reality.

**2. Including open opportunities in the win rate.** Only closed (won or lost) opportunities should go into the win rate denominator.

**3. Using quote amounts as deal size.** Post-negotiation discounts and scope reductions lower the realized amount. Use the actual value of won deals.

**4. Not cleaning out zombie opportunities.** Opportunities that haven't moved in months and that nobody is following up on artificially inflate the O value as long as they stay open in the pipeline. Set a rule to close opportunities with no activity for a defined period. For example, you could close opportunities that have been inactive for twice the average cycle length.

**5. Period inconsistency.** If you calculate win rate from the last 12 months and cycle length from the last 3 months, you're mixing the realities of two different periods. Use the same time window for all historical metrics.

**6. Mixing segments.** As shown above, combined averages produce misleading results.

**7. Calculating it only once.** Pipeline velocity isn't a number you calculate once and shelve. Its value lies in the trend.

## What are practical ways to increase pipeline velocity?

Here are actionable tactics for each of the four variables.

### Increasing opportunity count without sacrificing quality

- **Make outbound capacity systematic.** Personalized email sequences and multichannel outreach flows reduce the time your sales team spends on manual prospecting while increasing the number of qualified meetings. For B2B SaaS companies that would rather get outside support than build this in-house, [SAAS Corner's outbound sales and lead generation service](https://saascorner.co) is one option.
- **Enrich your data.** Missing or incorrect contact information means failing to reach the right person. Data enriched with company size, industry and decision-maker information improves both reach and targeting.

### Raising the win rate

- **Score your leads.** Directing your sales team's time toward opportunities with a high likelihood of winning directly impacts the win rate. Building [a step-by-step B2B lead scoring model](/blog/b2b-lead-scoring-model) is a good starting point.
- **Record loss reasons.** Make it mandatory to select a standard reason for every lost opportunity. These reasons could include categories such as price, timing, competitor or failure to reach the decision-maker. After three months, you'll see which reasons dominate.
- **Disqualify early.** Closing a poor-fit opportunity at the discovery stage is better than losing it at the proposal stage. Your win rate goes up and your cycle gets shorter.

### Increasing average deal size

- Review your packaging. Offer multi-year contracts or add-on module options.
- Analyze your won deals. Which customer profiles bring in larger deals? Shift your prospecting priority toward those profiles.
- Enforce discipline around discount authority. Unapproved discounts quietly erode average deal size.

### Shortening the sales cycle

- **Analyze time spent at each stage.** Find out which stage the cycle stalls in the most. In most B2B sales, the bottleneck lies between proposal and signature.
- **Automate follow-ups.** Post-meeting summary emails, reminders and alerts for stalled opportunities shouldn't be left to human memory. Even a simple workflow that automatically creates a task for the rep when an opportunity sits idle for 7 days makes a difference in the cycle.
- **Involve decision-makers early.** The most common factor that drags out the cycle is new stakeholders appearing at the end of the process. Clarify the buying process and approval chain during discovery.
- **Keep sales materials ready.** Preparing materials such as security documentation, reference case studies and standard contract templates from scratch every time costs you days.

## How can you track pipeline velocity automatically?

It's possible to calculate pipeline velocity manually in Excel every month. However, this method is error-prone and is usually abandoned after a few months. A more sustainable approach looks like this:

1. **Standardize fields in your CRM.** Make sure the opportunity creation date, stage transition dates, close date, won/lost status, realized amount and segment fields are mandatory and filled in consistently.
2. **Set up an automated weekly calculation.** Create a workflow that pulls data from your CRM, calculates the four variables by segment and writes the result to a spreadsheet or dashboard.
3. **Define threshold alerts.** Automatically notify the sales manager when pipeline velocity falls below a certain percentage of the previous 4-week average. The notification should also indicate which variable caused the drop.
4. **Use AI to interpret the results.** Instead of raw numbers, generating short weekly reports that summarize changes in the variables and list possible causes shortens the manager's decision-making time.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good pipeline velocity value?

There's no universal "good" value. Pipeline velocity varies widely depending on deal size, industry, sales model and company size. What matters is comparing your own value over time and across segments, and checking whether it's enough to meet your revenue target.

### How often should pipeline velocity be calculated?

For most B2B teams, weekly tracking and monthly review strike a good balance. For historical variables such as win rate and cycle length, it's generally healthier to calculate them over a window of the last 6 to 12 months so that enough data accumulates. For companies that close a small number of deals, very short periods produce statistical noise.

### What is the difference between pipeline velocity and pipeline size?

Pipeline size is a static snapshot showing the total value of open opportunities. Pipeline velocity, by contrast, also accounts for the likelihood of winning those opportunities and the time they take to close, measuring the pace of daily revenue generation. A large but slow, low-converting pipeline can generate less revenue than a small but fast one.

### Is pipeline velocity meaningful for a small sales team?

Yes. In fact, it's even more valuable for small teams when deciding where to spend their resources. However, if the number of closed deals is low, the win rate and cycle length can fluctuate based on just a handful of deals. In that case, you should use a longer data window and read the result as a directional indicator rather than a precise figure.

## Conclusion

Pipeline velocity is a simple yet powerful metric that measures sales performance through the combined effect of four core variables. The formula is straightforward: multiply the number of qualified opportunities, the win rate and the average deal size, then divide by the sales cycle length. Its value, however, depends on properly defined data, segment-level breakdowns and regular tracking. Even small improvements in each variable add up to significant overall growth. Shortening the cycle, meanwhile, is usually the most overlooked lever.
