**A sales quota is set by matching the company's revenue target to each rep's realistic selling capacity.** The core formula is: *Quota per rep = (Company target × over-assignment factor) ÷ number of effective reps.* Check that figure against capacity calculated from the bottom up: *Average deal size × win rate × number of manageable opportunities.* If the two figures are close, the quota is defensible. If there is a large gap, revisit either the target or the resource plan.

At many B2B companies, quotas are still set with "let's add 30 percent to last year." This approach is easy in the short term, but it quietly undermines team motivation, forecast accuracy and hiring plans. In this article, we walk through a concrete example showing how to calculate quota step by step, how to account for ramp time and attrition, and how to distribute quota fairly across reps.

## What exactly is a sales quota, and what types are there?

A sales quota is a measurable target that a rep or team is expected to reach within a given period. It is usually defined monthly, quarterly or annually, and it forms the basis of commission plans. The quota types most commonly used by B2B teams are:

- **Revenue quota:** The total value of closed deals (new ARR, total contract value or collected revenue). This is the most common type.
- **Volume quota:** A set number of deals, licenses or units. Works well for products with standard pricing.
- **Margin or profitability quota:** In industries where discounting has grown out of control, gross profit is targeted instead of revenue.
- **Activity quota:** Input metrics such as calls, emails, meetings and demos. Used especially for SDR/BDR roles.
- **Pipeline quota:** A target for generating a set amount of qualified opportunities. In outbound teams, it is a leading indicator of the outcome quota.

Most mature teams combine these. For example, an AE (Account Executive) is given a revenue quota, while an SDR is given a qualified-meeting and pipeline quota. To clarify which metrics you should track, see our [KPI guide to measuring sales team productivity](/blog/how-to-measure-sales-team-productivity-kpis).

## Why are quotas so often set incorrectly?

Quota mistakes usually stem from a single source: **never comparing the target to capacity.** The board sets a growth number, that number is divided by the number of reps, and that's it. This method leaves the following questions unanswered:

- Will newly hired reps sell at full productivity in their first months?
- If someone leaves the team during the year, who absorbs their target?
- Can the current pipeline and lead flow actually support this target?
- Do all regions or segments have the same potential?

A quota set without asking these questions is either so high it's unreachable, pushing even the best reps toward burnout, or lower than it should be, leaving money on the table for the company. Either way, the quota loses credibility. And a team that doesn't believe in its quota starts giving up in the first quarter.

## Top-down or bottom-up?

There are two basic approaches to setting quotas. A healthy quota sits where the two intersect.

### The top-down approach

You start from the company's annual revenue target. This target is derived from investor expectations, the growth plan, cash needs and market share goals. You then distribute that figure across teams and reps. The advantage is that it ties directly to the company's strategic goals. The disadvantage is that it can ignore reality in the field.

### The bottom-up approach

You start from a single rep's realistic capacity. How many qualified opportunities can a rep manage per month, how many of them do they win, and what is the average deal size? Multiplying these figures gives you productive capacity per rep, and multiplying by team size gives you total capacity. The advantage is that it's realistic. The disadvantage is that by projecting past performance into the future, it can cap growth ambitions.

**The right method:** Run both calculations and discuss the gap. If the gap is around 10–15 percent, you can move forward with minor adjustments. If the gap is 40 percent, the problem isn't the quota, it's the resource plan. You need more reps, more leads or higher conversion.

## The sales quota formula: Step-by-step calculation

The example below is built for a B2B software team selling to new customers. The figures are purely illustrative. Replace them with your own data.

### Step 1: Clarify the company target

Let's say the revenue the company wants from new customers next year is **12,000,000 TL**. Clarify from the outset whether this figure covers only new sales or also expansion and renewals. Different revenue types are usually owned by different teams and should not be lumped into a single number.

### Step 2: Calculate productive capacity per rep

Look at your historical CRM data. In our example, the values are:

- Average deal size: **150,000 TL**
- Qualified opportunity-to-win conversion rate: **20%**
- Number of new qualified opportunities an AE can manage per month: **8**

Calculation:

- Annual opportunities: 8 × 12 = **96 opportunities**
- Deals won: 96 × 0.20 = **19.2 deals**
- Productive capacity: 19.2 × 150,000 = **2,880,000 TL / year**

This figure represents the revenue a fully ramped rep can realistically generate with current processes. How reliable these conversion rates are depends on how clearly your pipeline stages are defined. Our [guide to defining sales pipeline stages](/blog/how-to-define-sales-pipeline-stages) is a good starting point.

### Step 3: Determine the number of effective reps (including ramp and attrition)

Headcount and effective selling capacity are not the same thing. In our example, the team looks like this:

- 4 experienced, fully ramped AEs
- 2 new AEs starting in January

Ramp assumption for new reps:

- Months 1–3: 0% productivity (training, learning the product, building pipeline)
- Months 4–6: 50% productivity
- Months 7–12: 100% productivity

A new rep's effective annual contribution: (3 × 0) + (3 × 0.5) + (6 × 1) = 7.5 months. That equals 7.5 ÷ 12 = **0.625 full-time equivalent (FTE)**.

Total: 4 + (2 × 0.625) = **5.25 FTE**

Let's add a **10% attrition allowance** for departures during the year and the gaps until replacements are hired:

5.25 × 0.90 = **4.725 effective FTE**

This step is usually skipped, and it is one of the most common reasons quotas are missed. A team of 6 on paper may actually spend the year with the capacity of fewer than 5 people.

### Step 4: Compare total capacity with the target

Total productive capacity: 4.725 × 2,880,000 = **approximately 13,600,000 TL**

With a company target of 12,000,000 TL, capacity exceeds the target by about 13%. That's a healthy picture. Had capacity fallen short of the target, hiring, lead generation or conversion improvements should have been discussed before setting the quota.

### Step 5: Apply the over-assignment factor

No team has every rep hitting 100% of quota. That's why the total quota distributed to reps is kept somewhat higher than the company target. The commonly used range is **10–25%**. The newer your team and the more uncertain your forecast, the higher the factor should be.

In our example, let's use 10% over-assignment:

12,000,000 × 1.10 = **13,200,000 TL total quota to distribute**

### Step 6: Calculate quota per rep

13,200,000 ÷ 4.725 = **approximately 2,790,000 TL / fully ramped rep**

- Annual quota for experienced AEs: **~2,790,000 TL**
- First-year quota for new AEs: 2,790,000 × 0.625 = **~1,745,000 TL**

### Step 7: Sanity-check the numbers

Now compare quota with capacity. A fully ramped rep has a capacity of 2,880,000 TL and a quota of 2,790,000 TL. Because the quota sits just below capacity, it is a **challenging but achievable** target.

Let's run one more check: 2,790,000 ÷ 150,000 = 18.6 deals. At a 20% win rate, that means roughly 93 qualified opportunities per year, or 7.75 per month. This falls within the rep's capacity of 8 manageable opportunities. However, pipeline generation also needs to be planned so that this many opportunities actually reach the rep.

## How do you tie quota to pipeline and lead generation?

A quota isn't just a revenue figure. Reversed, it is also a **pipeline requirements plan.** For the team in our example to reach 12 million TL, it needs roughly 80 deals per year and, at 20% conversion, around 400 qualified opportunities. If meeting-to-qualified-opportunity conversion is 50%, that means about 800 first meetings.

This reverse calculation tells you something important: for the quota to hold, you need to know in advance what volume the SDR team, marketing and outbound campaigns must generate. ## How do you distribute quota fairly across reps?

Giving everyone the same quota is easy, but it isn't always fair. A rep managing enterprise accounts in Istanbul and a rep covering the SMB segment in Anatolia may not have the same potential. For fair distribution, consider these factors:

- **Region or segment potential:** Map out the number of target accounts in each region and their estimated budgets. If you've clarified your [ideal customer profile (ICP)](/blog/how-to-define-ideal-customer-profile-b2b), this calculation becomes far more reliable.
- **Existing account portfolio:** Inheriting a warm pipeline is a significant advantage over a territory starting from scratch. Balance this in the quota.
- **Deal size and cycle length:** The enterprise segment has fewer but larger deals. Sales cycles can stretch to 6–9 months, which affects when deals close during the year.
- **Experience and ramp status:** As shown above, give new reps a prorated quota.

Weighting by regional potential can be done simply. Treat total market potential as 100% and calculate each region's share. Distribute total quota according to these shares. Then apply manual adjustments in the 5–10 percent range for experience and inherited pipeline.

## How do you spread quota across the year?

Dividing annual quota by 12 ignores seasonality. In B2B sales, budget cycles, holidays and year-end closes noticeably affect the rhythm of sales. Build a seasonality profile from the monthly distribution of closed deals over the past two to three years.

A sample quarterly distribution:

- Q1: 20% (budgets are just being approved, decision processes are slow)
- Q2: 25%
- Q3: 23% (summer slowdown)
- Q4: 32% (year-end budget spending and pressure to close)

With this distribution, an experienced AE's annual quota of 2,790,000 TL comes to roughly 558,000 TL in Q1 and roughly 893,000 TL in Q4. This approach both protects rep motivation and makes management's monthly forecasts more realistic. ## How should quota relate to compensation (OTE)?

Quota means little on its own. It should be evaluated together with the total pay a rep earns when hitting target (OTE, on-target earnings). A frequently used benchmark in B2B software sales is for annual quota to be about **4–5 times** OTE. This ratio varies by industry, margin and sales cycle. It should be used as a health check, not a hard rule.

If the ratio is too low, your cost of sales is too high relative to revenue. If it's too high, you're either expecting an unreachable target from the rep or paying below market. Both lead to talent loss.

Key points to watch in your commission structure:

- **Accelerators:** Increasing the commission rate once quota exceeds 100% helps retain top performers.
- **Avoid caps:** A commission cap can lead reps to push large deals into the next period.
- **Threshold:** Paying no commission below a certain percentage of quota (for example, 50%) establishes a minimum performance standard.

## When do activity quotas make sense?

A revenue quota measures the outcome. An activity quota measures the path to the outcome. Activity and pipeline quotas are especially useful for SDR teams, newly hired reps and products with long sales cycles, because revenue results only show up months later.

Set activity quotas using the reverse calculation as well. For example, if an SDR is expected to book 20 qualified meetings a month and roughly 2% of contacted prospects convert to a meeting, the SDR needs to reach about 1,000 people per month. When these prospects are approached through a sequence with multiple touchpoints, the number of activities grows even further.

A word of caution, though: if an activity quota rewards volume over quality, the team starts sending templated messages to poorly targeted lists. That lowers both reply rates and domain reputation. So always pair activity quotas with an outcome metric such as **meetings or qualified opportunities**.

## How do AI and automation change quota planning?

Automation directly affects two variables in the quota formula: **manageable opportunities per rep** and **conversion rates.**

- **Data preparation and research:** When building lead lists, enriching contact data and researching accounts are automated, reps spend more time selling. An AE who can manage 8 opportunities a month might move up to 10 once administrative load drops. That directly raises the capacity calculation.
- **Lead prioritization:** Scoring models help reps focus on the highest-probability opportunities and can improve win rates.
- **Call notes and CRM updates:** Automated note-taking and syncing to the CRM both save time and improve the quality of the historical data you'll use for quota planning.
- **Early warning:** Automated alerts when pipeline coverage drops or a rep's activity declines prevent end-of-quarter surprises.

An important caveat here: don't raise quotas in advance on the assumption that automation will increase capacity. First see the improvement in the data, then reflect it in the next period's quota. Otherwise, reps end up paying for a productivity gain that hasn't happened yet.

## The most common mistakes when setting quotas

- **Ignoring ramp time:** Giving a new rep full quota from month one hurts morale and inflates the team target on paper.
- **Blindly growing last year's number:** "We did 10 million last year, let's make it 13 this year" ignores changes in capacity and the market.
- **Calculating with dirty CRM data:** Opportunities with outdated close dates and incorrectly marked stages distort conversion rates.
- **Constantly changing quotas mid-year:** Except when unavoidable, quota changes erode trust. If a change is necessary, the reasoning should be shared transparently.
- **Failing to plan pipeline generation:** A quota gets set, but nobody owns the lead flow that's supposed to feed it.
- **Leaving reps out of the process:** Getting reps' own forecasts for their territories before setting quotas provides a reality check and increases ownership.

## How can you tell whether a quota is healthy?

A quota isn't a number you set at the start of the year and forget. Track these indicators quarterly:

- **Percentage of reps hitting quota:** If most of the team is falling far short, the problem is most likely systemic rather than individual. If nearly everyone is easily exceeding quota, it was set too low.
- **Pipeline coverage ratio:** Open pipeline value relative to remaining quota. If your conversion rate is 20%, you'd want to see roughly 3–5 times the remaining quota in pipeline.
- **Performance distribution:** Quota attainment concentrated in a handful of people may signal unfairness in territory allocation.
- **Ramp tracking:** Are new reps progressing in line with your ramp assumption? If not, you need to update your next hiring plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### By what percentage should sales quota be over-assigned?

Common practice is to set the total quota distributed to reps 10–25% above the company target. Experienced, stable teams can work closer to the lower end. Newly formed teams, teams with many reps still ramping, or teams with high forecast uncertainty should use a factor closer to the upper end.

### How do you set quota for a newly hired sales rep?

New reps are given a low or zero quota in their first months, and quota is increased gradually according to the ramp plan. For example, you might apply 0% quota for the first three months, 50% for the next three, and 100% from month seven onward. Ramp time should be based on product complexity and the average sales cycle.

### Should quota be set monthly, quarterly or annually?

Quota is usually planned annually and tracked by breaking it into quarters. For businesses with short sales cycles, a monthly quota can boost motivation. For B2B sales with cycles longer than three months, a quarterly quota more fairly smooths out natural fluctuations in deal timing.

### If quota can't be reached, should it be lowered mid-year?

Quota should only be revised when there is a major shift in market conditions, a product issue, or a fundamental change in territory structure. Lowering quota simply because performance is weak undermines the credibility of future quotas. First, examine pipeline generation, conversion rates and ramp assumptions to find the root of the problem.

## Conclusion

The right sales quota is the bridge between the company's growth target and the team's actual capacity. The formula is simple: multiply the target by the over-assignment factor and divide by the number of effective reps. Then validate that figure with a bottom-up calculation derived from deal size, win rate and opportunity capacity. When you account for ramp time, attrition, territory potential and seasonality, quota stops being a pressure tool and becomes a roadmap the team believes in.
