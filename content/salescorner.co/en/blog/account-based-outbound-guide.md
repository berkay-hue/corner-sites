Account-based outbound means your sales team focuses on a limited number of preselected, high-value companies instead of reaching out to broad, random lists. Within these companies, you reach multiple decision-makers in a coordinated way through email, LinkedIn and phone, using messages built on account-specific research. In short, the question is no longer "how many people did we reach?" but **"how many of our 50 target companies did we get inside the buying committee of?"**

In traditional outbound, success usually depends on volume: a list of thousands of contacts, a templated email sequence and a low but "good enough" reply rate. The account-based approach deliberately lowers volume and increases depth. In this guide, we walk through the process step by step, from selecting target accounts and tiering them to mapping decision-makers, designing multichannel campaigns and measuring at the account level. At every step, you'll also find actionable examples and numbers.

## What is account-based outbound, and how is it different from traditional outbound?

Account-based outbound is the sales-side counterpart of account-based marketing (ABM). ABM mostly describes how marketing teams "warm up" specific accounts through ads, content and events. Account-based outbound covers the direct, proactive outreach that SDRs and AEs carry out with those same accounts.

The key differences can be summarized as follows:

- **Unit of targeting:** Traditional outbound targets individuals; account-based outbound targets companies. One person at a company not replying doesn't mean the campaign is over. You work with 4-8 people at the same account in parallel.
- **List size:** In traditional outbound, thousands of contacts per month is considered normal. In the account-based model, an SDR typically works on 30-100 accounts at a time.
- **Level of personalization:** In the traditional approach, personalization is often limited to first name and company name. In the account-based approach, the message is built on the company's current situation (investment, new market, hiring, regulatory change).
- **Success metric:** Instead of reply rate, you track account engagement, meetings per account and pipeline value per account.
- **Sales and marketing alignment:** In account-based outbound, marketing supports the same accounts with ads and content. Both teams work from a shared target account list.

## Which companies is account-based outbound right for?

Not every B2B company needs to switch to this model. If at least two of the following conditions apply to you, account-based outbound deserves serious consideration:

- **Your average contract value is high.** If the annual value of a single customer easily covers several weeks of an SDR's cost, in-depth research makes economic sense.
- **Your total addressable market is limited.** For example, if you sell to manufacturers in Turkey above a certain revenue level, or to banks in a particular segment, your universe is already limited to a few hundred companies. These accounts need to be worked carefully rather than "burned."
- **The buying committee is large.** If the decision isn't made by one person but jointly by IT, finance, operations and senior management, outbound that focuses on a single person falls short.
- **The sales cycle is long.** In 3-12 month cycles, building relationships across the whole account is safer than depending on a single champion.

By contrast, for low-ticket products with fast decisions and a single buyer (for example, a low-cost monthly tool for small businesses), traditional, volume-driven outbound is usually more efficient. Many companies build a hybrid model: the most valuable accounts are worked account-by-account, while the rest of the market is covered with automated sequences.

## Step 1: Define your ideal customer profile at the account level

The foundation of account-based outbound is clearly defining which companies are truly the "right accounts" for you. The focus here is not on the buyer persona but on the **company profile**. If you haven't done this work yet, [our guide to defining your ideal customer profile (ICP)](/blog/how-to-define-ideal-customer-profile-b2b) is a good starting point.

When defining your ICP at the account level, consider the following criteria:

- **Firmographic data:** Industry, headcount, revenue range, geography, number of branches or plants.
- **Technographic data:** The CRM, ERP, cloud infrastructure or competitor products they use. For example, if you sell an integration for companies using a specific ERP, this criterion alone narrows down the list.
- **Structural indicators:** Size of the sales team, whether they have a separate operations or procurement department, whether they export.
- **Negative criteria:** Also write down which companies you don't want to work with. Very small budgets, long and bureaucratic tender processes, or business models that don't fit your product fall into this category.

Here's a practical method: Take the 10-20 accounts among your current customers that are the most profitable, closed the fastest and churn the least, and identify what they have in common. The common traits of accounts you lost or that churned will define your negative criteria.

## Step 2: Build your target account list and break it into tiers

Once your ICP is clear, you build a **target account list** (TAL) of companies that match this profile. In account-based outbound, the most critical decision is how to tier this list. It isn't possible to put the same depth of effort into every account.

A common tiering model looks like this:

- **Tier 1 (one-to-one):** The 10-30 accounts with the highest potential. For each one, you prepare a separate research file, account-specific messages and, if needed, custom content (a short analysis, a cost estimate, an industry-specific case story). Personalization is at its highest level.
- **Tier 2 (one-to-few):** 50-150 accounts. Accounts with similar characteristics are grouped into small clusters. For example, "mid-sized textile manufacturers that export" would be one cluster. Messages are personalized at the cluster level, with 1-2 custom sentences added for each account.
- **Tier 3 (one-to-many):** 200-1,000 accounts. Largely automated sequences, personalized at the segment level, are used. As accounts in this tier show engagement, they are promoted to Tier 2.

A simple scoring table makes it easier to decide which account goes into which tier. For example:

1. ICP fit (0-40 points)
2. Estimated contract value (0-30 points)
3. Current buying signal (0-20 points)
4. Existing relationship or referral connection (0-10 points)

You might set thresholds such as 75+ points for Tier 1, 50-74 for Tier 2 and anything below for Tier 3. Update the thresholds at the end of the first quarter based on actual results. You're very likely to find that accounts that look perfect on paper show no engagement at all.

## Step 3: Research accounts and collect buying signals

What separates account-based outbound from templated outbound is research. But research shouldn't go on forever. A reasonable upper limit is 30-60 minutes for a Tier 1 account and 10-15 minutes for a Tier 2 account.

For each account, look for answers to these questions:

- **What is the company focused on right now?** Entering a new market, a funding round, a merger, a new facility, a digital transformation project, and so on.
- **Which roles are they hiring for?** For example, a company looking for 5 sales reps at once may have an urgent need around sales productivity.
- **Are there changes in leadership?** A newly hired sales director or CIO tends to re-evaluate processes in their first 90 days.
- **Which tools do they use, and which competitors do they work with?** An account using a competitor's product calls for a different message.
- **What problems do they mention in public statements?** Annual reports, interviews and executives' LinkedIn posts are valuable sources here.

A significant part of this research can now be accelerated with AI. An AI agent can scan a company's website, news and job postings and produce a half-page summary for each account. Instead of researching from scratch, the SDR's job becomes verifying this summary and choosing the strongest "entry point." However, don't put an AI-generated summary into a message without verifying it. A wrong investment figure or a wrong executive name destroys trust in one go in account-based outbound.

## Step 4: Build a decision-maker map for each account

In account-based outbound, writing to just one person is like knocking on a single door and leaving the house if it doesn't open. You need to identify the roles that make up the buying committee at each target account. We covered this topic in detail in our article on [mapping decision-makers in B2B sales](/blog/b2b-decision-maker-map). Here, let's summarize the points that matter most for account-based outbound.

A typical B2B buying committee includes the following roles:

- **Economic buyer:** The person who approves the budget (CFO, general manager, business unit head).
- **Champion:** The person who feels the problem most and will advocate for the solution internally (usually a department manager).
- **Users:** Team members who will use the product day to day.
- **Technical evaluator:** The person who signs off on the IT, security or integration side.
- **Blocker:** Procurement, legal or stakeholders who resist change.

The practical goal is this: **map 5-8 people in Tier 1 accounts, 3-5 in Tier 2 and 1-3 in Tier 3.** Give each person a role tag and tailor your messages to that role. With the finance director, you lead with cost and payback period; with the operations manager, with time savings and process simplification; with the IT manager, with integration and security.

## Step 5: Enrich and verify contact data

You can't build a multichannel campaign without accurate email addresses, phone numbers and current job titles for the people you've mapped. In account-based outbound, data quality is even more critical than in traditional outbound. Because you're working with a limited number of accounts, every wrong contact means a missed opportunity.

Steps to follow at this stage:

1. **Finding people:** Match the roles on your map with real names. B2B data platforms like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) provide a practical starting point for listing people within an account using company, department and title filters.
2. **Enrichment:** Fill in email, phone, LinkedIn profile, seniority and department information. In the Turkish market, the coverage of global databases can be weak in some industries, so an approach that combines multiple sources is more reliable. In [our B2B data enrichment guide](/blog/b2b-data-enrichment-turkey-guide), we cover Turkey-specific sources and methods in detail.
3. **Verification:** Always verify emails before sending. Because account-based outbound targets a small number of domains, a high bounce rate damages both your sender reputation and how you're perceived by that company's email filters.
4. **Compliance check:** Document which channel you use and on what grounds under KVKK (Turkey's personal data protection law) and commercial electronic messaging regulations. Even in B2B communication sent to corporate addresses, don't neglect the legitimate interest assessment and an opt-out mechanism.

All of this data needs to be grouped in your CRM **under the account, not the contact.** That way, when an SDR opens an account, they can see all the contacts, interactions and next steps at that company on a single screen.

## Step 6: Design your multichannel campaign

In account-based outbound, channels complement each other. Email alone isn't enough, phone alone doesn't scale, and LinkedIn alone doesn't provide depth. The goal is to tell a consistent story to different people at the same account through different channels.

A sample 4-week plan for a Tier 1 account could look like this:

- **Week 1:**
  - LinkedIn connection request to the potential champion (without a note, or with a very short one).
  - First account-specific email to the economic buyer.
  - On the marketing side, LinkedIn ads targeting people at the account go live.
- **Week 2:**
  - First email to the champion (problem-focused).
  - Short, value-focused email to 1-2 people in user roles.
  - Follow-up email to the economic buyer.
- **Week 3:**
  - Phone call to the champion.
  - Integration-focused email to the technical evaluator.
  - Meaningful engagement with posts from people who accepted your connection request.
- **Week 4:**
  - Signals from everyone at the account are reviewed.
  - The question "who's the right person?" is asked through whoever replied.
  - If there's no response, the account is put on hold to be revisited 6-8 weeks later from a different angle.

When it comes to the number and spacing of emails sent to each person, the principles from our article on [how many steps an outbound email sequence should have](/blog/outbound-email-sequence-steps-timing) apply here too. The only difference is this: in account-based outbound, sequences going to different people at the same company must be coordinated in timing. Five people at the same company receiving the exact same email on the same day instantly undermines your claim to personalization.

The LinkedIn side isn't just about connection requests. If executives at your target accounts have already seen you before your message arrives, your chances of getting a reply go up. That's why having your company and your sales team regularly publish valuable content on their profiles is part of outbound. ## Step 7: How do you write an account-specific message?

The formula for an account-based message is simple, but executing it takes discipline: **Observation + implication + value + low-friction question.**

- **Observation:** A verifiable, current piece of information about the account.
- **Implication:** A likely business consequence or challenge stemming from that observation.
- **Value:** A brief insight into how companies in a similar situation have handled that challenge.
- **Question:** Instead of a meeting request, an easy-to-answer curiosity question.

Example (to the operations director of a manufacturing company):

> Hi Ayşe,
>
> I saw that you opened your second facility in Manisa last month. I also noticed you've posted openings for three different planning roles at the same time.
>
> At companies that start running production planning across two facilities with a single team, the biggest time sink in the first few months is usually that inventory and capacity data for each site is kept in separate spreadsheets.
>
> We've put together a short note summarizing how manufacturers at a similar growth stage have managed this transition.
>
> On the planning side, is your biggest challenge right now the data, or team capacity?

The message to the finance director at the same account is built on the same observation (the second facility) but from a different angle: the payback period on the investment, visibility into operating costs, and so on. This creates a narrative within the account that is consistent yet role-specific.

Things to avoid:

- Reading the "about us" text from the company's website back to them.
- Opening with a "congratulations" on your observation and jumping straight into a product pitch.
- Asking for a 30-minute demo in the first email.
- Writing more than three paragraphs. An account-based message is built on in-depth research, but it should be short.

## Step 8: How do you measure account-based outbound?

If you measure account-based outbound with traditional outbound metrics, you'll reach the wrong conclusions. Because send volume is low, the absolute number of meetings may not look impressive in the first few weeks. Track account-level metrics instead:

- **Account coverage rate:** What percentage of target accounts have you made contact with at least one person? By the end of the first month, you should have made contact at every Tier 1 account.
- **Account penetration:** The average number of people reached per account. If it's below 5 in Tier 1, your mapping is incomplete.
- **Engaged account rate:** The share of accounts where at least one person replied, accepted a meeting or showed meaningful engagement.
- **Meetings and opportunities per account:** At how many Tier 1 accounts did a qualified first meeting take place?
- **Pipeline value from target accounts:** How much of your total pipeline came from the target list.
- **Buying committee coverage (at the opportunity stage):** How many different roles are you talking to in open opportunities? Opportunities that depend on a single person carry the highest risk of being lost.

Let's walk through an example calculation. Imagine a Tier 1+2 list of 100 accounts. If, by the end of the quarter, you've made contact at 60 accounts, seen engagement at 25 and held a qualified first meeting at 12, your account-to-meeting conversion is 12%. By multiplying this rate by your average opportunity value and win rate, you can estimate the quarterly revenue potential of the target list. With this data, you can decide whether to expand the list or go deeper in the next quarter.

## How many people and how much time does account-based outbound require?

Account-based programs launched without capacity planning usually fall apart in the second month. Let's do a simple calculation.

Assume an SDR dedicates roughly 25 hours of their working week to active prospecting:

- Initial research and mapping per Tier 1 account: ~60 minutes
- Weekly outreach and follow-up per Tier 1 account: ~30 minutes
- Initial prep per Tier 2 account: ~15 minutes; weekly follow-up: ~10 minutes

Under these assumptions, one SDR can effectively manage about **15-20 Tier 1 accounts and 60-80 Tier 2 accounts** at the same time. Since Tier 3 accounts are run largely through automated sequences, they can be added on top of this capacity. Just remember that replies require manual follow-up.

AI-assisted research and draft generation can significantly shorten Tier 1 prep time. The time saved should be used not to cut corners on personalization quality, but to work more accounts in greater depth.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Keeping the list too large:** A 2,000-account "target list" isn't account-based outbound; it's traditional outbound with a new label.
- **Relying on a single person:** When the champion leaves or goes quiet, you lose the entire account.
- **Not reflecting research in the message:** Spending an hour on research and then sending a templated email means all that effort goes to waste.
- **Not coordinating channels:** The same person receiving an email, a LinkedIn message and a phone call on the same day comes across as pushy.
- **Leaving marketing out:** Ads and content warm up cold outreach by making you recognizable at target accounts. Without a shared target list, the two teams spend their energy on different companies.
- **Giving up too early:** In long sales cycles, the number of meetings may look low in the first quarter. If account engagement is rising, the program is on the right track.
- **"Burning" accounts:** Instead of following up with an unresponsive account for months with the same message, put it on pause and come back from a different angle when a new signal appears.

## Where does AI help in account-based outbound?

AI is the most important tool for making account-based outbound scalable. But it doesn't add the same amount of value at every step.

**Where it's strong:**
- Preparing account research summaries (news, job postings, website changes)
- Continuously monitoring buying signals and automatically updating account tiers
- Generating role-based message drafts
- Keeping account records in the CRM clean and up to date
- Classifying incoming replies (interested, not now, wrong person, rejection)

**Where human oversight is essential:**
- Approving the final message for Tier 1 accounts
- Deciding which observation to use as the "entry point"
- Phone calls and first meetings
- Reading the political dynamics within the buying committee

In a well-designed system, the AI agent prepares the research and first draft, while the SDR reviews it and adds the personal touch. That way, both speed and quality are preserved.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is account-based outbound the same as ABM?

No, but they complement each other. ABM covers marketing's efforts to target specific accounts through ads, content and events. Account-based outbound is the sales team reaching decision-makers at those accounts directly via email, phone and LinkedIn. The best results come when both teams work from the same target account list.

### How many companies should be on the target account list?

That depends on your team's capacity. For a single SDR, 15-20 Tier 1 and 60-80 Tier 2 accounts is a reasonable starting range. Measuring account engagement and meeting rates in the first quarter before expanding the list helps you set capacity realistically.

### When do you see results from account-based outbound?

The first engagement signals usually appear within a few weeks. However, meaningful pipeline impact can take one to two quarters, depending on the length of your sales cycle. That's why, in the early period, it's better to track account coverage and engagement rates rather than the number of meetings.

### Can a small team do account-based outbound?

Yes. In fact, it can be even more efficient for small teams, because limited time is concentrated on the most valuable accounts. Even a single person can start with 20-30 well-chosen accounts and make the process manageable with AI-assisted research and automation.

## Conclusion

Account-based outbound is a sales discipline built on "getting inside the right companies" rather than "reaching more people." Its foundation is a clear account-level ICP, a realistic target list broken into tiers, a decision-maker map for every account, verified contact data and a campaign design that coordinates channels. When you measure it with account-level metrics, you can see from the data which accounts deserve more investment.

Repetitive tasks in this process, such as research, enrichment, signal tracking and message drafting, can be accelerated significantly with the right automations.
