An ideal customer profile (ICP) is **a company-level definition of the shared traits of the businesses that get value from your product fastest, close most easily and stay the longest.** An ICP is built from your existing customer data, not from guesswork. You pick your best customers, compare them by traits such as industry, size, technology, need and buying trigger, and turn the recurring patterns into written criteria. The resulting definition decides who you reach out to, who you rule out and what your message emphasizes.

Many B2B teams start outbound with a broad target such as "mid-sized companies" or "tech firms". These definitions are so wide that almost every company fits. The outcome is familiar: low reply rates, meetings that never turn into deals and endless debates about "bad lead quality". In this guide, we explain what an ICP is, how it differs from a persona, how to derive it step by step from your own data and how to use it in your sales process.

## What is the difference between an ICP and a buyer persona?

The two are often confused, but they answer different questions:

- **ICP (ideal customer profile):** *Which company* should we sell to? It works at the company level and describes traits such as industry, headcount, geography, business model, technology stack and the current problem.
- **Buyer persona:** *Who* should we talk to inside that company? It works at the person level and describes role, area of responsibility, priorities, objections and place in the buying process.

The order matters: ICP first, persona second. Reaching the right person at the wrong company and reaching the wrong person at the right company both waste effort. One ICP usually contains several personas. The decision maker, the day-to-day user and the person who signs off the budget each need a different message.

## What data do you need to define an ICP?

The quality of an ICP depends on the quality of the data behind it. Before you start, gather these sources:

- **CRM records:** Won and lost opportunities, sales cycle length, deal size and loss reasons.
- **Customer success and billing data:** Which customers stayed, expanded, bought add-ons or churned.
- **Firmographic data:** Industry, headcount range, headquarters location and growth stage.
- **Technographic data:** The CRM, email, ERP or automation tools your customers use. If your product integrates with a specific tool, this criterion can be decisive.
- **Qualitative data:** Sales call notes and short interviews with customers. Questions like "Why did you choose us?" and "How did you solve this problem before us?" surface patterns that numbers alone do not show.

If you do not have many customers yet, do not skip this step. Start with a smaller data set, write the ICP down as a hypothesis and test it regularly against your outbound results.

## How do you build an ideal customer profile step by step?

1. **Pick your best customers.** Do not define "best" by revenue alone. Consider customers who closed quickly, actually use the product, need little support, stay long and refer you to others.
2. **Build a comparison group.** List lost opportunities and churned customers as well. Nothing clarifies an ICP like the difference between great-fit and poor-fit customers.
3. **Put shared traits in a table.** For each customer, record industry, size, geography, business model, technology stack, buying trigger and the decision maker's role in the same table.
4. **Find the recurring patterns.** Which traits show up often among your best customers and rarely among poor-fit ones? Those are your real ICP criteria.
5. **Write down the problem and the trigger.** The situation the customer is in when they look for you matters as much as company traits. Triggers such as building a new sales team, entering a new market, switching CRMs or manual processes no longer scaling determine timing.
6. **Define exclusion criteria.** Write down who you will not sell to. Criteria such as a budget mismatch, an infrastructure you do not support or a problem you do not solve protect your team's time more than anything else.
7. **Turn the ICP into a one-page document.** Sales, marketing and customer success should all use the same definition. Keeping it somewhere everyone can access shortens "good lead" debates.
8. **Test and update.** Test the ICP with outbound campaigns, track reply and conversion results by segment and revisit the definition periodically.

## Which criteria should an ICP definition include?

A good ICP says both who fits and why they fit. You can use the template below as a starting point:

- **Industry and sub-industry:** For example, a sharper definition such as "B2B software companies with a sales team and a subscription model" instead of just "B2B SaaS".
- **Company size:** A headcount range or sales team size range. Choose the measure that is meaningful for your product.
- **Geography and language:** Markets you can serve and where your message will sound natural.
- **Technology stack:** Tools you integrate with or replace.
- **Business model and sales motion:** Do they run outbound, rely on inbound, and how does their sales cycle work?
- **Core problem:** The problem the customer is trying to solve and that you genuinely solve.
- **Buying triggers:** Events that make you relevant right now, such as job postings, new executive hires, a funding round or a new product launch.
- **Exclusion criteria:** Company types you will not work with even if they look like a fit.

## How do you use an ICP in outbound sales?

An ICP that stays a document is useless. Its real value shows up when it becomes part of daily sales operations:

- **List building:** The target account list is filtered by ICP criteria. Companies that do not match never make it onto the list.
- **Segmentation:** Within the same ICP, you create sub-segments based on different problems or triggers and write a separate message for each.
- **Messaging:** The problem and trigger defined in the ICP are the raw material for the first line of a cold email. To scale personalization, see our guide on [how to write cold emails with AI](/en/blog/how-to-write-cold-emails-with-ai).
- **Qualification:** SDRs and AEs evaluate an opportunity against the ICP criteria, so "qualified" does not mean something different to each person. Our guide on [how to define sales pipeline stages](/en/blog/how-to-define-sales-pipeline-stages) is a good starting point for carrying this shared language into your pipeline.
- **Measurement:** Reply rate, meeting rate and win rate are tracked separately for each ICP segment. This breakdown shows which segment actually works. You can find which metrics to watch in our [sales team productivity KPI guide](/en/blog/how-to-measure-sales-team-productivity-kpis).

## How does automation make ICP work easier?

Writing an ICP once is easy. The hard part is evaluating hundreds of companies against those criteria consistently every day. Automation helps at three points:

- **Data enrichment:** When a new company is added to a list, its industry, size and technology data are filled in automatically.
- **Automatic matching and scoring:** The enriched data is compared with your ICP criteria and each account gets a fit score. Accounts that do not match are filtered out before they enter a sequence.
- **Trigger monitoring:** Signals such as job postings or executive changes are tracked, and matching accounts reach the right rep at the right time.

## What are the most common ICP mistakes?

- **A definition that is too broad:** "All SMBs" is not an ICP. If the definition does not tell you who is left out, it is not sharp enough.
- **Looking only at large customers:** The customer paying the biggest invoice may not be the most profitable or the easiest to sell to.
- **Ignoring lost and churned customers:** Without a comparison group, you cannot see which traits actually make a difference.
- **Relying only on firmographics:** Of two companies in the same industry and size range, only one may have the problem you solve. Problem and trigger criteria capture that difference.
- **Writing the ICP once and forgetting it:** As your product, pricing or market changes, so does your ideal customer.
- **Misalignment between teams:** If marketing works with one definition and sales with another, friction at lead handoff is inevitable.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an ICP the same as a target market?

No. A target market is a broad frame covering every company that could use your product. An ICP describes a narrow slice of that market that gets the most value from your product and is the easiest to win. The target market answers "where can we sell?", while the ICP answers "who should we focus on first?".

### How does a company with no customers define its ICP?

Without existing customer data, the ICP is written as a hypothesis. The founders' industry experience, discovery calls with potential customers and the companies competitors focus on can serve as starting inputs. You then send small outbound campaigns to a few narrow segments and refine the hypothesis based on which segment responds better.

### Can a company have more than one ICP?

Yes. Separate ICPs can be defined for different products, markets or sales motions. Each one, however, needs its own list, its own message and its own measurement. For small teams, it is usually healthier to focus on a single ICP first and move on to the next one only after getting repeatable results in that segment.

### How often should an ICP be updated?

It is good practice to review the ICP at regular intervals, for example every quarter, based on won and lost opportunities. It should be updated right away when a major feature is added to the product, pricing changes or you enter a new market.

## Conclusion

The ideal customer profile is the foundation of outbound sales. It decides who you reach, what you say and which opportunities count as qualified. A good ICP comes from your best customers' data, clearly states who is left out, includes the problem and the buying trigger, and is tested regularly.
