Successful post-trade show lead follow-up starts with breaking the 30 days into clear phases. In the first 48 hours, every contact collected is imported into the CRM and the hottest prospects get a call back. In the first week, you run personalized follow-up. In weeks two and three, you move forward with value-driven content and meeting requests. In week four, every lead is either moved into the pipeline or transferred to a long-term nurturing list. In short, every person you spoke with at your booth should have a **clear status** within 30 days: opportunity, nurturing, or closed.

Many SMEs commit a serious budget to trade shows: booth rental, design, accommodation, samples, and the working days the team loses. Yet after the show, business cards end up in an envelope and notes stay scattered across a few sales reps' phones. By the time someone asks "Who did we get back to?" two weeks later, much of the show's real value has evaporated. In this article, you'll find a day-by-day follow-up plan that an SME with a 3-10 person sales team can put into practice, along with templates, a prioritization method, and measurement metrics.

## Why is post-show follow-up won or lost in the first 48 hours?

At a trade show, visitors walk past dozens of booths in a single day. What makes them remember you is less your product and more the short, personal conversation you had at your booth, something like "You mentioned the downtime per shift on your packaging machine." That memory fades within a few days. Competing booths have spoken to the same person, and they'll most likely send a follow-up message too.

The first 48 hours matter for three reasons:

- **The context is still fresh:** The prospect remembers you, the problem you discussed, and your booth.
- **The first to respond wins:** A buyer with a given need usually starts a deeper conversation with the company that sends the first structured proposal.
- **Team motivation is high:** The team comes back from the show energized. A week later, day-to-day work swallows that energy.

That's why the most critical part of the 30-day plan actually begins before the show.

## What should be ready before you go to the show?

The follow-up process is designed before the show, not after it. If you come back without the following preparations in place, the first 48 hours will be spent cleaning up data.

1. **Lead capture form:** A business card alone isn't enough. Prepare a short form that opens on a tablet or phone. It should include name, company, job title, email, phone, product of interest, needs notes, timing, and an "A/B/C" preliminary classification.
2. **Trade show campaign in your CRM:** Create a lead source or tag in your CRM in the format "Show Name – Year." This lets you measure the show's return later.
3. **Ready-made templates:** Have your first follow-up email, LinkedIn connection note, and meeting invitation templates written before the show.
4. **Division of responsibility:** Which rep will be responsible for which segment, region, or product group? Decide this in advance.
5. **Calendar blocking:** Block out "trade show follow-up" time in the team's calendars for the first two days after the show. Don't schedule other meetings in those slots.

One simple rule will serve you well: **Take at least a one-sentence context note for every person you speak with at the booth.** "Asked about pricing" is not a note. "Unhappy with current supplier's lead times, opening a new line in March" is a useful note.

## Days 0-2: Collect, clean, and import your data into the CRM

On the evening of the show's last day or first thing the next morning, the priority is to gather all the data in one place.

### Step 1: Create a single list

Business cards, form submissions, reps' phone notes, and WhatsApp messages are merged into one spreadsheet. To scan business cards, you can use OCR-enabled apps or your CRM's mobile business card scanner. Always have a person review the results after scanning. OCR can make mistakes, particularly with Turkish characters and email addresses.

### Step 2: Deduplicate and enrich

Three people from the same company may have visited your booth. Group them under the same company record. You can use B2B data platforms to fill in missing email addresses or job titles. For example, tools like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) offer a practical solution for enriching company size, industry, and contact information. Even so, don't automatically add people you didn't speak with at the booth to your follow-up list. The strength of trade show follow-up comes from the fact that a real conversation took place.

### Step 3: Import into the CRM

When importing the cleaned list into your CRM, every record should have these fields filled in:

- Source: Show name and year
- Assigned rep
- Priority class (A/B/C)
- Needs notes
- Next step and its date

Instead of doing this import manually after every show, you can automate it. In a setup where form data lands in Google Sheets, you can use the [n8n method for importing a Sheets lead list into HubSpot](/blog/n8n-google-sheets-to-hubspot-lead-import) to create records automatically, complete with owner assignment. That way, leads flow into your CRM even while the show is still running.

## How should you prioritize trade show leads?

Not every lead deserves the same attention. A team that comes back with 150 contacts and tries to follow up with all of them at the same intensity will neglect the 20 most valuable opportunities. Here's a simple, practical classification:

| Class | Definition | First contact timeframe | Channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A – Hot** | Stated a concrete need, budget, or timeline; spoke with a decision-maker | Within 24 hours | Phone + personal email |
| **B – Warm** | Interested, but timing is unclear or not a decision-maker | Within 48-72 hours | Personal email + LinkedIn |
| **C – Cold** | Picked up a brochure, asked for general information, student or competitor | Within 1 week | Bulk but personalized email |

Let's look at an example: An SME that manufactures industrial equipment returns from an industry trade show with 160 contacts. After cleanup, 140 unique people remain. In classification, 18 are marked A, 47 are marked B, and 75 are marked C. In this case, the sales team's first two days should be dedicated exclusively to the 18 A leads. The B and C groups then become manageable with templates and automation.

To make your classification more robust, you can use BANT or a similar framework. If you'd like to see how this approach works in the field, take a look at [an SME client case study on lead qualification](https://cornergroup.co/blog/agency-lead-qualification-ile-kobi-musteri-vakasi).

## Days 1-7: First contact week

The first week serves a single purpose: **Moving the conversation from the trade show booth to a real business meeting.**

### For A leads (Day 1)

A leads should be called first. Keep the call short: remind them of your conversation at the show, confirm the need you discussed, and propose a 30-minute meeting. After the call, send a short summary email the same day.

Sample email:

> **Subject:** Following up after [Show name] – about your line expansion plans
>
> Hello Ayşe,
>
> Yesterday at our booth, you mentioned the new filling line you'll be bringing online in March and the downtime on your current system. As we discussed on the phone, I'd like to show you, in a 30-minute meeting, the configuration we use with manufacturers of similar capacity.
>
> Would Thursday at 10:00 or Friday at 14:00 work for you?
>
> Best regards,
> Mert

This email works because it references the specific conversation from the show and offers two clear options. Avoid passive closings like "Feel free to get back to us if you'd like more information about our products."

### For B leads (Days 2-3)

Send B leads a personal email and a LinkedIn connection request on the same day. The connection note should be brief: "Hi Can, we talked about warehouse automation at our booth at [show name]. I'd love to stay connected here as well." A company page that publishes content regularly, along with active executive accounts, makes it easier to keep these connections warm over time. For teams looking for professional support in this area, [Social Media Corner's B2B LinkedIn management service](https://socialmediacorner.co) can be a good starting point.

### For C leads (Days 4-7)

The C group receives a segmented email, for example by "food industry," "automotive suppliers," or "distributors." Even when sent in bulk, personalization should include at least two elements: the person's name and the product group they were interested in at the booth. The email can offer a resource prepared for the show: a product catalog, a short case summary, or a show-exclusive offer.

### Should I use WhatsApp?

In Turkey, many B2B buyers share their WhatsApp number at trade shows instead of a business card. WhatsApp is a fast and effective channel. However, commercial electronic message regulations, İYS registration, and consent processes must not be overlooked. You can find the details of what to watch out for in our [guide to WhatsApp follow-up and İYS consent in B2B sales](/blog/whatsapp-b2b-sales-follow-up-iys-consent-guide). The general rule is this: Use this channel with people who gave you their number themselves at the booth and agreed to message you on WhatsApp. Don't send bulk messages.

**First-week goal:** A meeting date should be confirmed with at least half of your A leads. Every B lead should have received at least one personal touchpoint.

## Days 8-14: Value week

The second week is dedicated to keeping the relationship alive with those who didn't respond to your first message, and to clarifying the next step with those you've already met. The most common mistake here is writing again with "Did you see my previous email?" Instead, every follow-up message should carry new value.

Examples of value-driven follow-ups:

- **Short case summary:** "I've attached a one-page summary showing how a manufacturer of similar size planned their transition." If you can't name the client, mentioning the industry and scale is enough.
- **Calculation tool:** A simple cost or payback calculation spreadsheet.
- **Show recap:** A short piece such as "Three trends that stood out at the show." This content can be used both in email and on LinkedIn.
- **Personal video:** A short 60-90 second screen or face-to-camera video that mentions the prospect's name and your conversation at the show.

Sample follow-up sequence (for a B lead who hasn't responded):

1. **Day 2:** Personal first email + LinkedIn connection
2. **Day 6:** Second email with a short case summary
3. **Day 10:** Phone call (if unreachable, leave a voicemail or short note)
4. **Day 13:** Personal LinkedIn message with the show trends content

At this stage, the goal for A leads you've already met is clear: **a second meeting, demo, discovery visit, or request for proposal.** Within 24 hours of the meeting, send a summary and a proposed next step.

## Days 15-21: Meetings and qualification

The third week is when your trade show list turns into a real sales funnel. This week, you answer one question for every lead: **Is this an opportunity, or not yet?**

Topics to clarify in the qualification meeting:

- **Need:** What problem are they trying to solve, and what is it costing them?
- **Timing:** When will the decision or implementation take place?
- **Decision process:** Who's involved? Procurement, the technical team, management?
- **Budget:** Has budget been allocated, or does it still need to be?
- **Alternatives:** Who is their current supplier, and who else are they talking to?

Leads that pass qualification are moved from "Lead" status to "Opportunity/Deal" status in the CRM. Each opportunity gets an estimated value, probability, and expected close date. This lets you see the pipeline the show generated realistically. If you want to run a probability-weighted calculation, the [guide to calculating weighted pipeline value](/blog/how-to-calculate-weighted-pipeline-value) will come in handy at this step.

Let's return to our example scenario: Say meetings were held with 11 of the 18 A leads and 7 of them were moved into the pipeline as opportunities. Of the 47 B leads, 9 agreed to a meeting and 4 became opportunities. In this case, the show has generated 11 active opportunities by the end of week three. These figures differ from industry to industry. What matters is that you measure your own numbers with the same method at every show.

## Days 22-30: Pipeline entry, archiving, and measurement

The final week is closing week. That doesn't mean closing sales, but **closing out the process**. A clear decision is made for every lead:

1. **Opportunity:** In the pipeline, with a clear owner and next step.
2. **Nurturing:** There's interest, but the timing is far off. Assign a monthly newsletter, LinkedIn content, and a reminder task for 60-90 days later.
3. **Closed – Not a fit:** Not in the target profile, a competitor, or no need. Closed with a note.
4. **Unreachable:** No response despite four or five different touchpoints. Moved to the nurturing list, with a single re-engagement attempt scheduled three months later.

There should be no "open lead without a next step" left in the CRM. This rule is the single most important discipline that makes trade show follow-up measurable.

### Prepare your trade show report

On day 30, a one-page report for management or the team should include:

- Total number of contacts and number of unique companies
- A/B/C distribution
- First contact rate and average time to first contact
- Number of meetings and meeting conversion rate
- Number of opportunities moved into the pipeline and their total/weighted value
- Cost per lead and cost per opportunity

Dividing your trade show budget by the number of contacts gives you your cost per lead. Comparing this figure with your digital channels helps you make sound decisions about which shows to attend next year. You can find the calculation details in our [guide to calculating B2B cost per lead (CPL)](/blog/how-to-calculate-b2b-cost-per-lead-cpl). For example, if a show with a total cost of TRY 450,000 produced 140 unique leads and 11 opportunities, your cost per lead would be roughly TRY 3,200 and your cost per opportunity roughly TRY 41,000. Combined with your average contract value and close rate, this figure shows whether the show was truly profitable.

## Which metrics should you track weekly?

Hold a 20-minute "trade show follow-up meeting" every Monday for the 30 days and review this table:

| Metric | Week 1 target | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First contact with A leads | 100% | – | – | – |
| First contact with B leads | 100% | – | – | – |
| Number of meetings | Half of A leads | Increase | Increase | – |
| Leads converted to opportunities | – | First opportunities | Main period | Final |
| Leads without a next step | – | – | – | 0 |

These targets are examples and should be adapted to your own sales cycle. What matters is that the targets are written down in advance and that you review the same table every week.

## What tools does this process require, and what does it cost?

You don't need an expensive tech stack for trade show follow-up. A sufficient toolkit for an SME looks like this:

- **CRM:** A CRM such as HubSpot, Pipedrive, or Zoho. Free or entry-level plans are often sufficient for post-show follow-up. What matters is that they offer source tagging, task assignment, and email templates.
- **Form/business card scanner:** Your CRM's mobile app or a simple form tool.
- **Data enrichment:** A B2B data tool to fill in missing information.
- **Automation:** Form-to-CRM integration, task creation, and reminders with n8n, Make, or Zapier.
- **LinkedIn:** A standard account is enough for most teams. If you're doing intensive prospecting, Sales Navigator is worth considering.

The real cost isn't software, it's **time**. For a 140-lead trade show list, you can roughly expect the following workload: in the first two days, half a day of the entire sales team's time goes to data cleanup and A leads. ## The most common mistakes in post-show follow-up

- **"The same email for everyone":** A bulk "Thank you for visiting our booth" email that doesn't reference your conversation at the show is indistinguishable from dozens of other messages in the prospect's inbox.
- **Giving up after one touchpoint:** No response to your first message doesn't mean lack of interest. Buyers' inboxes are full after a show. Plan 4-5 different touchpoints.
- **Leads without an owner:** A lead that the "whole team will follow up on" usually gets followed up by no one.
- **Tracking outside the CRM:** Conversations that live in a rep's personal Excel file or WhatsApp disappear when that rep leaves.
- **Not measuring:** If you don't know how many opportunities and how much revenue the show brought in, next year's trade show budget will be set by guesswork.
- **Stopping on day 30:** Leads moved to the nurturing list shouldn't be forgotten. A prospect who says "not yet" today could be your hottest opportunity six months from now.

## Frequently asked questions

### When should the first post-show follow-up email be sent?

Hot (Class A) leads should be contacted within 24 hours of the show ending, ideally with a phone call first, followed by a short summary email. For warm leads, 48-72 hours is sufficient, and for cold leads who showed general interest, first contact within a week is enough. The longer you wait, the less likely the prospect is to remember you and the topic you discussed.

### How many follow-up messages should you send to a trade show lead?

Over the 30-day process, 4-6 touchpoints spread across different channels is a reasonable number: email, phone, LinkedIn, and WhatsApp where appropriate. Each message should contain new value, such as a case summary, calculation tool, or industry insight. If there's no response after five or six touchpoints, the lead should be moved to the nurturing list and re-approached a few months later.

### How should I import trade show leads into my CRM?

The most reliable method is to use a digital form at the show and import the data into your CRM automatically, either directly or via a spreadsheet. Each record should include the source (show name), assigned rep, priority class, needs notes, and next step. If you're working with business cards, always run a manual review and deduplication after scanning.

### How do I measure whether a trade show was successful?

Divide the total cost of the show by the number of unique leads to calculate cost per lead, and by the number of opportunities to calculate cost per opportunity. On day 30, match the total and weighted value of the opportunities moved into the pipeline, and a few months later, the closed deals, to the trade show source. Comparing these figures with your other lead channels reveals the show's true return.

## Conclusion

Post-trade show lead follow-up is too costly a process to leave to chance. Collecting and prioritizing data in the first 48 hours, making personal contact in the first week, moving leads toward meetings by delivering value in weeks two and three, and giving every lead a clear status in week four turns your trade show investment into a real pipeline. The strength of this plan lies not in its complexity but in its discipline: an owner, a next step, and a date for every lead.
