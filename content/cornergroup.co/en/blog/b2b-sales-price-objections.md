In B2B sales, you handle a price objection by finding the real reason behind it, not by defending your price or rushing to offer a discount. "It's too expensive" rarely signals a genuine lack of budget. More often, it means the value isn't visible enough, the decision-maker isn't convinced, or the buyer is negotiating on reflex. The effective approach follows this sequence: clarify the objection, separate it from other objections, reframe the value using the customer's own numbers and, if needed, offer flexibility in exchange for something in return.

In this article, we explain how to reduce price objections from the outset, how to work through them step by step when they come up, how to respond when you hear "your competitor is cheaper," and how to turn discounting from a reflex into a rule-based process. The examples are written with SMB-scale B2B sales teams and SaaS companies in mind. The logic still applies to any B2B team selling services or products.

## What does a price objection actually mean?

The most common mistake sales reps make is reading every "too expensive" the same way. In reality, there are at least six distinct types of price objection, and each one calls for a different response.

- **Genuine budget constraint:** The customer wants the solution, but there's no budget allocated for this period, or it isn't enough. The issue here isn't persuasion. It's timing and payment structure.
- **Weak value perception:** The customer finds the price high compared with the benefit they expect to get. The problem isn't the price. The value simply hasn't been made concrete.
- **Competitor comparison:** There's a cheaper alternative on the table. More often than not, it's an apples-to-oranges comparison.
- **Lack of authority:** The person you're talking to isn't the one who approves the budget. Saying "it's expensive" is an easier way of saying "I can't sell this upstairs."
- **Risk aversion:** The customer isn't thinking about the price. They're thinking about what a wrong decision would cost them personally. They're more afraid of losing credibility than losing money.
- **Negotiation reflex:** Especially when procurement is involved, saying "it's expensive" is part of the process. You'll hear it no matter what the offer is.

Any response given without making this distinction is a shot in the dark. If you offer a discount to a customer with a value perception problem, you lose margin and confirm the impression that your price was inflated to begin with. And if you deliver an ROI presentation to a procurement specialist acting on negotiation reflex, you're wasting your time.

## Why do price objections usually come up at the final stage of the sale?

A price objection that arrives after the proposal has been sent is usually the bill for a step skipped during discovery. In early meetings, reps focus on presenting the product. They don't find out how much the customer's problem is costing them, how budgets get approved or who is involved in the decision. As a result, the proposal sits in the customer's mind as a cost that isn't tied to any number.

That's why price objection handling really starts long before the objection arrives. The most powerful way to reduce late-stage objections is to strengthen your discovery phase.

## What should you do during discovery to prevent price objections?

### Let the customer calculate the cost of the problem

When value is something you claim, it gets debated. When it's something the customer says themselves, it gets accepted. In discovery calls, use questions like these to put a number on the cost of the problem:

- "How many hours a week does this process currently take from your team?"
- "What's the average value of a missed opportunity for you?"
- "What happens over the next 12 months if this problem isn't solved?"

Let's take a hypothetical example. The sales team has 6 people, and each rep spends about 5 hours a week on manual data entry. That's 30 hours a week, or roughly 120 hours a month. If a rep's hourly cost is assumed to be 500 TL, this item alone represents 60,000 TL of inefficiency per month. When you then propose a solution at 25,000 TL per month, and the customer has stated both figures in their own words, the price conversation happens on entirely different ground.

### Raise the budget topic early and naturally

"What's your budget?" usually gets an evasive answer. Instead, move forward by offering a range: "Our engagements with companies of a similar size usually fall between X and Y TL per month. Does that range work for you to keep the conversation going?" This question does two things: it filters out poor-fit opportunities early, and it removes the surprise when the proposal arrives.

### Clarify decision-makers and the approval process

A significant share of price objections stem from the fact that the person you're speaking with can't approve the budget alone. When a finance director, general manager or procurement team joins the process later, someone who has never heard the value you've described sees the price for the first time. To reduce this risk, apply the [B2B decision-maker mapping](/blog/b2b-sales-decision-maker-map) method during discovery. Knowing who owns the budget, who evaluates the technical side and who holds veto power lets you present the price to the right person in the right language.

### Confirm the value before sending the proposal

Emailing a proposal and waiting is an open invitation for a price objection. Instead, hold a short "value summary" call: "To recap what we discussed, you want to solve these three problems, and they're costing you roughly this much per month. Have I understood that correctly?" Share the price once the customer confirms. If possible, do this during the call rather than in writing.

## What should you do, step by step, when a price objection comes up?

No matter how well you prepare, price objections will come. When they do, the following seven-step framework helps you move forward in a controlled way rather than acting on reflex.

1. **Pause and don't get defensive.** The first instinct is either to justify the price or to immediately signal flexibility. Both are wrong. A brief silence and a calm acknowledgment ("I understand, this is a significant investment.") set the tone of the conversation.
2. **Clarify the objection.** Identify the type of objection with questions like "When you say expensive, what are you comparing it to?" or "Is it over budget, or does it feel high relative to the value you expect?"
3. **Isolate the objection.** Asking "If we set price aside, does the solution meet your needs?" reveals whether price is the only obstacle. Often there's another concern hiding behind the price, such as integration, team adoption or contract length.
4. **Reframe the value using the customer's numbers.** Bring back the data you gathered in discovery: "When we spoke, you said this process costs you about 60,000 TL a month. Our proposal is less than half of that."
5. **Offer options, not discounts.** Instead of lowering the price, offer alternatives that change the scope, duration or payment structure. We cover this separately below.
6. **If you give flexibility, get something in return.** Every concession should come with something in return, such as a longer contract, upfront payment, permission to use them as a reference or participation in a case study.
7. **Close with a clear next step.** "Shall we set up a 20-minute call on Thursday so you can share this option with your finance team?" An objection conversation should never end with a vague "let us think about it."

### A sample dialogue

**Customer:** "We've reviewed your proposal, and frankly, it's more expensive than we expected."

**Rep:** "Thanks for sharing that. When you say it's higher than expected, does it exceed the budget you've set aside, or does it feel too high for the scope we're offering?"

**Customer:** "We actually have the budget, but management will have questions when they see this number."

**Rep:** "I understand. So what you really need is a summary that shows your management why this investment makes sense. In our meetings, we discussed that your team spends about 120 hours a month on manual work. Let me put that, along with the expected impact in the first 6 months, into a one-page summary you can take to management. Would that work?"

In this example, the rep didn't offer a discount. They found the real reason behind the objection, namely the internal approval process, and gave the customer the tool they needed to sell the deal to management.

## How do you respond when you hear "your competitor is cheaper"?

The competitor comparison is the most challenging type of price objection, because the customer is holding a concrete alternative. Three principles work here.

**First, never badmouth the competitor.** Saying "their quality is poor" means belittling the customer's option, and it puts them on the defensive.

**Second, clarify the comparison criteria.** Compare scope by asking, "Does the proposal you're comparing include these items?" Often the cheaper offer prices setup, training, support or integration separately.

**Third, talk about total cost of ownership.** The license fee isn't the only line item. A hypothetical comparison:

- **Solution A (your proposal):** 30,000 TL per month, setup and training included, 3-week implementation time.
- **Solution B (competitor):** 22,000 TL per month, setup an additional 60,000 TL, training an additional 25,000 TL, 8-week implementation time.

Over 12 months, Solution A totals 360,000 TL and Solution B totals 349,000 TL. The gap almost disappears. On top of that, the calculation doesn't account for the team starting to see results 5 weeks later with Solution B. Filling in a table like this together with the customer turns the "expensive" perception into a "structured differently" perception.

If the competitor genuinely offers the same scope for less, be honest and state your differentiation clearly. You don't have to win every deal. A customer won on price alone is lost on price alone.

## When should you offer a discount, and when shouldn't you?

Discounts aren't forbidden. The problem is when they're given without a plan and without anything in return. Arbitrary, rep-by-rep discounts erode margins and signal to customers that your prices are open to negotiation.

### Core rules for a discount policy

- **Set an approval matrix.** For example, reps can grant discounts of up to 5%, discounts of 5–15% require sales manager approval, and anything above 15% goes to the general manager.
- **Tie every discount to something in return.** Annual upfront payment, a 24-month contract, case study permission, a reference call or an earlier signing date.
- **Avoid end-of-month discounts.** Customers quickly learn the pattern and push their decisions to the end of the month.
- **Justify the discount.** Instead of saying "this is a special price for you," saying "We offer a standard 10% advantage for annual upfront payment" protects the consistency of your pricing.

### Your commission structure shapes discounting behavior

If your reps are paid solely on revenue, they'll naturally lean toward discounting to close deals faster. Factoring profit margin or discount rate into commission calculations balances this behavior. ## What flexibility can you offer without lowering the price?

One of the strongest responses to a price objection is to change the structure, not the price. This approach addresses the customer's concern while protecting your unit value.

- **Narrowing the scope:** Instead of the full package, offer a starter package that solves the customer's most urgent problem. For example, starting with 5 users instead of 10, or moving forward with one module instead of three.
- **Pilot period:** A paid 60–90-day pilot with clear success criteria. The success criteria should be defined in writing from the start so that the transition at the end of the pilot is beyond dispute.
- **Payment terms:** Spreading payments over several months without changing the total amount often resolves a budget objection on its own, especially for cash-flow-sensitive SMBs.
- **Tiered pricing:** A lower price for the first 3 months, then the standard price afterward. This ensures the customer doesn't pay the full fee during implementation before seeing value.
- **Outcome-based component:** Tying a portion of the fee to performance is an effective way to share risk, especially in outbound and lead generation services.

The flexibility you offer depends on the type of objection. Payment structure usually works best for budget constraints, a pilot for risk aversion, and narrowing the scope for value perception problems.

## How should you negotiate with procurement?

With enterprise customers, the process often moves to procurement after alignment with the technical team and the business unit. Procurement specialists are tasked with reducing costs, and their performance is mostly measured by the savings they achieve. That's why their request for a discount on the initial offer is structural, not personal.

- **Secure the business unit's support before procurement gets involved.** If the business unit wants you, procurement only negotiates the terms. It doesn't change the decision.
- **Plan your negotiation margin in advance.** Define in writing, before the meeting, the items you can concede on and your limits.
- **Make concessions in small steps.** Rather than one large discount, small steps in decreasing increments signal that you're nearing the end of the negotiation.
- **Use non-price items.** Items such as payment terms, price-lock periods or extra training hours can be counted as "savings" by procurement while affecting your margin less.

## How do you systematize price objection handling at the team level?

Individual skill matters, but sustainable results come from systems. To manage price objections at the team level, put these building blocks in place.

### Build an objection library

Gather the 10–15 objections your team hears most often, along with recommended clarifying questions and proven responses for each, in a single document. This library should be a living document, updated every quarter. It also makes a powerful training resource for new reps. If you want to build a comprehensive structure, our article on [building an effective sales enablement program for B2B sales teams](https://cornergroup.co/blog/b2b-satis-ekipleri-i-cin-etkili-satis-enablement-programi-olusturma) is a good starting point.

### Record objections and loss reasons in your CRM

Whether you use HubSpot, Pipedrive or Zoho, make the "objection type encountered" field mandatory for every opportunity and the "loss reason" field mandatory for lost deals. Use drop-down lists instead of free text: budget, value perception, competitor price, authority, timing and so on. After a few months, you'll be able to see which objections cluster in which segments and at which stages. For example, if competitor price objections are concentrated in a particular industry, the problem may lie in your positioning rather than your sales technique.

### Discuss price risk in pipeline meetings

For every opportunity at the proposal stage, "Has a price objection come up, what type is it and what's our plan?" should be a standard agenda item. This way, discount requests are handled in a planned manner, not at the last minute. When structuring the meeting, you can use the framework in our guide on [how to run a weekly pipeline meeting](/blog/weekly-pipeline-meeting-guide).

### Run role-plays and call analysis

At least once a month, run role-play sessions using real objection scenarios. One rep plays the customer, another the seller, and the team gives feedback. AI-powered call analysis tools can be useful for measuring how reps react when price comes up, how quickly they signal a discount and how talk time is distributed. We covered this approach in detail in our article on [the impact of AI-powered sales coaching on B2B teams](https://cornergroup.co/blog/ai-destekli-satis-koclugu-b2b-ekiplerinin-performansini-nasil-yukseltir). If you record calls, make sure you inform participants and don't overlook KVKK data protection requirements.

### Automate discount approvals

With an automation tool like n8n, you can set up a simple but effective discount approval workflow. When a rep enters a discount rate on an opportunity in the CRM, the workflow kicks in. If the rate is below the set threshold, it's approved automatically. If it's above, the manager receives a notification via Slack or email with an opportunity summary, the discount rationale and the commitment received in return. Once the manager approves, the CRM field is updated and the proposal template is generated with the new price. This workflow speeds up the process and ensures every discount is recorded along with its rationale.

## Which metrics should you track?

To know whether your price objection handling is working, monitor these indicators regularly:

- **Average discount rate:** By rep, segment and product. If one rep's average is significantly above the team average, there's a coaching need.
- **Percentage of deals lost on price:** If this rate is rising, either the target audience is wrong or the value messaging is weak.
- **Post-proposal close rate:** As your discovery phase gets stronger, this rate should rise.
- **Gap between list price and realized price:** Its trend over time shows the overall health of your pricing discipline.
- **Renewal rate among discounted customers:** How customers won with heavy discounts behave at renewal shows whether the discount actually created value.

The source of price objections is often at the very top of the sales funnel. Talking to companies with the wrong profile inevitably means more budget objections. ## Frequently asked questions

### What should your first response to a price objection be?

Your first response should be neither defending the price nor offering a discount. After a calm acknowledgment, clarify the type of objection with a question like "What are you comparing it to that makes it feel high?" You can only give the right answer once you know the real reason behind the objection.

### What should you do if the customer asks directly for a discount?

Rather than refusing outright, tie the discount to something in return. For example, by saying "We can offer an advantage with annual upfront payment or a 24-month contract," you turn the negotiation from a one-sided request into a mutual agreement. A discount given for nothing will be requested again at the next renewal.

### Is it better to share the price in the proposal or in a meeting?

If possible, share the price in a meeting first, after summarizing the value. Then send the written proposal. That way you see the customer's initial reaction immediately and can address any objection on the spot. Proposals sent only by email risk ending in silence or a written rejection.

### What is the single most effective step for reducing price objections?

Having the customer calculate the cost of their problem with their own numbers during discovery. When customers say themselves how much the problem costs them each month, they evaluate your proposal as a solution rather than a cost. This step prevents a significant share of late-stage objections from the outset.

## Conclusion

A price objection is often not the moment a sale is lost, but the moment it truly begins. A customer who says "it's expensive" is still at the table, telling you what you need to solve. Successful teams see this statement not as a threat but as a source of information. They clarify the type of objection, rebuild the value with the customer's numbers and always tie flexibility to something in return.

Lasting results require turning individual skill into a system: a strong discovery process, a clear discount policy, objection data recorded in the CRM, regular coaching and approval workflows supported by automation where needed. Once this structure is in place, price conversations get shorter, margins get healthier and forecasts become more reliable.
