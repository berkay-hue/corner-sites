A field sales visit plan is **a weekly working system, tracked in your CRM, that defines in advance which customers will be visited, how often, for what purpose and along which route.** In SMEs, the plan is built by segmenting customers by value and assigning each segment a fixed visit frequency, dividing territories by route, and recording the outcome of every visit. A well-designed visit plan ensures that reps go to the customers they *should* see, not the ones that happen to come to mind.

In this article, we walk step by step through how an SME that distributes industrial consumables, with a six-person field sales team, built its visit plan from scratch. The case is a representative scenario compiled from common situations Corner Group teams have encountered across different SME projects. The company name is not disclosed. The figures in the text are sample values used to illustrate how the method is calculated.

## What was the company's starting point?

The company sells cutting tools, abrasives, occupational safety equipment and maintenance consumables to industrial businesses in the Marmara and Central Anatolia regions of Turkey. Most of its customers are manufacturing plants in organized industrial zones (OIZs). The sales model is largely relationship-driven: the purchasing manager or maintenance supervisor places orders with a rep they trust.

At the outset, the picture looked like this:

- **6 field reps**, each responsible for their own city
- Roughly **1,100 customers and prospects** in the system
- A setup where orders were entered into the ERP (Logo), but visits were not recorded anywhere
- Reps keeping their visit plans in their heads or in personal Excel files
- The sales manager following up in the weekly meeting by asking, "Who did you see this week?"

The company had a clear growth target, but nobody really knew what was happening in the field. Revenue was growing, but it was unclear which customers and which reps' efforts were driving that growth.

## What exactly was the problem?

In the first two weeks of analysis, we identified three core symptoms together with the sales manager.

**1. Visits were concentrated in the comfort zone.** Reps frequently visited customers they had good relationships with and felt comfortable around, while putting off high-potential but hard-to-reach companies. Some small customers were seen three times a month, while certain plants with far higher annual purchasing potential had gone unvisited for months.

**2. Lost customers were noticed too late.** When a customer stopped ordering, it only showed up in the end-of-quarter revenue report. By then, the customer had already switched to a competitor.

**3. Routes were inefficient.** There were days when a rep drove from one end of an OIZ to the other, then to the city center, and back to the OIZ again. Unplanned routes were cutting the number of visits possible per day.

These symptoms closely resembled the [SME case where the sales team wasn't using the CRM](/blog/sales-team-not-using-crm-sme-case-study) that we covered earlier: the problem wasn't the tool, it was the lack of a system.

## Why was the visit plan falling apart?

The root causes behind the symptoms were:

- **Customer value wasn't defined.** Which customers were "important" was left to each rep's intuition.
- **There was no visit frequency rule.** No customer had a standard such as "must be seen at least once a month."
- **The purpose of visits was unclear.** Most visits were courtesy calls; there were no clear objectives such as taking an order, introducing a new product or following up on a sample.
- **There were no records.** If a visit didn't happen or produced no result, nobody knew.
- **The incentive system didn't reward visits.** Bonuses were tied solely to revenue; there were no incentives for winning new customers or recovering at-risk ones.

The solution was built in five steps corresponding to these five points.

## Which customers should be visited, and how often?

Segmentation is the foundation of a visit plan. The company's last 12 months of sales data were pulled from Logo, and customers were assessed on two axes:

- **Current revenue:** The amount the customer generated for the company over the last 12 months
- **Potential:** Estimated annual purchasing capacity based on plant size, headcount, production line type and consumables usage

These two axes produced four segments:

| Segment | Definition | Visit frequency | Visit type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A – Strategic** | High revenue, high potential | Once every 2 weeks | In person, with a planned agenda |
| **B – Develop** | Low revenue, high potential | Once every 3 weeks | In person, focused on expanding the product range |
| **C – Retain** | Medium revenue, limited potential | Once a month | Alternating in person and by phone |
| **D – Manage remotely** | Low revenue, low potential | Once every 6–8 weeks | Phone, email, WhatsApp |

The critical decision in this table was that **Segment B would be visited more often than Segment C.** Growth doesn't come from customers who already buy a lot; it comes from customers buying below their potential. This is also where reps pushed back the most: "Why should we visit a company that orders so little this often?" The answer was simple: that company is buying from a competitor, and the visits are about winning that share.

### Capacity check: Is the plan realistic?

Segmentation may look great on paper, but it collapses if it exceeds what a rep can actually carry. So we ran a simple capacity calculation. For a sample rep:

- An average of **20 working days** per month
- **2 days** set aside for the office, meetings and admin → **18 days** in the field
- With optimized routes, **5 in-person visits** per day
- Monthly capacity: 18 × 5 = **90 in-person visits**

Assume this rep's portfolio contains 12 A, 20 B, 35 C and 110 D customers:

- A: 12 customers × 2 per month = 24 visits
- B: 20 customers × approx. 1.4 per month = 28 visits
- C: 35 customers × 1 per month (half in person) ≈ 18 visits
- **Total in-person load: approximately 70 visits**

The remaining capacity of about 20 visits was allocated to **new customer acquisition**. Segment D was moved to remote management so it wouldn't eat into in-person capacity. For some reps, the numbers didn't add up; their portfolios were too large. Some of these reps' Segment D customers were handed over to the inside sales rep at the office.

## How were territories and routes designed?

The second step was to divide each rep's days into geographic clusters. Each rep's territory was split into **4–5 sub-territories** based on organized industrial zones and industrial estates. The rule was: **One day, one sub-territory.**

For example, one rep's week looked like this:

- **Monday:** Office, weekly plan approval, phone follow-up with Segment D, afternoon visits to customers near the city center
- **Tuesday:** OIZ 1 (north cluster)
- **Wednesday:** OIZ 1 (south cluster)
- **Thursday:** OIZ 2 and the nearby industrial estate
- **Friday:** Neighboring district or new customer day

This structure delivered two benefits. First, time spent on the road dropped noticeably and the number of daily visits increased. Second, it created predictability on the customer side. Knowing the rep came "on Tuesdays," purchasing managers began saving up their needs for that day.

Route planning didn't require complex software. A map view of the address data in the CRM, combined with the rep's local knowledge, was sufficient in most cases. What mattered was that the route was set **in advance** and not changed on a whim in the morning.

## How did the weekly visit plan work in practice?

The heart of the system became the **weekly planning ritual**. Every Friday afternoon, each rep built the following week's plan in the CRM. The plan drew on three sources:

1. **Customers overdue for a visit:** Those whose visit date had passed based on their segment were listed automatically.
2. **Open opportunities:** Deals at the sample, quote or trial stage.
3. **New customer targets:** At least 3–4 prospects set for that week.

On Monday morning, the sales manager held a **15-minute** call with each rep. Only three questions were asked:

- Which A and B customers will be seen this week?
- Are there any overdue visits, and why?
- Which visits have a clear sales objective?

Keeping the weekly planning meeting short was a deliberate choice. Long meetings were eating into time that should have been spent in the field.

### Every visit should have a purpose

Increasing the number of visits wasn't enough on its own. "Just dropped by for a cup of tea" visits wasted both the rep's and the customer's time. So when logging a visit in the CRM, **visit purpose** was made a mandatory field:

- Order taking / repeat order
- New product line introduction
- Sample or trial follow-up
- Complaint or issue resolution
- Pricing / annual agreement discussion
- First meeting (new customer)

After the visit, the rep filled in **three fields** from their phone: outcome (positive / neutral / negative), next step, and next step date. The form was deliberately kept short. Trying to make field teams fill in long forms is the most common reason a system gets abandoned within the first month.

## How were the CRM and field tools set up?

The company evaluated Pipedrive, HubSpot and Zoho CRM. The deciding criteria were:

- **A mobile app that's easy to use in the field**
- The ability to define segment and visit frequency through custom fields on customer records
- Visibility of activity (visit) records on a map and calendar
- Integration with Logo
- License costs suited to the team size

All three tools could meet these needs to varying degrees. There's no single right tool in a decision like this; the team's habits and budget are what decide it. Because the company prioritized the mobile experience reps would adopt most easily, it opted for a solution with a simpler interface.

Key configurations made during setup:

- **Segment (A/B/C/D)**, **owner rep**, **sub-territory** and **last visit date** fields were added to every customer record.
- A separate activity type called "Visit" was defined.
- An **"overdue customers"** filter was created based on segment and last visit date.
- Revenue data for the last 12 months from Logo was connected to sync to the customer record monthly.

ERP integration was a critical step because it allowed the visit plan to talk to order data. We covered the technical details in our [case study on CRM integration with Logo and Mikro](/blog/logo-mikro-crm-integration-sme-case-study).

## What was automated with n8n and AI?

To reduce the time reps spent on manual follow-up, several simple automations were built in n8n. None of them were complex, but together they made a big difference.

**1. Automatic plan suggestion on Fridays:** Every Friday morning, n8n pulled each rep's overdue customers and customers due for a visit the following week from the CRM, grouped them by sub-territory, and emailed the list to the rep. Instead of writing the plan from scratch, the rep edited this suggestion.

**2. Order decline alert:** Order data from Logo was monitored. When an A or B customer's orders over the last two months fell significantly below their own average, a notification went to the rep and the sales manager. This meant customers on the verge of being lost were spotted without waiting for quarter-end.

**3. Voice notes turned into visit reports:** Some reps preferred leaving voice notes to typing in the car. An n8n workflow transcribed the voice note, summarized it with an AI model, parsed the result into the next step and date fields, and added it to the visit record in the CRM. The rep only had to approve the summary.

**4. Weekly manager summary:** Every Monday morning, the sales manager received a short report showing each rep's number of visits last week, segment distribution, overdue A/B visits and newly opened opportunities.

There was an important principle here: **AI didn't make decisions; it reduced the workload.** The rep and the sales manager still decided which customers to visit; the automation simply reminded, summarized and alerted.

## What did the 12-week implementation timeline look like?

The system wasn't rolled out all at once. A phased transition was the single most important factor in reducing team resistance.

1. **Weeks 1–2 – Data and segmentation:** Logo data was extracted, the customer list was cleaned, duplicate records were merged, and A/B/C/D segments were defined together with the reps. Involving reps in this process gave them ownership of the segments.
2. **Weeks 3–4 – CRM setup and training:** Fields, filters and the mobile app were prepared. Training wasn't delivered in a classroom but in the field, alongside a rep during real visits.
3. **Weeks 5–6 – Pilot:** Two volunteer reps used the system in full. Form fields were simplified based on their feedback.
4. **Weeks 7–8 – Rollout to the whole team:** The weekly planning ritual began for the entire team.
5. **Weeks 9–10 – Automations:** The n8n workflows went live. Adding automation only after the system had become a habit was a deliberate choice.
6. **Weeks 11–12 – First review and incentive adjustment:** Metrics were reviewed, segments were reassessed, and components supporting visit discipline were added to the bonus scheme.

## Which metrics were used to measure success?

In field sales, looking only at revenue is misleading, because revenue is a lagging indicator. So leading and outcome metrics were tracked together.

**Leading metrics (weekly):**

- **Plan adherence rate:** How many of the planned visits actually took place
- **Segment coverage rate:** How many A and B customers were visited at the target frequency
- **Number of overdue visits:** Especially in Segments A and B
- **Number of new customer visits**
- **Purposeful visit rate:** Share of visits made with a clear sales objective

**Outcome metrics (monthly / quarterly):**

- Change in revenue from Segment B
- Number of product lines sold per customer
- Number of lost customers
- Number of new customers won and time to first order

By the end of the first quarter, the most noticeable changes the company observed were: overdue visits to A and B customers had largely disappeared, several at-risk customers were visited in time thanks to order decline alerts, and dedicated new-customer time had become a regular part of reps' weeks. As expected, the impact on revenue took longer to become clear; visit discipline generally takes a few months to translate into sales.

How to set targets under this new structure is a separate topic. When setting rep-level targets, the company followed the approach in our [step-by-step guide to setting sales quotas for SMEs](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quotas-smes) and allocated quotas based on portfolio potential.

## How much does it cost?

The cost of building a visit plan for a field sales team largely comes down to three items:

- **CRM license:** Billed monthly per user. Pipedrive, HubSpot and Zoho offer different plans; for field teams, mid-tier plans that include a mobile app and activity management are usually sufficient.
- **Setup and integration:** Consulting or internal time spent on data cleansing, field design and the ERP connection. ERP integration is the most variable part of this item.
- **Automation:** n8n can be self-hosted on your own server or used via its cloud version. Operations such as AI-powered summarization incur usage-based API costs; at SME scale, this amount usually remains limited.

The real question is less about cost and more about **return**. Let's run a simple calculation: if each of the six reps makes just 3 additional productive visits per week thanks to route and planning discipline, the team gains roughly 70 additional visits per month. If even a small share of these visits turns into a new product line sale or retains a customer on the verge of being lost, that covers the system's annual cost in most cases. We recommend running this calculation with your own numbers: additional visits per rep × conversion rate × average order value.

## What are the most common mistakes?

These are the mistakes we encounter most often in projects like this:

- **Segmenting by revenue alone.** This leaves customers with growth potential invisible.
- **Visit forms packed with long, mandatory fields.** Field teams fill in the form in the first week and abandon it by the third.
- **Positioning the system as a monitoring tool.** The message "we're going to track you" creates resistance. The message "less wasted driving, more orders" gets adopted.
- **Setting up automation first.** Automations built before core habits are formed end up running into a void.
- **Not updating the incentive system.** If bonuses are tied only to revenue, reps have no motivation to spend time on Segment B and new customers. Our [guide to building a B2B sales commission plan](/blog/b2b-sales-commission-plan-guide) is a good starting point here.

Also, if your company sells through distributors rather than directly to end users, the logic of the visit plan changes. In that case, the person the rep visits is the distributor, and the metrics tracked differ as well.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many visits should a field sales rep make per day?

There's no single right number; it depends on the industry, the geographic density of customers and the nature of the visit. In dense areas such as organized industrial zones, 4–6 quality in-person visits per day is a realistic range for a rep with a planned route. What matters more than the number is that every visit has a clear purpose.

### How is visit frequency determined?

Customers are segmented by current revenue and growth potential, and each segment is assigned a fixed visit interval. High-potential customers who currently buy little should generally be visited more often than the middle segment, because they represent a growth opportunity. Then you check that the total visit load doesn't exceed the rep's monthly capacity.

### How do you get a field team to use the CRM?

The most effective approach is to make the CRM a tool that makes the rep's job easier: short mobile forms, automatically generated weekly plan suggestions and the ability to log reports via voice notes all drive this shift. Involving volunteer reps in the pilot and simplifying the system based on their feedback significantly reduces resistance. An incentive system that supports visit discipline is also essential for lasting adoption.

### Do you need dedicated field sales software for visit planning?

At SME scale, usually not. The mobile apps, custom fields and activity management in tools like Pipedrive, HubSpot or Zoho CRM, combined with simple automations built in n8n, cover most of what a visit plan requires. Dedicated solutions may be worth considering for very large teams or situations requiring complex route optimization.

## Conclusion

A field sales visit plan is a discipline that directs reps' time toward the customers who generate the most value. What made the difference in this case was less a new piece of software than the combined application of five complementary steps: customer segmentation, segment-based visit frequency, territory and route discipline, short and purposeful visit logging, and an incentive system that supports all of this. n8n and AI automations were layered on top of this structure, reducing reps' administrative workload and ensuring at-risk customers were spotted in time.
