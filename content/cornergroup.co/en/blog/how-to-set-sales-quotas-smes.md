A sales quota is set by **reconciling a figure calculated backwards from the company's revenue target (top-down) with a figure calculated upwards from each rep's actual selling capacity (bottom-up)**. For SMEs, the soundest method is this: pull the average deal size, win rate and sales cycle from the past 6-12 months of data; distribute the company target across reps with a 10-20% buffer; and apply a ramp period for new hires and a seasonality factor for each month. The quota should sit at a level that most of the team can reach, but only by stretching.

In many SMEs, quotas are still set with the logic of "let's add 30% to last year's number." This approach either produces unreachable targets that drain the team's motivation in the very first quarter, or creates a comfortable but unproductive sales organization operating well below its potential. In this article, we share a practical, numbers-backed, step-by-step quota-setting method for sales teams of 3-15 people.

## What is a sales quota, and why should SMEs approach it differently?

A sales quota is a measurable target that a rep or team is expected to reach within a given period (month, quarter, year). The most common form is a revenue quota, but there are other types as well, such as gross profit, number of new customers or number of meetings.

In large companies, quota setting is usually a months-long joint effort between finance, sales operations and regional managers. In SMEs, the situation is different:

- **The data set is small.** In a company that closes 40-50 deals a year, a single large customer can skew all the averages.
- **The team is small.** In a team of 4, one rep leaving instantly wipes out 25% of capacity.
- **The founder or general manager may still be selling.** If it isn't clear how these sales count toward quotas, fairness issues arise within the team.
- **Marketing and lead flow are irregular.** The quota may depend too heavily on lead volume, which is outside the rep's control.

That's why SME quota models need to be simple, transparent and reviewable on a quarterly basis.

## What data do you need before setting a quota?

Before you start calculating, pull the following data from your CRM or sales records. Use the last 12 months if possible, or at least the last 6 months.

1. **Average deal size:** Total value of won deals / number of won deals.
2. **Win rate:** Won opportunities / closed (won + lost) qualified opportunities.
3. **Sales cycle length:** The average number of days from the date an opportunity is opened to the date it is closed.
4. **Monthly opportunity capacity per rep:** The number of active opportunities a rep can handle effectively at the same time.
5. **Lead and opportunity sources:** How much of your pipeline comes from inbound, how much from outbound, and how much from existing customers?
6. **Seasonality:** How revenue is distributed across months or quarters.
7. **Losses and churn:** If you have a recurring revenue model, the size of the revenue lost from churned customers.

If this data is scattered, or if the CRM isn't updated consistently, you need to solve that problem first. We explained in detail why your team might not be using the CRM and how to overcome it in our [SME case study on a sales team that doesn't use the CRM](/blog/sales-team-not-using-crm-sme-case-study). A quota set without reliable data is little more than a guess.

## A step-by-step method for setting sales quotas

We'll walk through the steps below using the scenario of a B2B SME with an annual new-sales target. The figures are examples; you can apply the same logic by substituting your own data.

### Step 1: Work backwards from the company target (top-down)

The first step is to clarify the revenue target set by the board or the founders. The critical distinction here is that **the total revenue target and the new sales target are not the same thing.**

Example scenario:

- The company's total revenue target for next year: 20,000,000 TL
- Expected revenue from renewals and repeat orders from existing customers: 8,000,000 TL
- **Revenue the sales team needs to bring in from new sales: 12,000,000 TL**

By dividing this 12 million TL by the average deal size, you find out how many deals you need to close:

- Average deal size: 150,000 TL
- Required number of deals: 12,000,000 / 150,000 = **80 deals**
- With a 25% win rate, the required number of qualified opportunities: 80 / 0.25 = **320 opportunities**

This last figure is very important, because if there isn't enough opportunity flow behind the quota, it can't be reached, no matter how reasonable it looks.

### Step 2: Calculate from the ground up with a capacity model (bottom-up)

Now look at it the other way around: how much can your team actually sell?

In our example scenario, let's say the team looks like this:

- 3 experienced reps (with the company for at least 1 year)
- 1 new rep (starting in January)

Looking at an experienced rep's past performance, they close an average of 2 deals per month. 24 deals per year × 150,000 TL = **3,600,000 TL at full capacity**.

A ramp period is applied for the new rep (we cover this in detail in the next step). The calculation puts the new rep's annual capacity at approximately **2,850,000 TL**.

Total team capacity:

- 3 × 3,600,000 TL = 10,800,000 TL
- 1 × 2,850,000 TL = 2,850,000 TL
- **Total: 13,650,000 TL**

### Step 3: Factor in the ramp period

Expecting a full quota from a new rep from their first month is one of the most common, and most costly, mistakes. If your sales cycle is 60-90 days, it will naturally take a new rep 2-3 months to close their first deal.

An example ramp plan:

| Month | Quota percentage |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | 0% |
| Month 2 | 25% |
| Month 3 | 50% |
| Month 4 | 75% |
| Month 5 onwards | 100% |

Calculation: 0 + 0.25 + 0.50 + 0.75 + 8 full months = 9.5 "effective months." Since full annual capacity is 3,600,000 TL: 3,600,000 × (9.5 / 12) = **2,850,000 TL**.

The ramp period should be proportional to your sales cycle. If your cycle is 30 days, a 2-3 month ramp may be enough; for 6-month enterprise sales, the ramp can stretch to 6-9 months. You can find a step-by-step breakdown of what a new rep should be doing during this period in [our plan for a new sales rep's first 90 days](/blog/new-sales-rep-first-90-days-smb-plan).

### Step 4: Reconcile the two figures and add an over-assignment buffer

We now have two figures:

- What the company needs (top-down): 12,000,000 TL
- What the team can deliver (bottom-up): 13,650,000 TL

Capacity is higher than need, which is a good sign. But how much quota should you distribute to reps in total?

This is where the concept of **over-assignment** comes in. It isn't realistic to expect every rep to hit their quota exactly; some will exceed it, some will fall short, and some will leave during the year. That's why the total quota distributed to reps is usually set 10-20% higher than the company target.

- 12,000,000 × 1.15 = 13,800,000 TL in total quota to distribute

This figure is very close to team capacity (13,650,000 TL). So the distribution could look like this:

- Experienced reps A, B, C: 3,600,000 TL each
- New rep D: 2,850,000 TL
- **Total: 13,650,000 TL** (approximately 14% above the company target)

If the top-down figure had come out significantly higher than capacity — for example, if the company wanted 18 million TL in new sales — you would have three options: hire new reps, build a plan to increase average deal size or win rate, or revise the target. Handing out a quota that exceeds capacity on the assumption that "it'll motivate them anyway" doesn't solve the problem; it just postpones it.

### Step 5: Split the quota into periods based on seasonality

Dividing the annual quota by 12 and assigning monthly targets looks practical, but it's unrealistic in most industries. Budget cycles, holidays and year-end purchasing all change the rhythm of sales.

Let's say your historical data shows revenue distributed across quarters like this:

- Q1: 20%
- Q2: 25%
- Q3: 20%
- Q4: 35%

For a rep with an annual quota of 3,600,000 TL, the quarterly quotas would be:

- Q1: 720,000 TL
- Q2: 900,000 TL
- Q3: 720,000 TL
- Q4: 1,260,000 TL

For SMEs, a **quarterly quota** is generally healthier than a monthly one. A single large deal slipping by a month can make a rep look like they've "failed" on a monthly basis, while on a quarterly basis that fluctuation evens out. Monthly tracking should still happen, but rewards and evaluations can be done quarterly.

### Step 6: Test how realistic the quota is with pipeline coverage

Once you've set the quota, the question to ask is: **Are there enough opportunities to support this quota?**

Pipeline coverage is the ratio of the qualified pipeline value at the start of a period to the quota for that period. If your win rate is 25%, you theoretically need pipeline worth 4 times your quota.

Example: For a rep with a Q1 quota of 720,000 TL, there should be at least 2,880,000 TL in qualified pipeline at the start of the quarter.

For reps who fall below this ratio, one of two things happens: either prospecting activity is increased, or the realism of the quota is discussed again. The most effective way to review the pipeline regularly is to establish a weekly rhythm; we explained how to do this in our [guide to running a weekly pipeline meeting](/blog/weekly-pipeline-meeting-guide).

If a lack of pipeline is a structural problem — in other words, if reps can't keep up with both closing deals and generating opportunities from scratch — you may need to treat outbound opportunity generation as a separate function. ### Step 7: Define activity targets for leading indicators

A revenue quota is a **lagging indicator**; you only find out the result at the end of the period. To see early on whether a rep is on track, you need **leading indicators**.

Let's work backwards in our example scenario:

- Annual target per rep: 24 deals
- 25% win rate → 96 qualified opportunities / year → roughly 8 opportunities per month
- If 50% of discovery meetings convert into qualified opportunities → 16 discovery meetings per month
- Roughly **4 discovery meetings per week**

This figure is far more concrete and controllable for a rep than a revenue quota. However, avoid making activity targets the main component of compensation; otherwise, reps will start accepting low-quality meetings just to hit the numbers. Activity targets are a **coaching tool**, while the revenue quota is a **performance tool**.

### Step 8: Tie the quota to the commission plan

A quota on its own is meaningless; what really matters to a rep is what they'll earn when they hit it and when they exceed it. The quota and the commission plan should be designed together.

Points to watch:

- **Threshold:** Does commission kick in at 50% or 70% of quota?
- **Accelerators:** Does the commission rate increase once 100% of quota is exceeded?
- **Cap:** Is there an upper limit on earnings? (For SMEs, it's generally recommended not to set a cap; otherwise, your best reps will push sales into the next period during the last month of the year.)
- **Ramp-period guarantee:** Do new reps receive a guaranteed commission in their first months?

## Which type of quota should you choose?

There's no single right quota type for every SME. Choose based on your business model:

- **Revenue quota:** The most common. Suitable if product or service prices are relatively fixed and margins are similar.
- **Gross profit quota:** Healthier in industries with high discount flexibility, where margins vary from deal to deal (distribution, manufacturing, project-based work). It prevents reps from adopting a "close at any cost" mindset.
- **New customer quota:** If gaining market share is the priority, a target of "at least X new logos per year" can be added alongside the revenue quota.
- **Recurring revenue quota (ARR/MRR):** In SaaS and subscription models, annual recurring revenue is used as the basis instead of one-off setup fees.
- **Blended quota:** For example, 70% revenue + 30% number of new customers. Flexible but complex; take care not to use more than 2 components.

The general recommendation for SMEs: **one primary quota metric and at most one secondary metric.** Reps should have a clear target in mind.

## How can you tell whether a quota is fair?

Once the quota is set, ask yourself the following questions regularly throughout the year:

1. **How much of the team is hitting quota?** If nobody hits it, the quota is probably too high. If everyone comfortably exceeds it, it's too low. In a healthy picture, most of the team lands close to quota, with a few above and a few below.
2. **Is there a correlation between success and territory or portfolio?** If the same rep consistently exceeds quota and their portfolio happens to be the most productive territory, the problem isn't the rep — it's the distribution.
3. **Do reps understand their quota?** When you ask a rep, "Why is your quota this number?", they should be able to explain the logic. If they can't, the quota is being perceived as a black box and won't be owned.
4. **Is pipeline coverage sufficient?** If coverage is consistently low at the start of each period, the quota problem is actually an opportunity-generation problem.

These questions need to be answered with data, not intuition. ## How should territory or portfolio differences be reflected in quotas?

Giving every rep the same quota may seem fair, but it often isn't. A rep managing an enterprise portfolio in Istanbul and a rep looking after SME customers in Anatolia don't have the same potential.

A simple method for reflecting portfolio differences:

1. Pull each portfolio's realized revenue and number of active accounts over the last 12 months.
2. Make a rough estimate of each portfolio's total market potential (number of companies in the target sector × average purchasing potential).
3. Distribute the team's total quota according to the potential weighting of each portfolio.

For example, if the experienced reps' total quota of 10,800,000 TL is split 40% / 35% / 25% based on potential, you get 4,320,000 TL, 3,780,000 TL and 2,700,000 TL. This creates a far fairer picture than a "3.6 million for everyone" approach.

## Should you change the quota mid-year?

Short answer: **no, as far as possible — but yes when necessary, under clearly defined rules.**

Constantly changing quotas during the year erodes the team's trust. Raising the target of a rep who has exceeded their quota in particular creates the perception that "good performance gets punished" and can cost you your best reps.

However, a revision is legitimate in the following situations:

- There has been a major change in the market (serious economic turbulence, a fundamental change in the price of a core product).
- A major change was made to the rep's portfolio (key accounts were transferred to someone else).
- Someone left the team and their portfolio was redistributed.
- A clear calculation error was made when the quota was set.

Define your revision rules in writing at the start of the year. For example: "Quotas are reviewed at the end of each quarter; they are revised only in the event of a portfolio change or a company-wide price change." This turns revisions into a predictable process rather than an arbitrary one.

Making sure the portfolio and customer knowledge aren't lost when a rep leaves is also critical for quota continuity; if the departing rep's pipeline isn't fully handed over to the new rep, the quota for that portfolio effectively becomes meaningless.

## The most common quota-setting mistakes SMEs make

These are the mistakes we encounter most often in the field:

- **Working top-down only:** Failing to compare a target set because "the company needs to grow" against team capacity.
- **Adding a percentage to last year:** If last year's conditions (team structure, product, pricing) have changed, this method is meaningless.
- **Skipping the ramp period:** Giving a new rep a full quota from their first month.
- **Ignoring seasonality:** Splitting the annual quota into 12 equal parts.
- **Leaving founder sales undefined:** Not specifying which rep's quota the large deals closed by the founder will count toward.
- **Not testing the quota against the pipeline:** Setting a quota without the opportunity flow to back it up.
- **Using too many metrics:** Revenue, margin, new customers, activity, collections… leaving reps unsure what to focus on.
- **Communicating the quota one-way:** "Announcing" the quota by email instead of discussing it one-on-one with the rep and sharing the logic behind it.

## How can CRM and automation make quota tracking easier?

Setting the quota is half the job; the other half is keeping it visible throughout the year. Quotas tracked in Excel files usually become outdated by the second quarter.

A practical setup could look like this:

- **Targets defined in the CRM:** In tools such as HubSpot, Pipedrive and Zoho CRM, revenue targets can be defined per rep and attainment can be monitored in real time.
- **Dashboard:** Quota attainment, pipeline coverage and weekly activity counts for each rep should be visible on a single screen.
- **Automated alerts:** With an automation tool like n8n, you can send automatic Slack or email notifications to the rep and their manager when pipeline coverage drops below a certain threshold (e.g., 3x).
- **Weekly summary:** Every Monday morning, you can automatically generate and share a per-rep summary of "quarterly quota, attained to date, remaining, weighted pipeline" using data pulled from the CRM.
- **AI-assisted forecasting:** AI tools can be used to generate end-of-period forecasts based on historical win rates and opportunity stages. However, these forecasts should support — not replace — the manager's deal-by-deal assessment.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a sales quota and a sales target?

A sales target is usually a general growth goal set at the company or team level. A sales quota is that target broken down to a specific rep, a specific period and a measurable metric. Quotas are usually directly linked to commission and performance evaluation.

### Should quotas be set annually, quarterly or monthly?

Quotas should be planned annually, but tracked and evaluated on a quarterly basis. Monthly quotas make sense for high-volume businesses with sales cycles shorter than 30 days. For B2B SMEs with long sales cycles, monthly quotas produce misleading results whenever a single deal slips.

### I have a new company with no historical data; how do I set quotas?

If you have no historical data, start the first 3-6 months with activity and opportunity targets instead of a revenue quota — for example, weekly discovery meetings and the number of qualified opportunities per month. Use this period to build up data on average deal size, win rate and sales cycle. Then set your first real revenue quota using the top-down and bottom-up calculations in this article.

### What should I do if most reps aren't hitting quota?

First, determine whether the problem lies in the quota, the pipeline or execution. If pipeline coverage is low, the problem is opportunity generation; if coverage is sufficient but the win rate is low, the problem is sales skills or process; if both are normal, the quota has probably been set above capacity. Make your revision decision after this diagnosis.

## Conclusion

A good sales quota is a bridge between what the company needs and what the team can actually deliver. Calculating backwards from the company target, calculating upwards from rep capacity, reconciling the two figures with a 10-20% over-assignment buffer, factoring in ramp and seasonality, and finally testing the quota against pipeline coverage gives SMEs a framework that is both practical and fair.

Remember: a quota isn't a number you set once and forget. It's a management tool that is monitored with data throughout the year, discussed transparently with the team and kept consistent with the commission plan.
