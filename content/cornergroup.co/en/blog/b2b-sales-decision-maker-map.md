In B2B sales, a decision-maker map makes visible everyone who influences the purchasing decision at a target company, based on their **role, influence, attitude and relationships with one another**. To build the map, you first define the roles on the buying committee (budget owner, technical evaluator, user, champion, veto holder). Then you assign real people to each role, score their levels of influence and support, and create an access plan for the missing links. A well-built map is the best antidote to surprises of the "everything looked fine, but we lost it at the last minute" kind.

Many sales teams still work a deal through a single person, often a mid-level manager who accepted the first meeting. However keen that person may be, if the finance director doesn't approve the budget, IT gets stuck on the security review, or procurement asks for three alternative quotes, the opportunity can sit in limbo for months. In this article, we walk through the roles that make up a decision-maker map, how to build one step by step, how to keep it alive in your CRM, and where AI and automation help, using a concrete scenario.

## What is a decision-maker map, and how is it different from an org chart?

At first glance, a decision-maker map looks like an org chart, but the two are not the same thing. An org chart shows the **formal hierarchy**: who reports to whom and where each department sits. A decision-maker map shows **actual influence**: whose opinion carries weight, who can quietly veto, and who sees the project as important for their own career.

Consider an example. On the org chart, a sales operations specialist appears four levels below the CEO. But if the CEO trusts this specialist's judgement on matters such as choosing a CRM, that person belongs in the "high influence" box on the map. The reverse is also true: a director with an impressive title may have no interest at all in the purchase in question.

A decision-maker map answers four questions:

- **Who?** Who are the people influencing the decision?
- **How much?** How much influence does each of them have on the decision?
- **In which direction?** Is their attitude towards us supportive, neutral or opposed?
- **What do they want?** What are each person's personal and organisational priorities?

## What roles are on a buying committee?

Roles should not be confused with job titles. The same person can hold more than one role, and in small companies the CEO may carry almost every role alone. In larger enterprises, on the other hand, each role may be held by a separate person, or even a separate team.

### Economic buyer (budget owner)

This is the person with the authority to spend the money. It is usually the CEO, the finance director or the vice president who manages the relevant department's budget. The economic buyer is interested not in product features but in **business outcomes**: lower costs, higher revenue, reduced risk.

### Technical evaluator

This person checks whether the solution is compatible with the existing infrastructure and whether it meets security and integration requirements. The IT manager, data protection officer or systems architect typically fills this role. They usually don't have the authority to say "yes", but they have considerable power to say "no".

### End user

These are the people who will use the product every day: sales reps, the operations team and customer service staff. User resistance directly affects post-purchase adoption, and therefore renewal.

### Champion

This is the person who sells on your behalf inside the company. They benefit personally from the project's success and advocate for you when you're not in the room. Every champion is an "internal contact", but not every internal contact is a champion. A true champion has two qualities: **influence** and **a willingness to advocate for you.**

### Veto holder and blocker

These are the people who can stop the decision. The legal department, procurement, or an executive with a strong relationship with the incumbent supplier may fill this role. Blockers don't always show open opposition; sometimes they exert their influence simply by skipping meetings or dragging out the process.

### Influencer

These are people whose opinion matters even though they have no formal role. External consultants, board members, or a former colleague trusted by the executive making the decision can fall into this category.

## How do you build a decision-maker map step by step?

The following seven steps can be adapted by teams selling to a 20-person SME as well as to a company with several hundred employees.

### 1. Clarify the scope of the opportunity

The map is built **per opportunity**, not per company. If you're selling both CRM licences and consulting services to the same company, you may encounter two separate groups of decision-makers. In the first step, answer these questions:

- What problem are we solving?
- Which departments are affected by this problem?
- What is the estimated deal size? (The larger the amount, the more approval layers there are.)
- Is the purchase new spend, or does it replace an existing solution?

When an existing solution is being replaced, the person who chose the old system should automatically go on your map as a "potential blocker".

### 2. Draft the first version with desk research

Before the first meeting, try to understand the company's structure as much as possible. The LinkedIn company page, employee profiles, the team page on the company website, press releases and job postings all offer valuable clues. For example, if the company recently posted a "Sales Operations Manager" vacancy, you can infer that it is restructuring its sales processes.

To gather relevant contacts and their contact details at the target account, you can use B2B data platforms such as [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya). These tools make it easy to filter by department, seniority and job title. Even so, always verify the accuracy of the data and review the legal framework before reaching out. Our article on [KVKK-compliant cold outbound and the legal boundaries in Turkey](/blog/kvkk-compliant-cold-outbound-turkey) is a good starting point on this topic.

At this stage, the goal is not certainty but a **testable hypothesis**. Add a "not verified" note next to each person in your draft.

### 3. Turn your first contact into a discovery source

The person you first meet with is your best source for learning about the rest of the map. However, asking "Who makes the decision?" directly often yields a superficial answer or puts the other person on the defensive. Ask process-oriented questions instead:

- "When you've bought software of a similar size in the past, how did the process work?"
- "If this project is approved, whose schedule and budget will change?"
- "On the technical side, whose opinion can you not move forward without?"
- "Who do you think would object to this project the most, and why?"
- "What information needs to be on the table for the final decision?"

The last question is especially important; it reveals which metrics the economic buyer looks at.

### 4. Score each person on the influence and support axes

A simple scoring system is the most practical way to visualise the map. Assign two values to each person:

- **Influence (1–5):** Their actual weight in the decision. A 5 means the power to approve or stop it single-handedly.
- **Support (-2 to +2):** -2 is openly opposed, 0 is neutral or unknown, +2 is an active advocate.

When you plot these two axes on a matrix, four zones emerge:

1. **High influence + high support:** Your champions. Strengthen them and prepare the internal presentation materials they need.
2. **High influence + low support:** The most critical risk zone. Devote most of your time here.
3. **Low influence + high support:** Valuable sources of information and coaches. Use them to learn about internal dynamics.
4. **Low influence + low support:** Monitor them, but don't spend resources on them.

### 5. Note personal gains and concerns

People make decisions based on their own priorities, not the company's. Add two short notes for each person: **What do they personally gain from this project?** and **What are they afraid of losing?**

For example, the IT manager's gain might be "consolidating scattered tools into a single system", while their fear might be "being held responsible for data loss during the migration". A sales rep who knows about this fear can turn that person from neutral to supportive by presenting a data migration plan instead of a demo.

### 6. Create an access plan for the missing links

Once your map is complete, you'll most likely see high-influence people you've never spoken to. Define an access strategy for each of them:

- **Introduction through the champion:** This is the healthiest route. Prepare a short, role-specific summary your champion can pass on to that person.
- **Direct contact with role-specific content:** Send the finance director a payback period calculation, not product features.
- **Peer-to-peer matching:** Bring in an executive of the same level from your own company. A 20-minute conversation between your CEO and their CEO can be more effective than weeks of email back-and-forth.
- **Social engagement:** Engaging meaningfully with the content a decision-maker shares on LinkedIn warms up the ground before a cold message. ### 7. Keep the map alive

A decision-maker map is not a document you create once and forget. People get promoted, leave the company and change priorities. Update the map after every important meeting and bring it up in weekly opportunity reviews. ## A concrete scenario: Selling a CRM to a mid-sized logistics company

Let's make the theory concrete with an example. The following scenario is fictional, but it reflects a picture commonly seen in the field.

A CRM consultancy is trying to sell a CRM implementation and integration project to a logistics company with around 150 employees. The project's estimated cost, including annual licences and implementation, is a mid-sized budget. The first contact is made with the sales manager.

**Initial draft map (after desk research):**

- Sales Manager – Influence: 3, Support: +2 (very keen in the first meeting)
- CEO – Influence: 5, Support: 0 (no contact yet)
- Finance Director – Influence: 4, Support: 0
- IT Manager – Influence: 4, Support: 0
- Sales Reps (12 people) – Influence: 2, Support: unknown

**What emerged after the discovery meetings:**

In the second meeting with the sales manager, it turns out that the company tried to switch to another CRM two years ago and the project was abandoned halfway. The person who led that project was the IT manager. On the map, the IT manager's support score is lowered from 0 to -1: they are wary of a failed project being repeated.

It also emerges that the operations director is the CEO's closest adviser and is consulted on every investment decision. This person, who has no direct link to sales on the org chart, is added to the map with an influence score of 4.

**Action plan:**

1. A listening meeting themed "What went wrong in the previous project?" is requested with the IT manager. No demo, no presentation; just questions.
2. Based on the concerns raised in that meeting, a phased migration plan is prepared: first, a 30-day pilot in a single regional office.
3. A one-page payback analysis is prepared for the finance director, calculating the cost of the current manual quoting process. For example, assuming each sales rep spends roughly 4 hours a week on reporting and quote follow-up, that adds up to 48 hours a week across the 12-person team, roughly equivalent to one full-time employee. The assumptions in the calculation are validated together with the client.
4. The operations director is offered a short meeting showing how the CRM could integrate with shipment planning; this way, the project shifts from being a "sales project" to a "company project".
5. Two of the sales reps are included in the pilot, bringing the users' voice to the decision table.

Without the map in this scenario, the sales team would most likely have relied on the sales manager's enthusiasm, sent the proposal, and then waited for months in the face of the IT manager's silent resistance.

## How do you maintain a decision-maker map in your CRM?

Keeping the map on a slide or in a sales rep's notebook is the surest way to lose the information. In particular, when the rep leaves the company, all the relationship knowledge goes with them. We covered this risk and how to mitigate it in detail in our article on [how to protect customer information when a sales rep leaves](/blog/protect-customer-knowledge-when-sales-rep-leaves).

Most modern CRMs are flexible enough to hold a decision-maker map. Here is the structure we recommend:

- **Custom fields on the contact record:** "Buying role" (dropdown: economic buyer, technical evaluator, user, champion, blocker, influencer), "Influence score" (1–5), "Support score" (-2 to +2).
- **Opportunity–contact association:** Role information should be tied not to the contact but to the relationship between the contact and the opportunity. The same person can be a champion on one opportunity and merely a user on another.
- **Opportunity-level checkpoint fields:** Yes/no fields such as "Met with economic buyer?", "Technical approval obtained?" and "Champion confirmed?".
- **Stage progression rules:** For example, you can require the economic buyer field to be filled in before an opportunity can move to the "Proposal" stage.

In HubSpot, you can set this up with association labels; in Pipedrive, with custom fields and contact–deal links. ## Where does AI help with decision-maker mapping?

AI won't build the map for you, but it can significantly lighten the research and analysis workload:

- **Account research:** Summarising the company's annual reports, press releases and job postings to identify likely priorities.
- **Meeting note analysis:** Automatically extracting the people mentioned, concerns and objections from meeting notes.
- **Role prediction:** Generating an initial guess at a person's likely buying role from their job title and department.
- **Message personalisation:** Drafting messages that describe the same product in different language for the finance director and the IT manager.

Two caveats matter here. First, AI predictions are hypotheses; they should not go on the map as "confirmed information" without human verification. Second, you must not overlook your legal obligations when feeding meeting recordings and personal data into AI tools. ## What are the most common mistakes when building a decision-maker map?

- **Confusing title with influence:** A "Director" title doesn't automatically mean high influence. Read influence from behaviour and process.
- **Relying on a single champion:** If your champion goes on leave or leaves the company, the opportunity is left without an owner. Aim for at least two supporters on every critical opportunity.
- **Avoiding blockers:** Never talking to an opposing stakeholder means letting them voice their objections in your absence. Talk to them early and openly.
- **Leaving procurement and legal until the last minute:** When these departments get involved at the contract stage, new terms and weeks of extra time can come into play. Learn their processes from the very beginning.
- **Mapping only for large opportunities:** For smaller opportunities, a simple version (3–4 people, two-axis scoring) can be put together in ten minutes and reduces surprises.
- **Ignoring users:** If post-purchase adoption is low, renewal and upsell opportunities are lost as well.

## How do you know the map is working?

You can track a few simple indicators to measure the value of your decision-maker map:

- **Multi-threading rate:** In what percentage of your open opportunities have you engaged three or more stakeholders?
- **Economic buyer access rate:** In how many of the opportunities that reached the proposal stage did you speak directly with the budget owner?
- **Loss reasons:** Is the share of opportunities lost due to "decision postponed" or "internal priorities changed" declining over time?
- **Sales cycle length:** Is there a difference in time to close between mapped and unmapped opportunities?

Comparing these metrics over several quarters lets you see, with your own data, which roles' access most affects your win probability. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many people should a decision-maker map include?

There is no fixed number; it depends on the size of the opportunity and the company's structure. In a small company, two or three people may be enough, while in an enterprise sale many stakeholders from different departments may go on the map. What matters is that the budget owner, technical evaluator and potential veto holder roles are not left empty.

### How can I tell whether a champion is genuine?

A genuine champion shares internal information with you, introduces you to other stakeholders and advocates for the project in meetings you're not part of. As a simple test, ask them to set up an introductory meeting with the economic buyer. If they give evasive answers, what you have is not a champion but merely an interested contact.

### Is it better to reach the decision-maker directly or to go through the champion?

Where possible, being introduced through the champion always makes for a stronger start, because trust is transferred. However, if the champion keeps putting off the introduction, making direct contact with a role-specific value proposition is a legitimate alternative. In that case, keeping the champion informed as you proceed is important to avoid damaging the internal relationship.

### At what stage should I build the decision-maker map?

The first draft should be prepared through desk research before the first meeting. It is then validated and expanded through discovery meetings, and before moving to the proposal stage, the budget owner and the technical approver should be clearly identified. After that, it should be updated after every significant interaction until the opportunity closes.

## Conclusion

In B2B sales, the difference between won and lost opportunities often lies not in product quality but in **reaching the right people at the right time**. A decision-maker map lets you define the roles on the buying committee, make each stakeholder's influence and attitude visible, and build a deliberate access plan for the missing links.
