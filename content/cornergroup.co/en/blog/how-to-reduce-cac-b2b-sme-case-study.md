**For B2B SMEs, the fastest way to reduce CAC (customer acquisition cost) comes down to three steps: narrowing your ideal customer profile, shifting budget to the channels that bring in closed customers, and plugging the leaks in your sales funnel with CRM data.** Cutting the budget alone won't lower CAC. What you really need is to win more of the right customers with the same money. In this article, we walk step by step through how an SME selling B2B services cut its CAC roughly in half over about two quarters.

This case reflects a situation we see often in SMEs. For confidentiality reasons, we don't name the company. The figures are representative and rounded. The goal is to show the calculation logic transparently so you can apply the method to your own data. We recommend keeping your own marketing and sales spreadsheet open in another tab as you read.

## What is CAC and why do SMEs calculate it wrong?

CAC is calculated by dividing **all sales and marketing expenses** incurred to acquire new customers in a given period by the number of new customers acquired in that period:

**CAC = (Marketing expenses + Sales expenses) / Number of new customers**

The formula is simple, but in SMEs the calculation usually breaks down in three places:

- **Only ad spend is counted.** Sales team salaries, commissions, CRM and data tools, agency fees and trade show costs are left out. As a result, CAC looks much lower than it really is.
- **Periods are mismatched.** In B2B, the sales cycle can last several months. Money spent in January turns into a customer in April. A monthly view is misleading; a quarterly or rolling three-month average is more reliable.
- **Lead cost is confused with customer cost.** CAC can rise while cost per lead (CPL) falls. Cheap leads aren't cheap if they don't close. We covered the difference between these two metrics in detail in [our guide on how to calculate B2B cost per lead (CPL)](/blog/how-to-calculate-b2b-cost-per-lead-cpl).

CAC isn't meaningful on its own, either. It needs to sit alongside two other metrics: **customer lifetime value (LTV)** and **payback period**. The LTV/CAC ratio shows how many lira you get back for every lira you spend on a customer. Payback tells you how many months it takes to recover that spend through gross profit.

## What was the company's starting position?

The company is a 25-person B2B SME selling operational consulting and annual software licenses to manufacturing and logistics firms. The sales team consists of 2 sales reps and 1 sales manager. On the marketing side, there's one specialist and an external digital agency.

Management's complaint was clear: "We put more money into marketing every quarter, and the number of leads keeps growing, but the number of new customers is flat."

Here's what we found in our first meeting:

- Most leads came from Google Ads and LinkedIn lead forms.
- The sales team tracked leads from their inboxes and in Excel. There was a CRM, but it was only half used.
- Attending two industry trade shows a year consumed a significant share of the budget.
- Nobody knew which channel brought in **closed customers**. Everyone knew which channels brought in **leads**.

## How high was CAC? The baseline calculation

First, we pulled all spending for one quarter into a single table. With representative figures:

- Google Ads and LinkedIn ads: 180,000 TL
- Trade show participation (booth, travel, materials, quarterly share): 120,000 TL
- Digital agency fee: 90,000 TL
- Sales team salaries and commissions (in proportion to time spent on selling): 300,000 TL
- Tools (CRM, email, design, data): 30,000 TL

**Total: 720,000 TL**

The company won 12 new customers that same quarter. **CAC = 720,000 / 12 = 60,000 TL**

The average annual contract value was about 90,000 TL, with a gross margin of around 70%. That works out to roughly 5,250 TL in monthly gross profit per customer. **Payback period = 60,000 / 5,250 ≈ 11.4 months.**

Customers stayed for an average of 2.5 years. That put LTV at roughly 90,000 × 2.5 × 0.70 ≈ 157,500 TL, and **LTV/CAC ≈ 2.6**. That ratio wasn't a disaster. But for an SME with tight cash flow, having every new customer drain cash for about a year was effectively putting the brakes on growth. Our goal was to bring the payback period below 6 months and push LTV/CAC above 4.

## Diagnosis: Where in the funnel was the money disappearing?

The first step in reducing CAC isn't cutting spend; it's **finding the leak**. We combined the last six months of data from the CRM, ad dashboards and the sales team's Excel files to map the funnel by channel.

Here's what emerged:

1. **Ad leads were plentiful but low quality.** A large share of form submissions came from students, competitors' employees, or businesses too small for the company to serve. The sales team spent most of its time filtering out these leads.
2. **The trade shows were expensive, but some closed customers came from them.** The problem wasn't the trade shows; it was the follow-up afterward. A significant portion of the business cards collected had never been contacted within two weeks.
3. **Referrals from existing customers had the highest close rate at almost zero cost.** But this channel was accidental, not systematic.
4. **The sales cycle was long.** It took an average of 4 months from first contact to signature. Opportunities sitting at the proposal stage went weeks without follow-up.
5. **There was no ideal customer definition.** "Manufacturing and logistics" covered everyone from a 10-person workshop to a 2,000-person factory.

In short, the diagnosis was this: the company was paying to bring in leads that would never close, while losing the leads that would close due to lack of follow-up.

## Step 1: Narrowing the ideal customer profile based on data

The first task was to review every customer won over the past two years, one by one. For each customer, we added employee count, industry sub-segment, decision-maker title, contract value, sales cycle length and customer tenure to the table.

The picture was clear: the most profitable and longest-staying customers were **multi-location manufacturers with 100–500 employees and their own warehouses**. The decision-maker was usually the operations director or plant manager. Customers matching this profile had noticeably shorter sales cycles than average and higher contract values.

Based on this, the new ideal customer profile (ICP) was defined as follows:

- **Industry:** Multi-location manufacturing, manufacturers with their own warehouses
- **Size:** 100–500 employees
- **Decision-maker:** Operations director, plant manager, supply chain manager
- **Trigger signals:** New facility openings, warehouse expansions, job postings for operations roles

This definition became the filter for every subsequent step. Ad targeting, outbound lists, content topics and sales call questions were all rewritten around this profile.

## Step 2: Shifting budget to channels that bring in closed customers

Once the ICP was clear, we made decisions channel by channel.

### Paid ads: Sharpen, don't cut

In Google Ads, broad keywords were switched off. Only industry-specific searches showing purchase intent were kept. On LinkedIn, targeting was narrowed by company size and job title. "Number of employees" and "number of locations" fields were added to the forms. Because the forms got longer, lead volume dropped, but quality rose significantly. The ad budget was reduced from 180,000 TL to 110,000 TL.

### Outbound: Opening a controlled, targeted channel

Thanks to the new ICP, the target market shrank to a few thousand companies that could actually be listed. That made it ideal ground for outbound. Target companies and decision-makers were filtered and listed using a B2B data platform such as [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya), then cleaned up through manual review. A trigger signal (such as a new facility or job posting) was noted for each account, and messages were personalized around that signal.

Infrastructure was the critical piece. To avoid putting the main domain at risk, a separate sending domain was set up and gradually warmed up over several weeks. We covered the technical details of this process in [our case study on domain warm-up for cold email](/blog/cold-email-domain-warm-up-b2b-case-study). Not a single campaign was launched until warm-up was complete.

The outbound sequence consisted of 4 touches: a personalized first email, a LinkedIn connection request, a follow-up email with a short case summary, and a polite break-up message. ### LinkedIn organic: Making the sales team visible

Instead of the company page, the personal profiles of the sales manager and founder were put front and center. Twice a week, they published short observations from the field, practical tips on warehouse efficiency, and anonymized case summaries shared with customer permission. As a result, anyone who received an outbound message and checked the profile found a trust-building content feed. This indirectly supported reply rates. ### Trade shows: One show, tight follow-up

One of the two trade shows was dropped, keeping the one that drew the most ICP-fit visitors. Meetings with target companies were scheduled before the show. A 48-hour rule was introduced for post-show follow-up: every contact had to be entered into the CRM and sent a personalized message within 48 hours. The trade show budget fell from 120,000 TL to 60,000 TL per quarter.

## Step 3: Turning the CRM into a real decision-making tool

If you can't measure CAC by channel, you can't reduce it. So three mandatory fields were defined in the CRM:

- **Lead source** (ads, outbound, trade show, referral, organic)
- **ICP fit** (A/B/C)
- **Loss reason** (budget, timing, competitor, poor fit, no response)

The sales team's resistance to the CRM was predictable. The solution was to turn the CRM from extra work into a tool that made their jobs easier. ### Eliminating manual work with n8n

A handful of simple n8n automations took over manual work the sales team had been spending hours on every week:

1. **Form → CRM flow:** Leads from ad forms were automatically added to the CRM and given an A/B/C score based on employee count and industry. C-scored leads were routed to a nurture email sequence before ever reaching sales.
2. **Trade show business card flow:** Photos of business cards taken on a phone at the show were read by an AI step, a contact record was created in the CRM, and a task was assigned to a sales rep.
3. **Proposal follow-up reminder:** If an opportunity sat idle at the proposal stage for 5 business days, the rep received a Slack notification; after 10 days, it was escalated to the sales manager.
4. **Weekly channel report:** Every Monday, new leads, opportunities, won customers and spend by channel were sent to management in a single summary table.

Total setup time for these automations was no more than a few weeks. Their most important impact was shifting the sales reps' time away from filtering and toward real sales conversations.

## Step 4: Shortening the sales cycle

The longer the sales cycle, the higher the CAC. Every hour a rep spends on an opportunity adds directly to cost through salary. That's why shortening the cycle is a direct CAC lever.

The changes implemented were:

- **The discovery call was standardized.** No opportunity moved to the next stage without asking about budget, decision process, timing and a concrete operational pain point in the first call.
- **Proposal turnaround was shortened.** Proposals were tied to modular templates. Preparation time dropped from days to hours.
- **The decision-maker was brought in early.** The operations director was always invited to the second meeting. This shortened the "I'll present it to management" waiting loop.
- **A weekly pipeline meeting was established.** Stalled opportunities were discussed one by one every week. We shared the structure of this meeting in [our weekly pipeline meeting guide](/blog/weekly-pipeline-meeting-guide).

## Step 5: Making referrals systematic instead of accidental

The cheapest customer is one recommended by an existing customer. But the company wasn't managing this channel at all. A simple program was put in place:

- On day 90 of each project, a short review call was held with customers who had seen measurable improvement.
- Satisfied customers were asked to send an introduction email to contacts in their industry.
- For every new customer that came through a referral, the referrer received a discount on their next period's service fee.

The cost of this channel consisted almost entirely of the discounts. Yet its close rate was the highest of all channels.

## Results: What happened to CAC after two quarters?

The first quarter of changes was largely a setup and transition period. The real impact became visible in the second quarter. Here's the representative picture for the second quarter:

- Google Ads and LinkedIn ads: 110,000 TL
- Trade show (one show, quarterly share): 60,000 TL
- Agency fee (reduced scope): 50,000 TL
- Outbound data, domain and tools: 25,000 TL
- Sales team salaries and commissions: 320,000 TL (commissions increased)
- CRM and automation tools: 35,000 TL

**Total: 600,000 TL**

The number of new customers won rose to 20. **CAC = 600,000 / 20 = 30,000 TL.**

With the same average contract value and margin, **the payback period fell to about 5.7 months**. **The LTV/CAC ratio rose above 5.** Since ICP-fit customers tend to stay longer, LTV is expected to increase further over time.

A few points stand out:

- **Total spend fell by only about 17%.** The main reason CAC was cut in half wasn't spending cuts; it was the increase in customer count.
- **Sales team costs went up.** As more customers closed, commissions rose. That's a healthy cost increase.
- **Lead volume fell, opportunity count rose.** Total lead volume decreased, but the share of leads that turned into sales conversations rose significantly.

## What mistakes should you avoid when reducing CAC?

It's also worth noting the traps SMEs often fall into during this process:

1. **Just cutting the budget.** Switching off ads can make CAC appear to drop for a quarter. Then the pipeline dries up and customer numbers collapse the following quarter.
2. **Optimizing for CPL.** Giving your agency a "lower the cost per lead" target rewards low-quality leads. The target should always be **customer acquisition cost by channel**.
3. **Jumping into outbound without infrastructure.** Mass sends from an unwarmed domain with unverified data damage your reputation and can land your domain on spam lists.
4. **Ignoring the sales cycle.** In B2B, this quarter's spend brings in next quarter's customers. Decisions should be based on at least three months of data.
5. **Tracking CAC in isolation.** Cheap customers who churn quickly can be more damaging than expensive but loyal ones. Always track LTV alongside it.

## Where should you start to reduce your own CAC?

To kick off a similar process at your company, we suggest a 90-day framework:

**First 30 days: Measurement**
- Pull all sales and marketing spend from the last two quarters into a single table, including salaries.
- Retroactively identify the source of every customer won.
- Calculate CAC by channel. You'll likely see major differences between channels.

**Days 31–60: Focus**
- Derive your ICP from your most profitable customers.
- Cut budget in the most expensive channel that brings in the fewest customers.
- Make lead source, ICP score and loss reason mandatory fields in your CRM.

**Days 61–90: Scaling**
- Launch a targeted outbound pilot based on your ICP.
- Set up simple automations for proposal follow-up and lead routing.
- Define your referral program and send out the first requests.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good CAC for B2B SMEs?

There's no absolute "good CAC" figure. CAC should always be assessed alongside the value a customer brings. In practice, an LTV/CAC ratio above 3 and a payback period under 12 months are considered healthy. For SMEs with tight cash flow, targeting a payback period of 6–9 months is safer.

### Should sales team salaries be included in the CAC calculation?

Yes. Sales team salaries and commissions should be included in CAC in proportion to the time spent acquiring new customers. Leaving them out makes CAC look lower than it is and leads to poor channel decisions. Time spent managing existing customers can be excluded from the calculation.

### How long does it take to reduce CAC?

Because B2B sales cycles last several months, a meaningful reduction is usually seen within two quarters. The first quarter goes to measurement, defining the ICP and reorganizing channels. The increase in closed customers typically becomes clear in the second quarter.

### Does outbound sales really reduce CAC?

If the ICP is clearly defined and the target market is small enough to be listed, outbound generally delivers a lower customer acquisition cost than broadly targeted ads. The prerequisites are accurate data, a warmed-up domain and personalized messaging. Outbound done through mass sends without proper infrastructure, on the other hand, increases both costs and brand risk.

## Conclusion

Reducing CAC is less about cutting spend and more about **directing money to the right places**. In this case, the company reduced its total budget only modestly. But it narrowed its ICP, rebuilt its channels around closed-customer data, turned its CRM into a decision-making tool, and used automation to close the follow-up gaps in its sales cycle. As a result, CAC was cut in half, and the payback period dropped from a year to under six months.
