A new sales rep's first 90 days should be planned in three phases: **days 1–30 for learning, days 31–60 for guided execution, and days 61–90 for independent ownership.** Each phase needs written goals, weekly checkpoints and measurable activity metrics. In the early months, closed deals are an outcome, not the primary measure of success. For SMBs, the most critical part of this plan is tying every week to concrete deliverables that a manager tracks, instead of sending the rep into the field to "figure it out on their own."

Large companies have onboarding departments, training academies and ready-made content libraries for new sales hires. In SMBs, the owner or a single sales manager usually carries this load on top of their day-to-day work. That's why a good plan isn't a luxury; it's a necessity. It shortens the rep's time to productivity, helps you spot a bad hire early, and makes the company's sales knowledge independent of any one person. In this guide, you'll find a day-by-day, week-by-week plan that a B2B company of 5 to 50 people can put into practice.

## Why are the first 90 days so decisive?

In the first three months, a new sales rep has to learn three things at once: **the product, the customer and the process.** If one is missing, the other two suffer. A rep who knows the product but doesn't understand the customer's pain will just list features. A rep who understands the customer but doesn't know the process won't log opportunities in the CRM and will miss follow-ups.

For SMBs, this period also has a cost side. The rep's salary, benefits, tools and the manager's time start adding up from day one. Revenue, however, often starts coming in only after 3–6 months, depending on the length of the B2B sales cycle. Unplanned onboarding widens this gap. Planned onboarding helps the rep fill the pipeline sooner.

The third reason is this: the first 90 days are the most honest window for finding out whether the hire was the right one. Without clear goals, you fall into a "let's give it a bit more time" loop, and a bad hire can drag on for 9–12 months.

## What should be ready before they start?

The plan starts not on the rep's first day, but at least a week before it. During what we might call "week zero," prepare the following:

- **Tool access:** Email, CRM account, phone/VoIP, calendar, shared drive, LinkedIn Sales Navigator (if you use it) and your internal communication tool. A rep who spends their first day waiting for passwords forms the wrong first impression of how the company operates.
- **Sales playbook:** It can be short; 10–15 pages is enough. This document should cover your ideal customer profile, buyer personas, the 10 most common objections and how to answer them, pricing logic, competitor comparison and definitions of your sales stages.
- **Recorded calls:** 5–10 recordings of discovery calls and demos by your best salesperson or the founder. If you have no recordings, start recording this week.
- **Territory or account list:** The segment, industry or region the rep will work on should be defined from the outset. Saying "you can call anyone" means nobody gets called the right way.
- **Written 30-60-90 goals:** This document should be read together with the rep on day one and mutually agreed, even if it isn't signed.

If much of this material doesn't exist in your company at all, [building a sales enablement program](https://cornergroup.co/blog/b2b-satis-ekipleri-i-cin-etkili-satis-enablement-programi-olusturma) before you hire will lower the cost of every future hire.

## The first 30 days: Learning and observation

The goal of the first month isn't to sell, but **to build the foundation in the rep's mind for selling the right way.** During this period, the rep observes, asks questions, takes notes and gets involved in the process through small, low-risk tasks.

### Week 1: Company, product and customer

- A 60-minute meeting with the founder or CEO: Why does the company exist, what problem does it solve, and who are the best customers?
- 2–3 sessions with the product or service team: How does delivery work, and where do customers struggle most?
- A list of the 10 most frequent complaints and 10 most frequent compliments from the customer service or account management team.
- Short introductory calls with 2–3 existing customers (where appropriate). Hearing the answer to "why did you choose us?" directly from a customer is worth more than any presentation.
- **End-of-week deliverable:** The rep should be able to explain the company's value proposition in 60 seconds, in their own words.

### Week 2: Process and tools

- Learning the definitions of the sales stages in the CRM: Under what conditions does an opportunity move from "Discovery" to "Proposal"?
- Reviewing the history of 10 opportunities in the current pipeline: Who was involved, when, with which email, and what objections came up?
- Listening in on at least 5 live sales calls (as a silent participant).
- Reviewing email templates and sequences.
- **End-of-week deliverable:** The rep should be able to log an opportunity correctly in the CRM from start to finish and explain the stages.

### Week 3: Market and competitors

- Building a target list of 50 companies that fit the ideal customer profile.
- Summarizing the websites, pricing (if public) and positioning of 3 main competitors.
- Researching what decision-makers in target industries are posting on LinkedIn.
- Role-play sessions: The manager plays the customer while the rep runs a discovery call. Hold at least 2 sessions a week.

### Week 4: First outreach

- First external outreach to relatively warm sources: Old opportunities, lost proposals, and web form leads that were never followed up.
- Start with 15–20 activities a day (calls, emails, LinkedIn messages) and ramp up toward the end of the week.
- Running the first 2–3 discovery calls with the manager present.
- **Day 30 review:** A 45-minute one-on-one with the manager. What has the rep learned well, where are the gaps, and where is extra support needed?

**Sample goals for day 30:**

- Passing the product and value proposition test (a short 15–20 question quiz prepared by the manager)
- Having entered the 50-company target list and 150+ relevant contacts into the CRM
- Having listened to 10+ sales calls and taken part in 4+ role-play sessions
- Having run 2–3 real discovery calls

## Days 31–60: Guided execution

In the second month, the rep is in the field, but not alone. The key word for this period is **rhythm**: a set activity volume every day and a set feedback loop every week.

### How should the daily rhythm be set up?

A daily template that works well in SMBs might look like this:

1. **09:00–09:30:** Reviewing the day's tasks and follow-ups in the CRM.
2. **09:30–11:30:** Prospecting block: Cold calls, emails and LinkedIn messages. This block should be closed to meetings.
3. **11:30–12:30:** Follow-up emails, proposal preparation.
4. **14:00–16:30:** Discovery calls, demos, customer meetings.
5. **16:30–17:30:** CRM updates, preparing for the next day, learning (listening to recordings, reading).

The value of this template is that it stops the rep from wasting time wondering "what should I do today?" By the end of the second month, this rhythm should have become a habit.

Still, a tool is no substitute for an ideal customer profile. The question "who are we looking for?" has to be answered first.

### The weekly feedback loop

In the second month, the manager's weekly routine should include:

- **1 one-on-one per week (30 min):** Activity metrics, stalled opportunities, learning needs.
- **2 call recording reviews per week:** Listening together to a selected segment of the rep's own recording and identifying one concrete point for improvement. Instead of a generic comment like "ask better questions," give actionable feedback such as "ask the budget question after you've clarified the problem, not in the first 10 minutes."
- **Joining the pipeline meeting:** The rep should learn to present their own opportunities in front of the team. Our [weekly pipeline meeting guide](/blog/weekly-pipeline-meeting-guide) suggests a format for running these meetings efficiently that also includes new reps.

### Embedding the sales methodology

During this period, the rep should start applying the sales approach the company has adopted (SPIN, Challenger, MEDDIC or a framework of your own). What matters isn't the name of the methodology but consistency. Asking the same core questions in every discovery call and entering the answers in the same CRM fields makes it easier both to measure the rep's development and to forecast sales.

**Sample goals for day 60:**

- 40–60 prospecting activities per day (varies by industry and sales model)
- 4–6 discovery calls per week
- 8–12 qualified opportunities in the pipeline that the rep sourced themselves
- Complete CRM data: Next step, date and decision-maker details filled in for every opportunity
- At least 1 opportunity that has reached the proposal stage

## Days 61–90: Ownership and independence

In the third month, the rep owns their pipeline. The manager's role shifts from "sitting alongside" to "supporting when needed."

### What changes during this period?

- The rep runs discovery calls and demos **on their own**. The manager joins only for critical opportunities or when asked.
- Preparing proposals, negotiating price and running closing calls become the rep's responsibility. Limits such as discount authority should be clearly defined (for example, "you can approve discounts up to 10%; anything above that needs approval").
- The rep builds their own weekly plan and presents it to the manager.
- The first deals start to close. In businesses with a short sales cycle (say, 30–45 days), expecting 1–3 closed deals in this period is reasonable. If your cycle is 4–6 months, look at the number of late-stage opportunities instead of closed deals.

### Commission and motivation

The third month is when the commission plan really kicks in. Many SMBs define a "ramp period" for the first 2–3 months, during which the rep receives a guaranteed bonus or a reduced quota. This structure keeps the rep from selling to the wrong customers under short-term pressure to close in the early months. For how to structure the ramp period and what comes after, take a look at our [guide to building a B2B sales commission plan](/blog/b2b-sales-commission-plan-guide).

**Sample goals for day 90:**

- Pipeline value equal to 30–50% of full quota (depending on the sales cycle)
- 6–8 discovery calls per week
- 2–4 active proposals
- First closed deal(s), or 1–2 opportunities very close to closing
- At least 5 demos run without manager involvement

## Which metrics should you measure?

The most common mistake is measuring a new rep's performance in the first 90 days **by revenue alone**. Revenue is a lagging indicator. What you really need to watch are leading indicators.

| Period | Main focus | Sample metrics |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–30 | Knowledge and preparation | Product test, number of calls listened to, target list built |
| Days 31–60 | Activity and quality | Daily activity, number of discovery calls, call-to-meeting conversion |
| Days 61–90 | Pipeline and results | Pipeline value, stage conversion rates, number of proposals, first closed deals |

Our [B2B sales team KPI guide](/blog/b2b-sales-team-kpis) offers a more detailed framework on what each metric means at each stage and which ones are truly leading indicators.

A word of caution: metrics only mean something if your CRM data is clean. That's why you should enforce the "if it's not in the CRM, it didn't happen" rule from day one. This rule makes measurement possible and ensures customer relationships stay with the company if the rep ever leaves. ## How much does a new rep cost, and when do they pay off?

It's impossible to give an exact figure, because salary levels, industries and sales cycles vary widely from company to company. Still, you can use a simple framework to run the numbers yourself:

1. **Direct cost:** Gross salary + employer contributions + benefits + tool/software licenses (CRM, data tool, phone, LinkedIn) + ramp bonus, if any.
2. **Indirect cost:** The manager's time. 8–10 hours a week in the first month, 4–6 hours in the second and 2–3 hours in the third is a reasonable estimate. Also factor in the time these hours take away from the manager's own selling.
3. **Payback period:** Take your average sales cycle and average deal size. Calculate how many closed deals the rep needs to generate enough gross profit to cover their total monthly cost.

A sample calculation: Say the rep's total monthly cost is 100 units, the gross profit on your average deal is 150 units, and your sales cycle is 60 days. If the rep starts building pipeline at the beginning of the second month, the first deals will close around the fourth month. Once they reach an average of 1 closed deal per month, they're covering their cost. This simple calculation gives a realistic answer to "why are there still no closed deals in month 3?" and helps you manage expectations properly.

## What are the most common mistakes SMBs make in the first 90 days?

- **Assigning quota from week one:** Under pressure, the rep sells to the wrong customer at the wrong price, or burns out.
- **Skipping shadowing:** Cutting the observation period short because "they're experienced anyway." Experience at a previous company doesn't mean knowing your customers and your product.
- **Not setting written goals:** Verbal expectations are remembered differently by day 30. A goal that isn't written down can't be evaluated.
- **Giving feedback only on what goes wrong:** Name the specific behaviors that go well, too ("finding out the customer's budget limit early in that call was great"). You make visible the behavior you want repeated.
- **Putting off CRM discipline:** A habit that doesn't take hold in the first month almost never takes hold later.
- **Leaving the rep on their own:** SMB managers are busy, but the weekly 30-minute one-on-one is the one meeting that should never be canceled.
- **Ignoring personal branding:** B2B buyers look at a rep's LinkedIn profile before meeting them. A professional profile and regular, value-adding posts can raise response rates to cold outreach. ## How can AI and automation speed up onboarding?

In small teams, AI and automation tools can tangibly lighten the manager's load:

- **Call summaries:** Summarizing recorded calls with AI lets the manager review a 45-minute recording in 5 minutes.
- **Role-play practice:** The rep can practice handling objections by asking an AI assistant to play a specific buyer persona (for example, "a cost-focused, skeptical procurement manager").
- **Automated reminders with n8n:** For example, an automatic notification to the rep for opportunities that haven't been updated in the CRM for 7 days, or a weekly activity summary emailed to the manager every Friday. These can be set up with simple workflows.
- **Account research:** Generating a prep note before a meeting that summarizes the target company's latest news, hires and LinkedIn posts.

But remember one limit: AI is no substitute for the rep truly understanding the customer's problem. Use tools to speed up learning, not to skip it.

## What should you do at the end of day 90?

Day 90 should close with a formal review meeting. In this meeting, give clear answers to three questions:

1. **How many of the goals were met?** Go through the written 30-60-90 goals one by one.
2. **Is the trajectory right?** Even if the numbers don't fully add up, is the trend upward? Is activity quality improving?
3. **What's the plan for the next 90 days?** The timeline for moving to full quota, development areas and additional training needs.

If the outcome is negative, talk about it honestly. Sometimes the problem isn't the rep but the target market, the playbook or product-market fit. If more than one rep gets stuck at the same point, the problem is likely in the system, not in the hiring.

## Frequently asked questions

### When should a new sales rep move to full quota?

In most B2B SMBs, the move to full quota happens between months 3 and 6. The longer your sales cycle, the longer the ramp period should be. A practical approach is to increase quota gradually: 25% in month 2, 50% in month 3, 75% in month 4 and 100% from month 5 onward.

### Does an experienced salesperson also need a 90-day plan?

Yes, but the content differs. An experienced rep knows sales techniques, but doesn't know your product, customer profile, competitors or internal processes. For this person, the plan can be applied with a shorter observation period and a faster path to independence.

### Should a new rep make cold calls in the first 30 days?

Generally not in the first two weeks. That period should be used to build product and customer knowledge. In weeks 3 and 4, the healthiest approach is to start with warmer sources such as old opportunities, lost proposals and form leads that were never followed up, then gradually move to fully cold calling.

### How do I know at the end of 90 days that the hire was a mistake?

If activity goals are consistently missed, feedback isn't applied and CRM discipline never develops, that's a strong signal. On the other hand, if activity is high and learning is fast but there are no closed deals yet, the issue most likely has to do with timing or the sales cycle. Written, measurable goals are essential for telling these two situations apart.

## Conclusion

A new sales rep's first 90 days are one of the most important investments an SMB makes in its sales capacity. A plan divided into three phases (learning, guided execution and ownership), with each phase tied to written goals and weekly feedback, shortens ramp time, surfaces bad hires early and keeps sales knowledge within the company, independent of any one person. The success of this plan comes less from a perfect document than from consistency. Weekly one-on-ones that never get canceled, a complete CRM and a measurement approach focused on leading indicators form its foundation.
