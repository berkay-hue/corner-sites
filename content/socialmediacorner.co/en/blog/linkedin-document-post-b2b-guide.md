A LinkedIn document post is a PDF, PPT or DOC file shared in the feed as a set of swipeable pages. For B2B companies, the formula for a successful document post is simple: prepare **a 6-12 page PDF that solves one problem, reads well on mobile, carries one idea per page and ends with a clear call to action**, then publish it with a strong opening caption. Because this format can convey the essence of a long blog post in a few minutes and moves readers forward page by page, it is one of the most effective ways to reach decision-makers.

In this guide, we cover when document posts work, their technical requirements, how to create one step by step, a sample page structure, how to write the post caption and how to measure performance. The goal is to give you a practical framework so you can get your first document ready to publish this week.

## What is a LinkedIn document post and why does it work for B2B?

On LinkedIn, document posts are commonly referred to as "carousels." Technically, though, they are files uploaded via the "Add a document" option when creating a post. Users see the first page in the feed and move to the next pages by swiping right or using the arrows. They can also be viewed in full-screen mode.

On the B2B side, several things make this format valuable:

- **It increases dwell time.** Readers stay on the post longer as they flip through the pages. The LinkedIn feed treats the time a user spends on a piece of content as an important signal. We cover this in detail in our [guide to how the LinkedIn algorithm works](/blog/how-linkedin-algorithm-works-b2b-guide).
- **It simplifies complex topics.** The steps of a procurement process, the components of a cost calculation or a compliance checklist can feel overwhelming as plain text, but become digestible when split into pages.
- **It gets saved and shared.** Checklists, templates and frameworks are the kind of content people save to "look at later." Saves are also one of the signals that support distribution.
- **It demonstrates expertise.** A well-crafted document proves that your company thinks about the topic systematically. It builds trust before a sales conversation even begins.
- **It is reusable.** The same content can be repurposed in sales presentations, email newsletters or as a downloadable resource on your website.

## Which topics suit the document format?

Not every piece of content is worth turning into a document. Creating a document for a single announcement, a short opinion or an event photo is wasted effort. The document format shines with content that has **structure**:

1. **Step-by-step processes:** "7 critical steps in an ERP migration," "A 5-stage method for supplier evaluation"
2. **Checklists:** "10 items to check before a cybersecurity audit"
3. **Comparisons:** "Lease or buy? A comparison across 6 criteria"
4. **Mistake lists:** "The 8 most common mistakes in industrial procurement"
5. **Frameworks and models:** A model that visualizes your own methodology
6. **Data and research summaries:** Key findings from your own survey or industry report
7. **Case stories:** An anonymized project story following a problem, approach and result flow
8. **Mini guides:** A summarized, visual version of a blog post

Here's a practical test: if you can turn the topic into a 6-10 item list, it will most likely make a good document.

## Technical requirements: file type, size and page count

Knowing the technical limits before you start designing prevents last-minute surprises.

- **Supported formats:** PDF, PPT, PPTX, DOC and DOCX. In practice, **PDF** is the safest option; fonts and layout display without changes.
- **File size:** LinkedIn allows up to 100 MB. However, keeping the file under 10-20 MB makes sense for fast loading. Compress high-resolution photos.
- **Page limit:** The technical maximum is 300 pages. There is no point in coming anywhere near this limit in organic posts.
- **Page ratio:** The two most common choices are **1:1 square (1080×1080 pixels)** and **4:5 portrait (1080×1350 pixels)**. The portrait format takes up more space in the mobile feed and draws more attention. Landscape (16:9) presentation pages can also be uploaded, but because they appear small on mobile, the text may become unreadable.
- **Document title:** LinkedIn asks for a document title during upload. This title appears at the top of the document. Instead of file names like "Presentation_final_v3," write a title that promises the reader a benefit.
- **Accessibility:** Because the text is embedded in images, screen readers may not be able to detect the content. It is good practice to also summarize the main idea in writing in the post caption.

## How do you create a LinkedIn document post step by step?

### 1. Clarify the goal and the reader

Every document should serve a single goal. Is it brand awareness, follower growth, website traffic or a warm conversation starter for the sales team? Documents written without a defined goal usually try to say everything and end up saying nothing well.

Next, define the reader. "Maintenance managers at manufacturing companies" and "finance directors at SaaS companies" don't speak the same language. Doing a B2B persona exercise first to clarify who you are writing for makes it easier to choose the right tone and examples.

### 2. Define a single promise

The cover page should offer the reader a one-sentence promise. Compare weak and strong examples:

- Weak: "Digital transformation in logistics"
- Strong: "7 steps you can take to reduce warehouse errors"

The second title clearly expresses the scope (7 steps), the benefit (reducing errors) and the actionability ("you can take").

### 3. Plan the flow

The skeleton of a good document often looks like this:

1. **Cover:** The promise and a short subtitle
2. **Problem:** A pain point the reader recognizes
3. **Why it matters:** The cost or risk of the problem (in general terms, without made-up numbers)
4. **Solution pages:** One item or one step per page
5. **Summary:** All items at a glance on a single page (encourages saves)
6. **Call to action:** Follow, comment, request a demo, download the resource

Write this structure first in a simple text document, and move to design only once the content is settled. Writing content inside a design tool wastes time and lowers the quality of the copy.

### 4. Keep the text short on every page

A document page is not a blog paragraph. A practical rule: **one headline and no more than 25-40 words of explanation per page.** The headline should make sense even when read on its own, because some readers move through the document by scanning only the headlines.

A sample page:

> **Step 3: Set counting frequency based on product value**
> Count high-value products weekly, mid-value products monthly and low-value products quarterly. This way, the team spends its time where the risk of loss is highest.

### 5. Design for mobile

A significant share of LinkedIn usage happens on mobile. Don't publish without testing the design on a phone.

- **Font size:** At least 40-48 pt for headlines and at least 24-28 pt for body text (for a 1080-pixel width).
- **Contrast:** Dark text on a light background, or vice versa. Avoid gray text on gray.
- **Margins:** Leave at least 60-80 pixels of space on every side.
- **Consistency:** The same font, the same color palette, the same headline position. Stick to 2-3 colors from your brand identity.
- **Page number and progress:** An indicator like "3/9" lets readers know where they are and increases the urge to finish.
- **Swipe cue:** A small arrow or the words "Swipe →" on the cover can move people from the first page to the second.
- **Logo:** Small and in the corner of every page. When the document is shared as a screenshot, your brand remains visible as the source.

Canva, Figma, PowerPoint or Google Slides are all sufficient for the job. What matters isn't the tool but a consistent template. Once you build a good template, subsequent documents can be produced much faster.

### 6. Design the call-to-action page with care

The person who reads the document to the end is your most engaged reader. Tell them clearly what you want them to do. Choose a single call to action:

- "Share this list with your team"
- "Which step is the hardest in your process? Tell us in the comments"
- "Follow our page for more operations content"
- "Visit the link in our profile for a free assessment"

Links inside documents may not always be clickable. That's why it's safer to put the main link in the post caption or the first comment.

### 7. Export and check the file

After exporting to PDF, run through these checks:

- Are all pages the same size?
- Have spelling and punctuation errors been cleaned up?
- Is the file size reasonable?
- Is every page easy to read when opened on a phone?
- Is there any customer information, pricing or internal data that should remain confidential?

### 8. Upload and publish

On the post creation screen, select the document icon, upload the file, enter the document title and add your post caption. You can publish from the company page or a personal profile. Set the publishing time according to your content plan. ## How many pages should a document have?

There is no definitive answer, but experience supports the following ranges:

- **6-12 pages for organic posts:** Long enough to deliver value, short enough to be read to the end.
- **12-20 pages for in-depth guides:** Only if the topic truly calls for it. In that case, stating the page count on the cover helps manage the reader's expectations.
- **For documents used as ads:** Preview pages can be short, while the full content can be more comprehensive. We touch on this below.

If removing a page doesn't take anything away from the document, that page is unnecessary. Filler pages lower completion rates.

## Example: a 9-page document structure

Let's imagine a document from an industrial maintenance software company titled "6 steps to reduce unplanned downtime":

1. **Cover:** "6 steps to reduce unplanned downtime | A practical checklist for maintenance teams"
2. **Problem:** "Why is it so hard to act before a breakdown happens, rather than when it does?" Followed by two sentences describing the knock-on effects of unexpected stoppages on the production line.
3. **Step 1:** List your critical equipment. Classify each machine as A, B or C based on its impact on downtime.
4. **Step 2:** Collect failure history in one place. Move from paper forms to digital records.
5. **Step 3:** Build a preventive maintenance schedule using both manufacturer recommendations and actual usage data.
6. **Step 4:** Set spare parts inventory based on the critical equipment list.
7. **Step 5:** Make it easy for operators to report early warning signs.
8. **Step 6:** Review mean time between failures and repair time every month.
9. **Summary and call to action:** A one-page summary of the six steps and the line "Which step would you start with? Tell us in the comments."

Notice that there is no product promotion anywhere in the document. Value comes first. Readers figure out for themselves which of these steps the software could help with. If you like, you can add a single small line to the last page, such as "Demo requests for teams looking to digitize this process," but the heart of the document should remain educational.

## How should you write the post caption?

No matter how good the document is, it's the post caption that brings readers to the first page. Only the first 2-3 lines are visible in the feed; the rest opens with "see more."

**An effective post caption follows this structure:**

1. **Hook (first line):** A problem the reader recognizes or an unexpected observation. Example: "Most maintenance teams are good at fixing breakdowns. The real challenge is predicting them."
2. **Context (2-3 lines):** Why the document was created and who it's for.
3. **Content summary:** A short list of the items in the document. This improves both accessibility and scannability.
4. **Call to action:** A single question or action.
5. **Hashtags:** 2-4 relevant hashtags that are actually in use.

Keeping the post caption between 150 and 300 words is generally a good balance. If you want to add an external link, test its impact on reach with your own data. Some teams prefer to put the link in the first comment.

Responding quickly to comments in the first hour after posting is also important. When employees share the document with their own commentary, it reaches audiences the company page could never reach on its own. ## How do you create documents from existing content?

You don't have to write every document from scratch. Many of the assets you already have can be turned into documents with minor edits:

- **Blog posts:** The subheadings are already candidates for document pages.
- **Webinar presentations:** Adapt landscape slides to a portrait format and select the strongest 8-10 ideas.
- **Sales presentations:** Remove product-focused sections and keep the industry insights.
- **Customer questions:** The 8 questions your sales team hears most often make a perfect "FAQ" document.
- **Proposal attachments and technical documentation:** Process descriptions stripped of confidential information.

You can find the overall logic of this repurposing process in detail in our [guide to adapting blog posts for LinkedIn and Instagram](/blog/adapt-blog-post-for-linkedin-and-instagram). It's also possible to turn the same document into an Instagram carousel with minor changes; however, the page limit and visual expectations are different on Instagram.

Yes. LinkedIn Campaign Manager offers the **Document Ads** format. With this format, you can show your document as sponsored content to your target audience and, if you wish, make the first few pages freely available while requiring a Lead Gen Form to be filled out to see the rest.

This approach is especially effective for the following content:

- Industry reports and research summaries
- Comprehensive checklists and templates
- Pricing or return-on-investment calculation guides

**Practical tips:**

## How do you measure the success of a document post?

Looking only at the number of likes is misleading. Evaluate the following metrics together for document posts:

- **Impressions and reach:** How many people the post reached.
- **Engagement rate:** The ratio of likes, comments, shares and clicks to impressions.
- **Comment quality:** Are the commenters part of your target audience? Do their job titles and companies match your ideal customer profile?
- **Saves and shares:** An indicator that the content has lasting value.
- **Follower growth:** New followers gained by the company page after the post.
- **Profile and page visits:** Whether the document sparked curiosity.
- **Sales signals:** Connection requests, direct messages and demo requests received after the post.

Compare this data across a series of at least 6-8 documents rather than individual posts. Which topic, which cover type, which page count and which publishing day deliver better results? Patterns only become visible with enough data. To interpret company page data correctly, refer to our [guide to reading LinkedIn page analytics](/blog/how-to-read-linkedin-page-analytics).

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Uploading presentation slides as they are:** Landscape, text-heavy sales slides are unreadable on mobile and come across as advertising.
- **No promise on the cover:** Titles like "Q3 Update" don't persuade anyone to swipe.
- **Too much text per page:** Readers drop off on the second page.
- **Opening with the product:** The first pages should focus on the reader's problem, and the last page on your solution.
- **Inconsistent design:** A different font and color on every page undermines the perception of professionalism.
- **Missing call to action:** Failing to guide the engaged reader who made it to the end wastes your most valuable moment.
- **Publish and forget:** Not responding to comments in the first hours prevents the discussion and reach from growing.
- **One-off attempts:** Abandoning the format entirely when a single document doesn't get the expected attention. Try again with a different topic, hook and structure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a LinkedIn document post the same as a carousel?

In practice, yes. Most of the swipeable posts known as "carousels" on LinkedIn are document posts uploaded as PDFs. On the advertising side, there is also a separate format called "Carousel Ads," a sponsored format in which each card carries its own image and link.

### What is the best size for a document post?

The 4:5 portrait format (1080×1350 pixels), which takes up the most space in the mobile feed, is generally the best choice. The square format (1080×1080 pixels) is also a safe option. Landscape slides appear small on mobile and reduce readability.

### Is it better to share from the company page or a personal profile?

Using both together delivers the best results. The company page serves as the official source of the content, while shares by employees and executives with their own commentary reach their personal networks. Personal profiles generally achieve higher organic reach.

### How many document posts should I share per week?

For most B2B companies, one document post a week or every two weeks is a sustainable rhythm. Documents are labor-intensive content; maintaining quality matters more than frequency. You can balance the days in between with text, image and video posts.

## Conclusion

LinkedIn document posts are one of the most efficient ways for B2B companies to make their expertise visible. The key to success isn't complicated: choose a single problem, create a cover with a clear promise, put one idea on each page, design for mobile and guide the reader to the next step with a clear call to action. Publish your first document even if it isn't perfect, track the data and improve one variable with each new document.
