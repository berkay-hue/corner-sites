**Choose your LinkedIn posting language based on the language your target customers use to research and make purchasing decisions.** For B2B companies whose customers are mostly in Türkiye, Turkish should be the primary language. For companies that sell into international markets or are looking for global investors and partners, English takes priority. For companies serving both markets, the best results usually come from a planned mixed-language strategy.

This decision is more strategic than it looks. The wrong language choice lowers engagement, spends your ad budget on the wrong audience and blurs who your company is "talking to." In this article, we walk step by step through the pros and cons of posting in Turkish and in English, a framework to make the decision easier, mixed-language models, language choice in ads and how to measure which language is working.

## The short answer: What language does your audience buy in?

Many companies approach this question with the idea that "LinkedIn is a professional platform, so English looks more prestigious." Perceived prestige matters, but on its own it isn't a strategy. The real question is this: **In which language does the decision-maker understand your content fastest, trust it most and take action?**

Three typical scenarios:

- **A company selling to the local market:** An industrial equipment supplier earns nearly all its revenue from factories in Türkiye. The decision-makers are procurement managers, production directors and business owners. Posting in English would shrink this company's audience and create distance. Its primary language should be Turkish.
- **An export-focused company:** A software company has most of its customers in Europe and the Gulf countries. Turkish posts speak to the team and the local ecosystem but don't directly feed the sales funnel. Its primary language should be English.
- **A hybrid company:** A consulting firm serves both holding groups in Türkiye and multinational companies across the region. This company needs to use both languages in a deliberate ratio.

The fastest way to clarify your own situation is to look at your CRM. Over the last 12 months, what percentage of your opportunities and closed deals came from Türkiye, and what percentage from abroad? That ratio is the first draft of your language strategy.

## What are the advantages of posting in Turkish?

Turkish content often performs better than expected in the local market. The main advantages:

- **Lower reading barrier:** Even professionals with good English pick up content in their native language more easily while scrolling quickly through their feed. On LinkedIn, attention is won or lost in a window of a few seconds.
- **More personal tone and more comments:** People are more willing to write comments in their native language. Comments are one of the most valuable signals affecting distribution on LinkedIn.
- **Local context:** Topics such as VAT regulations, incentive programs, local trade fairs, industry associations or supply chain issues specific to Türkiye feel much more natural in Turkish content.
- **Less competition:** With English LinkedIn content, you compete with thousands of companies from all over the world. With Turkish content, the pool of competitors is much smaller, and becoming an authority on a niche topic is easier.
- **Recruitment and employer branding:** If you're looking for talent in Türkiye, Turkish content builds a warmer connection with candidates.

The limitation of posting in Turkish is also clear: potential customers, investors and partners abroad either never see your content or have to rely on LinkedIn's automatic translation feature. Automatic translation frequently loses meaning with technical terms and humorous expressions.

## What are the advantages of posting in English?

For the right company, English content is a serious growth lever:

- **Global reach:** Your potential audience isn't limited to a single country. For companies that export or want to export, this is decisive.
- **International credibility:** When a buyer from abroad researches you, seeing regular, high-quality English content on your company page sends the message that "this company is ready to work internationally."
- **Investor and partner relations:** For startups seeking investment or manufacturers looking for global distributors, English content serves as a showcase.
- **Decision-makers at multinational companies:** At some multinational companies in Türkiye, English is the language of internal communication. For this audience, English content doesn't feel out of place and is sometimes even received more naturally.

The risk of English is this: for your local audience, the content can create a "this isn't for us" feeling. On top of that, artificial English that reads like a translation can do more damage than not posting in English at all. A global audience compares your content with other global content, which raises the quality bar.

## What does the LinkedIn algorithm think about language?

LinkedIn tries to show users content they can understand. Although it doesn't publicly disclose in detail which signals the algorithm uses, the following can be observed in practice:

- **The first wave of engagement is decisive.** A post is first shown to a small portion of your network. If this audience doesn't understand the content and doesn't engage with it, the post doesn't reach a wider audience. If most of your followers speak Turkish, it's natural for an English post to underperform in that first wave.
- **Follower composition matters.** Content resonates most with the countries and languages your company page followers come from.
- **Automatic translation exists, but it's no miracle.** LinkedIn offers a "See translation" option on some posts. That means an extra click and a risk of lost meaning.

For a more comprehensive framework on how the algorithm distributes content, take a look at [our guide to how the LinkedIn algorithm works for B2B companies](/blog/how-linkedin-algorithm-works-b2b-guide). As far as the language decision goes, the summary is this: **The algorithm evaluates you based on your existing audience.** Changing the language without changing your audience usually results in low reach.

## A 5-question decision framework for choosing the right language

Answer the following five questions together with your management team. For each question, score a point in favor of either "Turkish" or "English."

1. **Where does most of your revenue and sales opportunities come from?** Look at your CRM data. For example, if 80 percent of your opportunities are in Türkiye, this question clearly points toward Turkish.
2. **Where is your growth target for the next 12-24 months?** If you sell locally today but your new year plan aims to enter the German or UK market, you need to start laying the groundwork for English content now.
3. **Who follows your company page?** Review the location, seniority and industry breakdown of your followers in your page analytics. If most of your followers are in Türkiye, English content will get low reach in the short term.
4. **Who will produce the content, and in which language can they write well?** If no one on the team can write fluent, natural English, low-quality English content will hurt the brand. Resource planning is part of the strategy.
5. **What is the primary goal of the content?** Lead generation, employer branding, investor relations and thought leadership may call for different languages. Recruitment content might be in Turkish, while investor communications are in English.

If four or five of the five questions point in the same direction, your primary language is clear. If the scores are split, you'll need to choose one of the mixed models in the next section.

## Mixed-language strategy: Which model fits you?

There are four common models for companies serving two markets at once. Each has a different use case.

### 1. Two languages in a single post

The same post contains the Turkish text first, followed by the English text (or vice versa). There is a single visual.

- **Advantage:** You reach two audiences with one post, and engagement is concentrated on a single post.
- **Disadvantage:** The text gets longer. The first lines visible in the feed before "see more" serve only one language. Readers have to scroll to find their own language.
- **When to use it:** For short, information-focused posts such as corporate announcements, award news, trade fair participation and partnership announcements.

Tip: In this model, separate the two languages with a clear divider, and choose which language comes first in the opening line based on your primary audience.

### 2. Alternating posts (ratio model)

Some posts are published in Turkish and others in English. The ratio is determined by your revenue and target distribution.

- **Example:** A company publishing 3 posts per week with a mostly local market might plan 2 Turkish posts and 1 English post. That's roughly a 70/30 split.
- **Advantage:** Each post speaks clearly to a single audience, and the text stays short.
- **Disadvantage:** Both audiences see posts in their feed that aren't aimed at them. It requires content calendar discipline.

The way to make this model sustainable is to build the language split into your calendar from the start. Adding a "language" column to the template described in [our guide to creating a LinkedIn content calendar for B2B companies](/blog/b2b-linkedin-content-calendar) keeps the ratio from drifting.

### 3. Market-specific Showcase Pages

On LinkedIn, you can create Showcase Pages linked to your main company page. Some companies use these pages for a specific market, product line or language.

- **Advantage:** Each page speaks entirely to its own audience. Follower bases and analytics are kept separate.
- **Disadvantage:** Feeding two separate pages means twice the content and management workload. Because the follower base is split, each page may grow more slowly.
- **When to use it:** For mid-sized and large companies whose international operations function like a separate business unit and that have the resources to produce content regularly.

You can also set your company page's name, tagline and description in more than one language. This localizes your page information rather than your posts, but it creates a good first impression for someone visiting your page from abroad.

### 4. Language split by person

The company page posts in the primary language. Executives and sales team members responsible for international markets produce English content from their own profiles.

- **Advantage:** The corporate page stays consistent. The global audience is reached through personal profiles, which are the strongest channel.
- **Disadvantage:** It depends on individuals producing content regularly.
- **When to use it:** For companies with an export team, or whose founder or senior executives have international visibility.

In practice, the most effective model for many B2B companies is **a combination of alternating posts and a language split by person.**

## Company page or employee profiles?

The language decision isn't made for the company page alone. On LinkedIn, personal profiles often get more engagement than company pages. That's why you need to think about your language strategy together with employee posting.

A concrete example of how this might be split:

- **Company page:** Mostly Turkish. Bilingual for corporate announcements when needed.
- **General manager / founder:** Mixed, depending on the target market. Industry vision and thought leadership content can be in English.
- **Export and international sales team:** English. Customer visits, trade fair notes and industry observations.
- **Local sales team:** Turkish. Customer problems, short case stories and product usage tips.
- **HR and employer branding:** Turkish.

This structure allows each person to reach the audience they naturally speak to in that audience's language. We covered how to involve your employees in this process in detail in [our article on LinkedIn employee advocacy programs for B2B companies](/blog/b2b-employee-advocacy-linkedin).

Keep in mind: language consistency matters on personal profiles too. If a sales rep's profile headline and "About" section are in English but all of their posts are in Turkish, a visitor from abroad will be confused. LinkedIn also lets you create an additional version of your profile in a second language. Team members who sell internationally should consider using this feature.

## How should you choose the language for LinkedIn ads?

There's flexibility in organic content. In advertising, however, choosing the wrong language means directly losing budget. Points to watch:

- **Profile language is selected when setting up a campaign.** In LinkedIn Campaign Manager, the target audience is filtered by the profile language members use in the LinkedIn interface. This doesn't mean "everyone in Türkiye uses the Turkish interface." Many professionals in Türkiye use LinkedIn with the English interface. Selecting only Turkish as the profile language can leave these people out of your campaign.
- **Location and language are separate decisions.** The audience "located in Türkiye with English as their profile language" and the audience "located in Türkiye with Turkish as their profile language" can be tested in separate campaigns.
- **The ad copy language should match the landing page language.** Sending people from an English ad to a Turkish landing page or a Turkish lead form lowers conversion.
- **Include language in your A/B tests.** Testing the same offer in two languages, with similar budgets and timeframes, gives you data specific to your own audience.

## Localization, not translation: How should you write English content?

The most common mistake is translating a post written in Turkish into English word for word. The result may be grammatically correct, but it reads as artificial. Instead, follow these steps:

1. **Rewrite the message, don't translate it.** First answer the question "What's the main idea of this post?", then express that idea from scratch in the target language.
2. **Localize your examples.** For a Turkish reader, "a production plant in an Organized Industrial Zone (OSB)" is a good example. For a German reader, the same example needs context. Change the example if necessary.
3. **Adapt currencies and units.** Turkish lira makes more sense in local content, while euros or dollars make more sense in global content. Pay attention to date and number formats too.
4. **Avoid stock phrases.** Formulas like "Dear valued followers" or "We are proud to announce" sound clichéd in English as well. Write directly and concretely.
5. **Use AI tools for drafts, but let a human produce the final version.** AI speeds up translation and localization. However, industry terminology, brand tone and cultural nuances must always be checked by a fluent human editor.
6. **Keep technical terms consistent.** Create a mini bilingual glossary for product names, feature names and industry terms. That way, content written by different people stays consistent.

The same applies in reverse. When adapting English campaign content from a global headquarters into Turkish, localize rather than translate. Leaving words like "leverage" and "synergy" as they are in Turkish text can create distance with local decision-makers.

## How do you measure which language works?

A theoretical framework gets you to the right starting point. But your own data provides the final answer. A simple test plan:

1. **Set the duration:** At least 8-12 weeks. Shorter periods are vulnerable to individual viral posts skewing the results.
2. **Hold variables constant:** Posts in both languages should share a similar format (e.g., both carousels), similar topics and similar posting times. Otherwise, you'll end up measuring the format, not the language.
3. **Tag your posts:** Mark each post in your tracking sheet as "TR," "EN" or "TR+EN."
4. **Track the right metrics:**
   - Engagement rate per impression
   - Number and quality of comments (are they coming from relevant decision-makers?)
   - Follower growth after posting and the location and seniority breakdown of new followers
   - Clicks to your website and, if you can measure them, conversions on the site
   - Incoming messages, demo requests and feedback reaching the sales team
5. **Compare and decide:** At the end of the test, compare the average performance of each language. Give more weight to metrics close to business outcomes, not just the number of likes.

For example, if your English posts get less engagement but are the source of demo requests from abroad, the lower engagement is an acceptable cost. Conversely, if your Turkish posts get high engagement but that engagement comes from people outside your target audience, the situation should be re-evaluated.

## Common mistakes

The traps companies fall into most often with their language strategy:

- **Posting in English for prestige:** A company with a local audience switching to English "to look more corporate" usually ends up losing reach and engagement.
- **Unplanned language mixing:** Turkish one week, English the next, then a half-baked mix of both languages in a single post. The audience can't tell who the company is talking to.
- **Poor-quality English:** Grammar mistakes and artificial phrasing undermine trust with a global audience. If you don't have the resources, it's better to cut back on English content.
- **Forgetting the text in visuals:** The post copy may be in English while the text on the visual is in Turkish. In carousels and infographics, the language of the copy and the visual should match.
- **Hashtag and keyword mismatch:** English hashtags on a Turkish post, or vice versa, make it harder for the content to reach the right audience.
- **Replying to comments in the wrong language:** Reply to users in the language they commented in. It's a small detail, but it strengthens the relationship.
- **Neglecting page information:** If your posts are in English but your company page description is only in Turkish, visitors from abroad get lost at the very first step.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does posting in Turkish on LinkedIn reduce reach?

No, posting in Turkish doesn't reduce reach on its own. LinkedIn first shows content to your existing network. If your followers and connections are mostly in Türkiye, Turkish content generally gets more engagement and wider distribution. What determines reach isn't the language but how well the content matches your audience.

### Is it a good idea to write in both Turkish and English in the same post?

A single bilingual post works for short, information-focused posts (corporate announcements, event participation, awards). It isn't recommended for long thought leadership content, however, because it makes the text much longer and forces readers to scroll to find their own language. For this kind of content, alternating posts or a language split by person is more effective.

### Should an exporting company switch entirely to English?

If most of your revenue and target market are abroad, English can be your primary language. However, dropping Turkish content entirely can weaken your employer brand among employees, talent candidates and local suppliers in Türkiye. For most exporters, a mixed model that is primarily English with a certain share of Turkish content delivers more balanced results.

### How long does it take to know which language works better?

For a meaningful comparison, a test of at least 8-12 weeks with format and posting time held constant is recommended. During this period, by publishing a sufficient number of posts in each language, you can compare metrics such as engagement rate, new follower profile, website clicks and sales inquiries. Shorter tests are vulnerable to individual successful posts skewing the results.

## Conclusion

There's no universally right answer to whether you should post on LinkedIn in Turkish or English. But there is a clear way to decide: **Look at where your customers are, where your growth target points, who makes up your follower base and in which language you can produce high-quality content.** If you sell to the local market, choose Turkish; if you're targeting global customers, choose English; if you serve both markets, choose a planned mixed model. Then validate that decision with your own data through an 8-12 week test.

Language isn't a detail of your content strategy; it's a declaration of who you're talking to. When it's set up correctly, it both strengthens trust in the local market and opens the door to new markets.
