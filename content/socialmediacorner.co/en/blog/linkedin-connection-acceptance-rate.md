The most effective way to increase your LinkedIn connection request acceptance rate is to send the request to the right person, from a profile that inspires trust, with personalized context. Three main factors determine your acceptance rate: **targeting quality**, **profile credibility** and **the reason behind the request**. Engaging with a person's content before you send the request, writing a short note that doesn't sound salesy and measuring results weekly will noticeably raise most B2B teams' acceptance rates within a few weeks.

Many sales and marketing teams use LinkedIn like a "mass invitation machine." They send requests to hundreds of people using the same template, then conclude that the channel doesn't work when results are poor. But the problem isn't the channel. It's the approach. In this post, we'll walk you through how to calculate your acceptance rate, which steps will raise it, which mistakes are holding you back and how to turn accepted connections into sales opportunities, with concrete examples.

## What is the acceptance rate and how is it calculated?

The acceptance rate shows what percentage of the connection requests you sent during a given period were accepted. The calculation is simple:

**Acceptance rate = (Number of accepted requests ÷ Number of sent requests) × 100**

For example, say you sent 80 requests in one week and 28 of them were accepted. Your acceptance rate is 35%.

There are two things to keep in mind:

- **Fix your measurement window.** People may not accept requests right away; some respond a week or two later. For a fair comparison, evaluate each week's requests 14 days after they were sent.
- **Measure by segment.** A single overall rate is misleading. When you track segments such as "procurement managers," "CFOs" and "operations directors" separately, you can see which audience is warmer toward you.

## What is a good acceptance rate?

There's no universal "right number," because it varies by industry, target job title and the size of your network. Still, we can suggest a practical framework for interpreting your results:

- **Below 20%:** A sign of a serious problem with targeting or your profile. Before looking at your messaging, look at who you're sending requests to.
- **Between 20% and 40%:** A reasonable range for an average B2B effort, but with room for improvement.
- **Above 40%:** Your targeting and warm-up process are working well. At this point, you can shift your focus to the conversation after acceptance.

What really matters is less a fixed target and more **establishing your own baseline and tracking the trend**. Use the average of your first two weeks as a reference, then measure how each change affects that average.

## Is your profile ready before you send requests?

The first thing someone does after receiving a connection request is look at your profile. That glance of a few seconds largely determines whether they'll accept. Your profile needs to quickly answer these questions for the other person: Who is this, what do they do and why might they matter to me?

Key elements to check:

1. **Profile photo:** A clear, professional photo in which your face is plainly visible. A logo, landscape or blank avatar instantly undermines trust.
2. **Headline:** Instead of just writing "Sales Manager," explain who you add value for and how. For example: "Helping manufacturers digitize their procurement processes | X Software".
3. **Cover image:** A simple image that conveys your company or area of expertise.
4. **About section:** A customer-focused text made up of short paragraphs, ending with how people can get in touch.
5. **Recent activity:** If your profile shows no posts or comments over the past few weeks, it creates the impression of a "passive salesperson."

## Who should you send connection requests to?

The most common reason for a low acceptance rate is poor targeting. A broad list such as "all general managers in Türkiye" will underperform no matter how good your note is.

### Clarify your ideal customer profile

Before sending requests, answer these questions:

- Which industries do the companies we work with operate in?
- What is the employee count range of these companies?
- Which job titles are involved in the decision-making process? (Decision-maker, influencer, user)
- Which geographies are our priority?

For example, for a company selling HR software, the target audience might be defined as "HR directors, HR managers and HR business partners at companies with 50–500 employees in the services and manufacturing sectors." This clarity improves both your acceptance rate and the quality of the conversation after acceptance.

### Prioritize people you have something in common with

People more readily accept requests from those they know or share some context with. Move people with these signals to the top of your list:

- **Mutual connections:** Especially if you share several mutual connections from the same industry.
- **Same group or event:** Being in the same LinkedIn group, or attending the same trade show or webinar.
- **Recent activity:** People who have posted in the last 30 days are actively using LinkedIn and are more likely to see your request.
- **Content engagement:** People who have liked or commented on your posts or your company's posts.
- **Profile visits:** People who have viewed your profile are already curious about you.

## How do you warm up before sending a connection request?

To the recipient, a cold request is a random invitation from an unfamiliar name. The warm-up process breaks down this "stranger" perception. Getting on someone's radar before sending your request significantly increases the chances they'll accept.

A practical 7-day warm-up sequence could look like this:

1. **Day 1:** Visit the person's profile. LinkedIn may notify them, and they'll see your name for the first time.
2. **Day 2 or 3:** Like one of their recent posts.
3. **Day 4 or 5:** Leave a meaningful comment on the same post or another one. Not "Great post," but two or three sentences that add to the discussion.
4. **Day 6 or 7:** Send the connection request with a short note referencing your comment or their post.

This approach may not be feasible for everyone; warming up all 100 requests a week this way isn't realistic for most teams. That's why you should split your list into **A and B priorities**. Put the A list (your 20–30 most valuable prospects) through the full warm-up process, while a profile visit and a personalized note are enough for the B list.

Another powerful dimension of warming up is your own content. If you regularly share useful posts about your industry, the person receiving your request will see a valuable content stream on your profile. ## Should you add a note to your connection request?

This is one of the most debated topics on sales teams. The short answer: **It depends on the goal and context, but don't decide without testing.**

Advantages of adding a note:
- It provides context and answers the question, "Why is this person sending me a request?"
- It keeps the warmth going by referencing your engagement during the warm-up process.

Disadvantages of adding a note:
- A poorly written note does more harm than a request with no note at all. A salesy note is an instant reason for rejection.
- LinkedIn may limit the number of personalized notes on free accounts, so it can make sense to save notes for your most valuable prospects.

Our recommendation: Within the same segment, send half your requests with a note and half without for two weeks, then compare the results. Data from your own audience is always more valuable than general advice.

### How do you write a good connection note?

A good note has these four qualities:

- **Short:** 200–300 characters is enough. Keep it to two or three sentences.
- **Personalized:** It should include real context, such as something in common, a post or an event.
- **Sales-free:** No product mentions, demos or meeting requests.
- **Focused on the other person:** Centered on "you," not "I."

### Sample connection notes

**After content engagement:**
> "Hi Ayşe, I commented on your post about transparency in the supply chain. I'd love to follow your perspective on this topic and would be glad to connect."

**Shared event:**
> "Hi Mehmet, last week I watched the panel you took part in at the logistics summit in Istanbul. Your insights on warehouse automation were spot on. I'd like to stay in touch."

**Mutual connection:**
> "Hi Zeynep, I saw that Can is one of our mutual connections. I also work on customer success processes at SaaS companies. I'd love to connect our networks."

**Career change:**
> "Hi Emre, I saw your new role, congratulations. I've been following your posts on the digital transformation of finance teams with interest. I'd love to connect."

### A note example to avoid

> "Hi, I'm a sales manager at Company X. Our solution reduces companies' costs. Could we set up a 15-minute call?"

This note asks for the person's time before they even know you. At the connection stage, the goal isn't to sell but **to get permission**. The sales conversation belongs after acceptance.

## Which mistakes lower your acceptance rate?

Dropping the habits that drag your acceptance rate down is just as important as raising it. These are the mistakes we see most often:

- **Sending in bulk with the same template:** People easily spot a copy-and-paste note.
- **Sending requests directly to very senior people:** Sending a request to the CEO of a holding company with no warm-up usually goes unanswered. For these people, become visible first through content engagement.
- **Selling in the note:** Phrases like pricing, product features or demo invitations.
- **Incomplete profile:** A profile with no photo, an empty headline or no visible activity for months.
- **Irrelevant targeting:** Sending requests to job titles that have no connection to your product.
- **Letting unaccepted requests pile up:** Hundreds of long-pending requests pose a risk to your account's health.
- **Using automation tools aggressively:** Sending hundreds of requests a day with tools that violate LinkedIn's terms of use can lead to your account being restricted.

## When and how often should you send requests?

### Weekly volume

LinkedIn places a cap on the number of invitations you can send per week. Although the platform doesn't share an exact number, this limit has been observed to vary based on account history and acceptance rate. For a safe and sustainable approach:

- **Spread your weekly target across days.** For example, if you're aiming for 100 requests a week, sending 20 a day on weekdays looks more natural than sending 100 in a single day.
- **Put quality ahead of volume.** Sending 200 requests at a 15% acceptance rate brings 30 connections; sending 80 requests at a 45% acceptance rate brings 36. The second scenario is both more efficient and safer for your account.

### Timing

Your request is more likely to be seen during the hours when the person is active on LinkedIn. B2B professionals generally check the platform more on weekdays, at the start of the workday and during lunch breaks. To find the best time window for your own audience, you can adapt the approach from our post on [the best time and day to post on LinkedIn](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-linkedin-b2b) to connection requests as well. Use weekly tests to compare different time slots, such as "Tuesday morning" and "Thursday afternoon."

## How do you manage pending requests?

Unaccepted requests quietly pile up, and most teams forget about them. Yet a high number of pending requests both muddies your acceptance rate measurement and can negatively affect your account health.

A simple management routine:

1. **Check your pending requests every week.** You can find sent invitations in the "My Network" section on LinkedIn.
2. **Withdraw requests older than 3–4 weeks.** After that point, the likelihood of acceptance drops considerably.
3. **Add the people you withdrew to a "warm-up list."** Engage with their content first. LinkedIn doesn't let you resend a withdrawn invitation to the same person for a certain period (about three weeks); use that time to warm them up.
4. **Keep your total number of pending requests below a set level.** For example, you could make it an internal team rule not to exceed 300–400 pending requests per person.

## How do you measure and improve your acceptance rate?

You can't improve what you don't measure. A simple weekly tracking sheet lets you see clearly which changes are working.

Your sheet should include these columns:

- Week
- Segment (job title / industry)
- Number of requests sent
- Note included? (Yes/No)
- Warm-up done? (Yes/No)
- Number of accepted requests (after 14 days)
- Acceptance rate
- Number of people who replied after accepting
- Number of people who converted to a meeting

A sample reading: If, over three weeks in the "operations directors" segment, requests sent without a warm-up had a 22% acceptance rate while warmed-up requests rose to 41%, you'll know that warming up is worthwhile for this segment. Decisions like these are based on your own data rather than guesswork.

**Test only one variable at a time.** If you change the note, targeting and send time all in the same week, you won't know which change drove the improvement. ## What should you do after your connection is accepted?

A high acceptance rate on its own isn't a measure of success. The real goal is to turn these connections into meaningful conversations and, ultimately, sales opportunities.

Our recommended post-acceptance sequence:

1. **Thank-you message (same day or the next day):** Short and sales-free. Something like, "Thanks for accepting my connection request, I'll keep following your posts."
2. **Share value (within 1–2 weeks):** Share a report, article or industry insight that matches the person's interests. Again, no selling, just value.
3. **Conversation-starter question:** A question that reflects genuine curiosity about their work. Something like, "Which process is taking up the most time for your team this year?"
4. **Meeting proposal:** Only after the other person has shown interest, move forward with a low-commitment proposal.

## How do you manage acceptance rates at the team level?

A method that works for a single salesperson can create new problems when scaled to a team. Multiple salespeople sending requests to the same company, or inconsistent messaging, can damage your brand perception.

Things to watch for in team management:

- **Account mapping:** Determine which salesperson is responsible for each target company. Make sure the same person doesn't receive requests from two different salespeople.
- **Shared note library:** Create note templates tested by segment, but require every salesperson to personalize them.
- **Profile standards:** Everyone on the team should have a consistent company description, cover image and headline structure on their profile.
- **Weekly metric sharing:** Discuss acceptance rates in team meetings and share the best-performing approaches with the team.
- **Content support:** Provide salespeople with shareable content so their profiles look "active."

## Frequently asked questions

### Does adding a note to a LinkedIn connection request increase the acceptance rate?

A well-written, personalized and sales-free note generally has a positive effect on acceptance rate. However, a note that looks like a template or asks directly for a meeting can produce worse results than a request with no note. The best approach is to test requests with and without notes on your own target audience for a few weeks.

### How many LinkedIn connection requests should I send per week?

LinkedIn applies a weekly cap on invitations, and this limit can vary from account to account. For most B2B salespeople, 50–100 well-targeted requests a week, spread across weekdays, is a sustainable starting point. Before increasing volume, make sure your acceptance rate is at a healthy level.

### Should I withdraw connection requests that haven't been accepted?

Yes, regularly withdrawing requests older than 3–4 weeks is good practice. It clarifies your measurements and prevents pending requests from piling up excessively. You can warm up the people you withdrew through content engagement and send them a new request later.

### If my acceptance rate is low, what should I change first?

Check your targeting first, because no note can save requests sent to the wrong people. Then see whether your profile inspires trust in terms of photo, headline and recent activity. Once these two elements are fixed, move on to testing your note copy and warm-up process.

## Conclusion

Your LinkedIn connection request acceptance rate isn't a matter of luck; it can be managed directly through targeting, your profile, warm-up, note copy and measurement discipline. Choosing the right people, becoming visible before sending a request, offering context without selling and tracking results every week turns a low-converting invitation process into a sustainable social selling channel. Remember, every accepted request is a beginning; the real value emerges in the quality of the relationships you build with these connections.
