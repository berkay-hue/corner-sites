The LinkedIn algorithm first shows a post to a small audience. It then looks at how that audience responds, the quality of the content and the relationship between the author and the viewer to decide whether to push the post out to a wider audience. In short, LinkedIn is trying to answer one question: **"Will this content spark a meaningful conversation for this person?"** It rewards the quality of comments, reading time and how well the content aligns with the author's subject-matter expertise far more than the number of likes.

For B2B companies, the practical takeaway is this: posting more does not mean reaching more people. When you deliver content to the right people, in the right format, that genuinely sparks discussion, the algorithm works for you. In this guide, we explain the stages the LinkedIn algorithm goes through, which signals it weighs most heavily and which behaviours it penalises. We then walk through a step-by-step posting process, with concrete examples, that B2B teams can start using tomorrow morning.

## How does the LinkedIn algorithm work, in brief?

LinkedIn does not publicly disclose every detail of its algorithm. However, statements from the platform's engineering teams and years of observation across the industry suggest that the feed generally works in a few stages.

### Stage 1: Initial quality classification

When a post is published, the system evaluates it quickly. The post is placed into one of three groups:

- **Spam:** Excessive tagging, suspicious links, repetitive content or boilerplate phrasing.
- **Low quality:** It doesn't break any rules, but its engagement potential looks weak.
- **Clear/high quality:** Suitable for distribution in the feed.

At this stage, grammar, formatting, the number of people tagged and the account's past behaviour all play a role. A profile that has been posting consistently high-quality content for years passes this filter more easily.

### Stage 2: Test audience

A post that passes the filter is first shown to a limited audience. This is usually a portion of your first-degree connections and followers. In the industry, this period is often referred to as the "golden hour". No exact time frame has been officially announced, but it is a widely accepted observation that reactions in the first few hours are decisive.

### Stage 3: Expansion and ranking

If the test audience engages meaningfully with the post, the content is opened up to a wider audience. If people are reading, commenting, sharing or saving it, the post reaches second- and third-degree connections, as well as people who follow related topics. At this stage, the algorithm ranks content separately for each user. The same post might sit near the top of a procurement manager's feed while never appearing in a software developer's feed at all.

### Stage 4: Long shelf life

Posts on LinkedIn have a longer lifespan than on many other platforms. A well-performing piece of content can keep appearing in feeds days after it was published. That's why numbers that look low on day one can recover over the following days if a good discussion has started.

## Which signals does the algorithm look at?

We can group the signals that drive the algorithm's decisions into three main categories: **relationship**, **interest** and **engagement quality**.

### Relationship: Who sees whose content?

LinkedIn prioritises content in a user's feed from people they have a close relationship with. These are people they message frequently, whose content they comment on or who work at the same company. From a B2B perspective, the implication is clear: if you don't have a genuine relationship with people in your ideal customer profile, your content won't easily reach them. Rather than growing your network at random, deliberately building connections with your target audience is also the soundest investment from the algorithm's point of view.

### Interest: Subject-matter expertise and consistency

In recent years, LinkedIn has openly stated that it gives more visibility to content that offers "knowledge and advice". The algorithm looks at two things: Is the author qualified to speak on this topic, and is the viewer interested in it?

For example, if the operations director of a logistics software company regularly writes about warehouse efficiency, the system associates them with that topic. When the same person one day shares a completely unrelated viral post, that post usually underperforms. That's why **staying consistent around 2-3 core topic pillars** is the most effective way to teach the algorithm what you talk about.

### Engagement quality: Not all engagement is equal

In the algorithm's eyes, the weight of different engagement types ranks roughly as follows:

1. **Meaningful comments:** Comments of a few sentences that add to the topic and start a two-way dialogue. One-word comments like "Great post" carry far less value.
2. **Saves and sends (sharing via DM):** Strong indicators that the user found the content valuable.
3. **Reposts with commentary:** Sharing with your own commentary added is more valuable than a plain repost.
4. **Reactions (likes, etc.):** They generate a signal, but on their own it's weak.
5. **Dwell time:** How much time the user spends on the post. Expanding a long text with "see more" and reading it, or flipping through the pages of a document post, strengthens this signal.

In B2B content, the target audience is generally more reluctant to write comments. A CFO might read and save your content but never comment. That's why dwell time and save signals are the invisible but critical fuel of B2B posts.

## Which gets more reach: a personal profile or a company page?

In practice, personal profiles get far more organic reach than company pages. This is because of the weight the algorithm places on relationship signals. People prefer talking to people, not brands, and LinkedIn rewards this behaviour.

That doesn't mean the company page is unnecessary. The right setup looks like this:

- **Company page:** Corporate announcements, case studies, recruitment content, product updates and the foundation for advertising.
- **Executive and expert profiles:** Opinions, experience, industry commentary and stories from the field.
- **Employees:** A distribution network that amplifies company content by enriching it with their own commentary.

To make this trio systematic, take a look at our article on [how to structure employee advocacy on LinkedIn at B2B companies](/blog/b2b-employee-advocacy-linkedin). One important caveat: employees copying and pasting the same text is a weak signal for both the algorithm and the reader. When each employee adds a sentence or two from their own perspective, the impact multiplies.

## Which content formats stand out in B2B?

There is no such thing as "the best format, always" on LinkedIn. The algorithm doesn't reward the format; it rewards the engagement that format generates. That said, certain formats are frequently observed to stand out in a B2B context.

### Document (PDF carousel) posts

Swipeable PDF posts naturally increase dwell time because they move the user forward page by page. In B2B, they're ideal for checklists, process diagrams, "in 5 steps" guides and data visualisations. Keeping the page count between 6 and 12 makes it easier for readers to get to the end.

### Long-form text posts

LinkedIn posts have a limit of roughly 3,000 characters, and well-written long-form text is still very powerful. The critical part is the first two or three lines before the "see more" cut-off. If those lines don't spark curiosity, the chances of the reader seeing the rest are low.

### Short video

60-90 second videos of an executive speaking directly to camera are effective for building trust. Because a large share of B2B viewers watch videos on mute, **captions are a must**.

### Polls

Polls generate easy engagement, but they should be used carefully. Constantly posting polls tires out followers and lowers the quality of engagement. Use polls as a research tool, and interpret the results in a separate post.

### Newsletters and articles

Because a newsletter reaches subscribers via notifications and email, it offers a distribution channel that is partly independent of the algorithm's feed ranking. It's a powerful tool for long-term thought leadership. For setup steps, see our [guide to launching a LinkedIn Newsletter for B2B companies](/blog/how-to-start-linkedin-newsletter-b2b).

## Does sharing external links reduce reach?

This is one of the questions B2B marketers ask most often. LinkedIn doesn't officially say "we penalise it". Still, many marketing teams observe that external links added to the post text generally reduce reach. The logic is easy to understand: the platform wants users to stay on LinkedIn.

Practical solutions:

- **Deliver the content within the post.** Summarise the key ideas of your blog post in the post itself, so the link only serves as a "read more" option.
- **Test putting the link in the first comment.** It works for some accounts and makes no difference for others. Decide based on your own data.
- **Compare posts with and without links.** Comparing two posts on similar topics within the same month gives more reliable results than general advice.

If driving traffic to an external link really is your goal, don't forget to use UTM parameters to measure that traffic accurately.

## Which behaviours does the algorithm penalise?

Here are some habits that quietly reduce your reach:

- **Irrelevant tagging:** Tagging 10-15 people who have nothing to do with the content. If the tagged people don't engage, this turns into a negative signal.
- **Engagement pods:** Groups that comment on posts in an organised, artificial way. LinkedIn has stated that it has taken steps to detect these kinds of patterns. A short-term boost can turn into a long-term loss of credibility.
- **Engagement bait like "Type YES in the comments":** It generates low-quality engagement and cheapens the brand in the reader's eyes.
- **Too many hashtags:** The impact of hashtags on feed distribution appears to have declined compared to the past. 2-3 relevant hashtags are enough.
- **Posting too frequently:** Publishing posts back to back on the same day can cause them to cannibalise each other's reach. For most B2B accounts, 2-5 posts per week is a sustainable range.
- **Heavy editing after publishing:** According to some teams' observations, editing a post repeatedly right after publishing can hurt its early performance. Fixing minor typos is fine. But doing your checks before publishing is the healthiest approach.

## A step-by-step, algorithm-friendly posting process for B2B companies

You can adapt the following process to your own team to put the theory into practice.

1. **Define your topic pillars.** Choose 2-3 core themes for your company and your executives. For an industrial automation firm, for example, these themes might be "production efficiency", "maintenance costs" and "field experience in digital transformation".
2. **Plan your weekly rhythm.** Decide in advance which format goes out on which day and from which profile. Our [guide to building a LinkedIn content calendar for B2B](/blog/b2b-linkedin-content-calendar) is a good starting point for this.
3. **Write the first two lines separately.** Once you've finished the rest of the post, spend 10 minutes on the opening lines. Start with a concrete figure, a contrarian view or a familiar problem statement.
4. **End with a question, but make it a real one.** Instead of "What do you think?", ask an easy-to-answer, specific question such as "On your team, who owns maintenance planning: operations or finance?"
5. **Publish at the right time.** Test when your target audience is active on LinkedIn using your own data. To get started, you can use the approach in our article on [the best time and day to post on LinkedIn](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-linkedin-b2b).
6. **Be present in the first hour.** Respond quickly and thoughtfully to comments in the first hour after posting. Every reply opens a new line of dialogue and strengthens the post's "conversation" signal.
7. **Comment on other people's content.** In the 15-20 minutes before and after posting, leave meaningful comments on posts from your target audience. This strengthens the relationship signal and also drives visits to your profile.
8. **Involve employees in the process, but without copy-and-paste.** Ask team members to add their own perspectives.
9. **Review after 48 hours.** Log impressions, engagement rate, number of comments and saves in a spreadsheet. See which opening line, which format and which topic worked.

## A concrete example: Same content, two different approaches

Let's imagine a B2B software company publishing a new customer case study.

**Weak approach (company page):**
"Our new case study is live! Click the link to read how we boosted our client's operational efficiency. #digitaltransformation #efficiency #software #B2B #innovation #technology"

This post has everything the algorithm dislikes. The value sits outside the post, there's an external link, there are lots of generic hashtags and there's nothing to start a conversation.

**Strong approach (product manager's personal profile, document post):**
"One of our customers' planning teams spent 4 hours every Monday updating the same Excel file. The problem wasn't a lack of software. The problem was that three different departments kept the same data in three different ways. I've summarised how we simplified the process in 8 pages. The step we struggled with most is on page 5. On your team, which task takes up the most time in weekly planning?"

The company page then reposts this with its own commentary, and two sales reps amplify the post by adding their own customer observations. The link to the detailed case study goes in the first comment.

In the second approach, the content creates value within LinkedIn, uses a format that increases dwell time, speaks with a human voice and opens a dialogue with a specific question. Almost every signal the algorithm looks for is present here.

## How do you measure algorithm performance?

The only way to know whether you're "beating" the algorithm is regular measurement. These are the core metrics you should track:

- **Impressions:** How many times the content was viewed. On its own, this can be misleading.
- **Engagement rate:** Total engagement as a ratio of impressions. A fairer way to compare posts of different sizes.
- **Comment-to-reaction ratio:** If the ratio of comments to reactions is rising, your content is starting conversations.
- **Follower demographics:** Do the job titles, industries and company sizes of the people engaging match your target audience?
- **Profile and page visits:** How many people took a closer look at you after seeing the content.

Demographic data is especially critical in B2B. A post that gets 50,000 impressions but reaches people outside your target audience is less valuable than one that gets 5,000 impressions and reaches 30 decision-makers. ## Frequently asked questions

### What does the LinkedIn algorithm reward most?

The LinkedIn algorithm rewards posts that start a meaningful conversation with their target audience and keep readers spending time on the content. Comments that add to the topic, saves and long reading times are far stronger signals than the number of likes alone. The author's consistency on a topic also increases visibility.

### Which gets more reach: a company page or an executive profile?

In organic reach, personal profiles generally perform much more strongly than company pages. This is because the algorithm prioritises person-to-person relationship signals. The best results come from a structure in which the company page serves as the corporate foundation, while executive and employee profiles amplify content in their own voices.

### How many times a week should I post on LinkedIn?

For most B2B accounts, 2-5 posts per week is a range that is both sustainable and healthy from the algorithm's perspective. Posting back to back on the same day can cause posts to reduce each other's reach. Consistency and quality matter more than frequency.

### Do hashtags still work?

The impact of hashtags on feed distribution appears to have declined significantly compared to the past. Using 2-3 relevant hashtags that clarify the topic of the post is enough. Excessive, generic hashtag use, on the other hand, can make a post look like spam.

## Conclusion

The LinkedIn algorithm is not a mysterious black box. At its core, it's a system that tries to surface conversations people genuinely find valuable. For B2B companies, the winning formula is clear: stay consistent on specific topic pillars, deliver value within the post, put personal profiles and employees at the centre of your strategy, actively manage the dialogue in the first few hours and measure everything with data. Tactics that promise shortcuts to reach may work for a while, but lasting visibility comes from content your target audience actually wants to read.
