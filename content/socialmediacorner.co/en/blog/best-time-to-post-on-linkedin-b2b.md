For B2B companies on LinkedIn, the best-performing days to post are generally **Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday**, and the most effective time slots are **08:00–10:00** and **12:00–13:30**. These windows match the times when decision-makers start their workday and check their feed over lunch. Still, the "best time" is not a universal rule. Your own audience's behavior determines the definitive answer, and you can only find it through 4–6 weeks of consistent testing.

In this article, we look at why the widely accepted time slots work. We offer a day-by-day and hour-by-hour B2B posting framework and explain how things change by industry and content format. Most importantly, we share a step-by-step testing method to help you find your own best posting time. The goal is not to hand you a single "magic hour," but to build a decision-making process your team can reuse every week.

## Why is there no single "right time" on LinkedIn?

The LinkedIn feed isn't chronological. The algorithm first shows a post to a small portion of your followers and connections. Based on how this initial group responds, it decides whether to distribute the post to a wider audience. That's why posting time matters so much: it directly affects how much engagement a post gets in its **first 60–90 minutes**.

If your target audience isn't on LinkedIn at that time, your post performs poorly in this initial test. After that, no matter how good the content is, its reach stays limited. The same post, shared when your audience is active, quickly gathers comments and reactions and then reaches second- and third-degree users as well.

The main variables that determine the "best time" are:

- **Your audience's job titles and roles:** A production manager and a marketing director don't share the same daily rhythm.
- **Industry:** Field-based teams and office-based teams log in to LinkedIn at different times.
- **Geography and time zone:** The optimal time differs for an audience in Türkiye versus one in Germany or the Gulf countries.
- **Content format:** A long document post and a 30-second video suit different moments of consumption.
- **The posting account:** A company page and an executive's personal profile have different distribution dynamics.

In other words, the widely accepted time slots are a good **starting point**, but not the final destination.

## When during the day do B2B decision-makers use LinkedIn?

LinkedIn is not a platform like Instagram, where activity peaks in the evenings and on weekends. Most B2B users treat the platform as part of their workday. If we consider the day of a typical office worker in Türkiye, LinkedIn usage concentrates around these moments:

- **07:30–09:00, commuting and starting the day:** People check their feed on public transport, on the company shuttle or over their first coffee. Mobile usage dominates.
- **09:00–10:00, the email and messages round:** Once at their desk, professionals check their emails and LinkedIn notifications. They also scroll through the feed at this point.
- **12:00–13:30, lunch break:** A more relaxed reading moment. Long-form text and document posts get read more at this time.
- **16:30–18:30, end of the day:** Meetings wrap up and the workload eases. Executives in particular return to the feed at the end of the day.

This rhythm explains why best-time recommendations cluster around morning and lunchtime. LinkedIn is also used after 21:00 and on weekends, but the audience active at these times is narrower and more specific. Founders, freelancers and job seekers, for example, stand out in this group.

## Which day of the week should you post?

Choosing the day is as important as choosing the time. Here's a general framework for the days of the week for a B2B audience:

### Monday: Planning day, scattered attention

Monday mornings are taken up by weekly meetings, reports and backlogged emails. As a result, posts published at 09:00 on Monday often underperform. If you're going to post on Monday, the **afternoon (14:00–16:00)** makes more sense. Light, quickly consumed content works well on this day: the week's agenda, a short industry note or a poll.

### Tuesday: One of the strongest days

By Tuesday, the week has settled into its rhythm and attention spans are high. Scheduling your heavier content for Tuesday is a good habit. Case studies, thought leadership pieces and product announcements suit this day. The **08:00–10:00** and **12:00–13:00** windows stand out.

### Wednesday: Ideal for engagement

Midweek is productive for content that sparks discussion. Opinion posts, industry commentary and posts ending with "What do you think?" tend to attract more comments on Wednesday.

### Thursday: Long-form content and document day

On Thursday, decision-makers start thinking about the following week. It's a good day for guide-style carousels, reports, webinar announcements and event registrations. The lunch break (**12:00–13:30**) is especially strong for long document posts.

### Friday: Lighter and more human

Work focus drops on Friday afternoon, but that isn't a disadvantage for every type of content. Team culture, behind-the-scenes content, weekly recaps and employee stories can land well on Friday morning (**09:00–11:00**). Generally, avoid posting critical announcements after 15:00 on Friday.

### Saturday and Sunday: Use them selectively

There's less competition on weekends, meaning you're up against fewer posts in the feed. On the other hand, the active audience also shrinks. For personal profiles, especially more reflective posts by founders and executives, Sunday evening (**19:00–21:00**) can sometimes deliver surprising results. For company pages, however, weekends are generally not a priority.

## An hour-by-hour B2B posting framework

The table below can serve as a starting point for a Türkiye-based B2B audience. The values in the table are not hard rules. Think of them as **hypotheses** to be tested.

| Time slot | Audience behavior | Suitable content type | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| 07:30–08:30 | Mobile, quick browsing | Short text, single image, short video | Medium |
| 08:30–10:00 | Starting the day, checking notifications | Announcements, thought leadership | High |
| 10:00–12:00 | Peak meeting time | Low priority for scheduled posts | Low |
| 12:00–13:30 | Lunch break, relaxed reading | Carousels, documents, long-form text | High |
| 13:30–16:30 | Busy working hours | Polls, short questions | Medium-Low |
| 16:30–18:30 | End of day, executive activity | Opinion posts, discussion questions | Medium-High |
| After 19:00 | Narrow but relevant audience | Personal profile posts | Depends |

In practice, a good starting point for many B2B teams might be: one main post at 08:30 on Tuesday and Thursday, and one engagement-focused post at 12:15 on Wednesday.

## Does the best time vary by industry?

Yes, and significantly. The examples below show how the same general framework can be adapted by industry.

- **Manufacturing, industrial and logistics:** In these sectors, a large share of employees work in the field or on the production line and start their day early. Posts between **07:00–08:30** may perform better than those during office hours. The lunch break also tends to be earlier (around 12:00).
- **Technology and SaaS:** This audience works more flexibly and logs in to LinkedIn more often throughout the day. The 09:00–10:00 morning and 17:00–18:30 late-afternoon windows are often strong.
- **Finance, law and consulting:** Mornings are filled with meetings and client calls. Lunch breaks and the end of the day are generally more productive.
- **HR and recruitment-focused content:** For job postings and employer branding content, evenings, when job seekers are active, and Monday mornings are also worth considering.
- **Education and public sector:** Because working hours are stricter, the lunch break is the clearest window of opportunity.

### If you have an international audience

If you sell to markets outside Türkiye, you should set your posting time according to **the target market's local time**. For example, a company addressing audiences in both Türkiye and Germany could target 09:30–10:30 Türkiye time, which corresponds to 08:30 in Germany. This window fits the workday in both countries. For content aimed at the Gulf countries, you need to account for their different weekend schedules. If you address multiple markets, distributing through market-specific **Showcase pages** or the personal profiles of local teams solves the time-overlap problem.

## How does content format affect posting time?

Not all content demands the same attention span. Matching the format to the timing lets you get more out of the same content.

1. **Document and carousel posts:** These are 8–12-page pieces that need to be swiped through. The **lunch break**, when users can spare a few minutes, is the best time.
2. **Short video (30–90 seconds):** Suited to mobile consumption. It works well **in the morning between 07:30–09:00**, during the commute. Adding subtitles is essential at these times, since most users watch with the sound off.
3. **Long-form text posts:** Posts that tell a story or share a lesson get read more early in the morning or at the end of the day.
4. **Polls:** Since polls stay open for several days, the day matters more than the hour. A poll launched on **Tuesday or Wednesday morning** gathers engagement throughout the week.
5. **Event and webinar announcements:** You can follow a sequence: the first announcement 7–10 days before the event, a reminder 2–3 days before, and a final call on the morning of the event. Place the main announcement on Tuesday or Thursday morning.
6. **LinkedIn Live:** For live broadcasts, the lunch break (12:30–13:15) or the end of the workday (17:30–18:15) makes it easier for people to join.

## Is timing different for a company page and a personal profile?

It is. And this difference is one of the most overlooked aspects of B2B strategy. On LinkedIn, personal profiles generally achieve broader organic reach than company pages, because users prefer to engage with people. That's why you need to plan the timing of your company page and your employee and executive profiles together.

The approach we recommend is **staggered posting**:

- **08:30:** The company page shares the main content.
- **08:45–10:00:** 3–5 designated employees write meaningful comments on the post. Not a quick "congrats," but a sentence or question that adds value to the content.
- **12:00–13:00:** An executive or an employee who is an expert on the subject shares the same topic on their personal profile, from their own perspective and in their own words.
- **The next day:** The sales team uses the content as a reference in conversations with relevant prospects.

This structure boosts the company page's engagement in the first hours and keeps the content visible across different networks throughout the day. ## How do you find your own best posting time?

General recommendations are enough to get started, but the real competitive advantage comes from your own data. By applying the 7-step method below for 4–6 weeks, you can build a posting schedule tailored to your audience.

### 1. Review your existing data

Export the last 3–6 months of posts into a spreadsheet. For each post, record the posting day, time, format, impressions, reactions, comments, reposts and clicks. The **Followers** tab in LinkedIn page analytics also shows your audience's breakdown by job title, industry and company size. This breakdown gives you the first clue about which rhythm to target. You can find out which metrics to look at and how in our guide to [LinkedIn sayfa analitiğini okuma](/blog/how-to-read-linkedin-page-analytics), on reading LinkedIn page analytics.

### 2. Choose 3 time slots to test

Don't test too many variables at once. For example, choose these three slots:

- A: 08:30
- B: 12:15
- C: 17:30

### 3. Keep format and content quality constant

For a time test to be meaningful, you need to hold the other variables as constant as possible. Use similar topics, similar lengths and the same format. If you post a video one week and a carousel the next, you won't know whether the difference comes from the time or the format.

### 4. Test each slot at least 4–6 times

The performance of a single post is highly subject to chance. Don't draw conclusions until you've accumulated at least 4–6 posts for each time slot. A company posting 3 times a week will complete this test in about 6 weeks.

### 5. Use the right metric

Looking only at impressions can be misleading, because your follower count may grow during the test. Use these metrics instead:

- **Engagement rate:** (Reactions + comments + reposts + clicks) ÷ impressions
- **Engagement in the first 2 hours:** The metric that most clearly shows the effect of posting time.
- **Number of comments:** One of the signals the algorithm values most.
- **Click-through rate:** The most important business metric if the post links to content or a form.

### 6. Compare results and pick the winner

Calculate the average and median values for each time slot. A single viral post pulls the average up, so looking at the median gives a more reliable result. For example, if the 12:15 slot consistently delivers a higher engagement rate than the others, move your main content to that time.

### 7. Retest every quarter

Your audience changes, the algorithm changes and seasonal rhythms change. Usage habits shift during the summer months, holiday (Bayram) weeks and Ramadan. Running a short test round every 3 months keeps your schedule up to date. ## Why is the first hour after posting so critical?

Choosing the right posting time is half the job. The other half is what you do in the first hour after publishing. Here's what to do during that time:

- **Stay available for 60 minutes after posting.** Respond quickly and meaningfully to every comment. Replies to comments also count as engagement and keep the discussion alive.
- **Write the first comment yourself.** Start the conversation by adding extra context, a question or a related resource to the post.
- **Avoid editing the post right after publishing.** Fixing small typos usually isn't a problem, but changing the post frequently puts the initial distribution phase at unnecessary risk.
- **Be active for 15–20 minutes before posting.** Commenting on posts by people in your target audience can increase the likelihood of your own post appearing in their feeds.

Scheduling posts in advance with planning tools is very valuable for efficiency. Even so, a "schedule it and forget it" approach wastes the first-hour advantage. For every scheduled post, a team member should be assigned to manage engagement at that time.

## Common LinkedIn post-timing mistakes

1. **Blindly trusting generic lists:** "Best time" lists created for global audiences are usually based on US time zones. They can't be applied directly to an audience in Türkiye.
2. **Piling all posts into the same time slot:** Putting all 3 weekly posts at 09:00 means you only reach one segment of your audience.
3. **Publishing more than one company post on the same day:** For most B2B company pages, one post a day is enough. Leaving at least 18–24 hours between posts prevents them from cannibalizing each other's reach.
4. **Keeping the time test too short:** Making decisions based on two weeks of data means building a strategy on random results.
5. **Mixing up organic and ad timing:** The timing logic that applies to organic posts works differently in paid campaigns. In ads, budget and bidding strategy are more decisive. Be careful when carrying organic test results over to ad planning.
6. **Not accounting for public holidays and vacation periods:** Making major announcements during long holiday weeks causes most of the content to go unnoticed.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the worst time to post on LinkedIn?

For B2B audiences, the weakest times generally include Friday after 15:00, Saturday, and overnight between 22:00 and 06:00. Monday morning can also underperform due to heavy meeting traffic. Still, these time slots may vary depending on your audience, so you should test your own data before making a final decision.

### How many times a week should I post on LinkedIn?

For B2B company pages, 3–5 posts a week is a balanced starting point in most cases. Consistency and quality matter more than frequency. Three strong posts shared regularly each week usually perform better than daily posts shared irregularly.

### Does scheduling posts in advance reduce reach?

There is no clear evidence that posts scheduled with LinkedIn's native scheduling feature or reliable planning tools get less reach simply because they were scheduled. The real risk is that no one responds to comments when the scheduled post goes live. If you schedule posts, be sure to assign someone to be responsible for the first hour.

### Should the company page and employees share the same content at the same time?

No, staggered posting is more effective. The company page posts in the morning, and employees support it with meaningful comments in the first hour. Executives and experts then share the same topic later in the day, from their own perspective and in their own words. This keeps the content visible across different networks throughout the day.

## Conclusion

The short answer to the question of the best time to post on LinkedIn is, for B2B audiences, **Tuesday–Thursday, 08:00–10:00 and 12:00–13:30**. The long answer is this: the best time is the one you derive from your own audience's data. Start with the general framework and rigorously test 3 time slots over 4–6 weeks. Then match format to timing and make the most of the first hour after posting through engagement. When you run your company page and employee profiles together under a staggered plan, you get far more reach and engagement from the same content.
