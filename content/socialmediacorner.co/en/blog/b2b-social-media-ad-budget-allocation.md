A B2B social media ad budget is allocated by working backward from your sales target to a total figure, then splitting that figure across **funnel stages, platforms and a testing reserve**. As a practical starting template, put roughly **60-70% of the budget into proven campaigns, 20-30% into promising new experiments and 10% into purely experimental ideas**. Within the funnel, leave enough awareness budget to feed your conversion-focused campaigns and your retargeting. The exact ratios depend on brand awareness, the length of your sales cycle and the size of your target audience. However, concentrating the budget in a single campaign or on a single platform almost always wastes resources in B2B.

In this post, we'll walk step by step through how to split your ad budget based on clear logic rather than guesswork. Using sample figures, we'll cover setting the total budget, allocating it across funnel stages, balancing LinkedIn and Instagram, and reallocating the budget after campaigns go live. The figures in the examples are not real company data. They are illustrative and only show how the calculation works, so replace them with your own numbers.

## What questions should you answer before allocating your budget?

Budget allocation doesn't start in an Excel spreadsheet. It starts with a few fundamental questions. If you can't answer them clearly, then whatever ratio you choose, you'll keep asking "why did we put the money here?" as the campaign progresses.

- **What business outcome do you expect from advertising?** Meeting requests, demo sign-ups, quote requests, webinar attendance or brand awareness. Each requires a different campaign objective and comes with a different cost structure.
- **How long is your sales cycle?** A software subscription that closes in two weeks and an industrial equipment sale that takes twelve months can't be managed with the same budget split. The longer the cycle, the larger the share allocated to awareness and nurturing.
- **How large is your target audience?** In a niche market where only a few hundred companies in Türkiye could be buyers, a large budget quickly turns into showing the same ads to the same people again and again. If the audience is small, the audience sets your budget ceiling.
- **What is a customer's lifetime value to you?** In businesses with high revenue per customer, a higher cost per lead (CPL) is acceptable.
- **Does your sales team have the capacity to handle incoming leads?** Allocating a budget that brings in 100 leads to a team that can follow up on 20 a week will leave those leads to go cold.

These questions also help you build a shared language with the management team that approves the budget. Saying "We want this many qualified opportunities this quarter, and that requires this much spend" instead of "Let's spend this much per month" completely changes the budget conversation.

## How do you set the total ad budget?

In B2B, the soundest method is to **work backward from your sales target**. The "whatever we spent last year plus ten percent" approach hides what your campaigns are actually meant to achieve.

The example below shows a quarterly calculation:

1. **Set the target number of customers.** For example, you expect 10 new customers from social media ads this quarter.
2. **Apply your opportunity-to-customer conversion rate.** If, according to your sales team's historical data, 20% of sales-qualified opportunities (SQLs) become customers, you need 50 SQLs for 10 customers.
3. **Add your lead-to-SQL conversion rate.** If 25% of leads from ads become qualified opportunities, you need 200 leads for 50 SQLs.
4. **Multiply by your target cost per lead.** If your cost per lead in past campaigns or during a test period was around 1,500 TL, then 200 leads × 1,500 TL = **300,000 TL** in conversion budget.
5. **Add the supporting stages.** For conversion campaigns to work efficiently, you need awareness and retargeting budget to warm up the audience. This share usually makes up a significant portion of the total. In our example, let's assume we add 120,000 TL.
6. **Set aside a testing reserve.** About 10% for new creative and audience experiments, i.e. 40,000-45,000 TL.

This brings the total quarterly budget to roughly **460,000 TL**, or an average of around 150,000 TL per month. The value of this calculation lies not in the number itself but in the fact that every line item is tied to an assumption. Once the campaign starts, you can see which assumption didn't hold and adjust the budget accordingly.

If you don't have historical data, plan the first 4-8 weeks as a **learning period** and use a more modest budget during that time to establish your real CPL and conversion rates. Then redo the calculation with actual data. Modeling the business contribution of ad spend from the outset like this will also strengthen your hand later when [measuring B2B social media ROI](/blog/measure-b2b-social-media-roi).

## How do you split the budget across funnel stages?

Most B2B buyers aren't ready to fill out a form the moment they first see an ad. That's why putting the entire budget behind a "book a demo now" message means a high CPL in the short term and a drying-up lead pool in the long term. Thinking about the budget in terms of funnel stages solves this problem.

### Awareness: introducing yourself to the audience

The goal at this stage is to become visible to decision-makers who don't know you yet. Short videos, visuals featuring industry insights, thought leadership content and your brand story work well here. The key metrics to track are reach, frequency, video view rate and engagement.

The real function of the awareness budget is to build a **retargetable audience** for the later stages. 5,000 people who watched 50% of your video are a far more valuable retargeting pool than 50,000 people you know nothing about.

### Consideration: deepening interest

This is the stage where you offer more concrete value to people who have shown interest. Case stories (which can be told without naming the client), comparison guides, checklists, webinar invitations and reports are used here. The goal is for the person to make their first "small commitment" with you: downloading a piece of content, registering for an event or browsing a few pages of your website.

### Conversion: moving to a sales conversation

These are campaigns that generate direct sales opportunities, such as demo requests, free assessments and quote requests. They carry the highest click and lead costs, but they produce output the sales team can act on directly. On LinkedIn, native forms reduce friction at this stage. If you're curious about the setup, take a look at our [LinkedIn Lead Gen Form setup guide](/blog/how-to-set-up-linkedin-lead-gen-form).

### Retargeting: bringing back those who've gone cold

People who visited your website but didn't fill out a form, watched your video but didn't engage, or opened the form but didn't complete it. The retargeting budget looks small because its audience is small, but it is usually one of the most efficient line items.

### Sample splits by brand maturity

The ratios below are starting points, not hard rules:

| Situation | Awareness | Consideration | Conversion | Retargeting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New to the market, low-awareness brand | 40% | 25% | 20% | 15% |
| Growing brand with some awareness | 25% | 25% | 35% | 15% |
| Established, well-known brand in its category | 15% | 20% | 45% | 20% |

If a brand that's new to the market leans on conversion campaigns too early, it ends up trying to sell to a cold audience and gets few results at a high cost. If a well-known brand overspends on awareness, it keeps introducing itself to an audience that already knows it.

## LinkedIn or Instagram? How do you balance your platforms?

In B2B, most of the ad budget often goes to LinkedIn. The reason is simple: LinkedIn lets you target people by job title, seniority, industry, company size and even specific company lists. The price of this precision is usually higher click and impression costs. Setting up targeting correctly has a direct impact on those costs. For details, see our [guide to B2B targeting in LinkedIn ads](/blog/linkedin-ads-b2b-targeting).

Instagram (and the Meta ad network in general) isn't as sharp as LinkedIn when it comes to job title targeting. However, it offers lower unit costs, strong visual and video formats, and broad retargeting options. Instagram's share can be increased especially in the following situations:

- If your decision-makers have a profile where business and personal identity overlap, such as SMB owners, founders or entrepreneurs,
- If your product can be explained visually (architecture, design, food equipment, event services and so on),
- If you want to reinforce the awareness you built on LinkedIn at a lower cost,
- If employer branding and talent acquisition are also among your advertising goals.

Choosing the right campaign objective is critical to keep your Instagram budget from going to waste. We covered this in detail in our post on [choosing a campaign objective for Instagram ads](/blog/instagram-ad-campaign-objective).

### A practical starting point for platform allocation

- **Companies selling to corporate and technical decision-makers:** LinkedIn 70-80%, Meta (Instagram/Facebook) 20-30%. The Meta side is used mainly for retargeting.
- **Companies selling to SMBs and founders:** LinkedIn 50-60%, Meta 40-50%.
- **Companies with visually driven products or those that rely on industry events:** Start both platforms at similar ratios and rebalance based on data from the first 6-8 weeks.

What matters is seeing the two platforms not as competitors but as parts of the same journey. Recapturing a decision-maker who watched a video on LinkedIn on Instagram via their website visit is often more efficient than sticking to a single platform.

Seeing which messages your competitors run on Meta, and for how long, also informs your platform decision. We explained how to do [competitor analysis with the Meta Ad Library](/blog/meta-ad-library-competitor-analysis) in a separate post.

## How large should the testing budget be?

If your entire ad budget goes to campaigns you "already know," you stop learning. If it all goes to experiments, you can't get predictable results. A common way to strike this balance is the **70/20/10 approach**:

- **70% – Proven campaigns:** Campaigns with known costs and outcomes that consistently deliver leads or engagement.
- **20% – Experiments to scale:** New audience segments, a new offer or a new ad format that looked promising in initial testing.
- **10% – Experimental ideas:** A never-tested message, expansion into a different industry, a new platform format.

In a new account with no historical data, this ratio is reversed. In the first month, almost everything is a test. During this period, avoid testing too many variables at once. If you change the audience, the creative and the offer in a single campaign, you won't be able to tell what drove the result.

### The minimum budget needed to make a test meaningful

A common mistake in testing is splitting the budget across too many small ad sets. For example, if you spread a monthly test budget of 20,000 TL across 8 different campaigns, each will produce only a handful of conversions, and you'll pick the winner by chance rather than on statistical grounds. A better approach:

1. Test no more than 2-3 variables at a time.
2. Wait for each variation to produce enough results to support a meaningful decision (for example, at least 15-20 clicks or a few conversions).
3. If the budget isn't enough for that, extend the test period or reduce the number of variations.

## Example: How do you allocate a monthly budget of 100,000 TL?

Let's imagine a B2B company with some brand awareness that sells a software solution to IT and operations managers at large enterprises. The sales cycle is 3-6 months, and the target audience consists of a few thousand companies in Türkiye.

| Line item | Platform | Budget | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thought leadership video campaign | LinkedIn | 18,000 TL | Awareness, building a video viewer audience |
| Industry report / guide download | LinkedIn | 20,000 TL | Consideration, initial lead data |
| Demo request (Lead Gen Form) | LinkedIn | 27,000 TL | Conversion |
| Campaign for a target company list | LinkedIn | 10,000 TL | Focus on priority accounts |
| Website visitor and video viewer retargeting | Instagram / Facebook | 12,000 TL | Reminders, conversion support |
| Carousel explaining the solution | Instagram | 5,000 TL | Consideration, lower-cost reach |
| Experimental creative and audience tests | Both | 8,000 TL | Learning |

In this allocation, LinkedIn gets roughly 75% and the Meta side about 25%. From a funnel perspective, roughly 18% goes to awareness, 25% to consideration, 37% to conversion and target account campaigns, 12% to retargeting and about 8% to testing.

This table is not a template but a starting hypothesis. If, at the end of the first month, it turns out that the guide download campaign is generating qualified leads at a much lower cost while the demo campaign is more expensive than expected, the budget is shifted in that direction the following month.

## How much budget should go to target accounts (ABM)?

In account-based marketing (ABM), ads are shown to a predefined list of companies. If you're working with a list covering the 100-300 companies your sales team is actively pursuing, allocating 10-20% of the total budget to these campaigns is a reasonable starting point.

The value of the ABM budget becomes clear when it's considered together with the sales team's outbound efforts. If decision-makers at a company a sales rep is reaching out to by email or LinkedIn message are also seeing your ads during the same weeks, that first contact no longer feels like "a message from a stranger." Companies that want to synchronize advertising and outbound in this way need a data-driven approach. One thing to watch out for is that **frequency** rises quickly with small lists. If the same person sees the same ad over and over in a single week, costs increase and brand perception suffers. With small audiences, you need to keep daily budgets low and refresh creatives frequently.

## When and how should the budget be reallocated?

A budget plan shouldn't stay exactly as written once it goes live. But changing it every day isn't right either. Platform algorithms re-enter the learning phase with every major change, and performance fluctuates during that process.

### Weekly check

Each week, look at these questions:

- Is spend progressing at the planned pace, or are some campaigns unable to spend their budget?
- Has frequency exceeded the threshold you set?
- Has the click-through rate on your ad creatives dropped noticeably (creative fatigue)?
- Is there feedback from the sales team on the quality of leads coming in through forms?

### Monthly reallocation

At the end of the month, compare campaigns not only by cost per click or cost per lead but by **results further down the sales funnel**. A campaign with a low cost per lead that produces no qualified opportunities is not more valuable than an expensive campaign that turns into meetings.

These rules help when shifting the budget:

1. **Make changes gradually.** Instead of doubling a campaign's budget in one go, increasing it by around 20% every few days keeps the algorithm stable.
2. **Watch the audience when scaling a winner.** A campaign that performs well with a small audience may start showing more frequently to the same people as its budget grows.
3. **Don't shut down a loser right away; diagnose it first.** Is the problem the audience, the creative, the offer or the landing page?
4. **Account for seasonality.** Budget planning periods, year-end, public holidays and industry trade shows affect B2B buying behavior. Adjust your budget for these periods in advance.

### The right measurement for the right allocation

To reallocate your budget, you need to be able to see which campaign drove which result. Ad platforms' own reports aren't enough on their own. You also need to track the source accurately in your web analytics and CRM. Tagging every ad link with a consistent structure is the foundation of this. ## What are the most common budget allocation mistakes?

- **Putting the whole budget into one campaign:** Running only conversion campaigns leads to audience exhaustion and rapidly rising costs within a few weeks.
- **Spreading it too thin across many campaigns:** Each campaign gets so little budget that none of them can complete the learning phase.
- **Leaving creative production out of the budget:** An ad budget isn't just the money paid to platforms. Videos, visuals and copy need to be refreshed often. Set aside resources for creative production alongside your media budget.
- **Ignoring sales team feedback:** If what marketing calls a "good lead" isn't the same as what sales calls a "good lead," the budget drifts toward the wrong campaigns.
- **Overreacting to short-term results:** In a business with a long sales cycle, shutting down an awareness campaign based on the first two weeks of data dries up your pipeline three months later.
- **Disconnecting from organic content:** The message you highlight in your ads should also be supported on your company page and in employee posts. Otherwise, people who arrive at your page from an ad will see an inconsistent picture.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much budget should B2B companies allocate to social media advertising?

There's no single right number. The budget should be calculated backward from your target number of customers, your conversion rates and an acceptable cost per lead. If you don't have historical data, the safest path is to plan the first 1-2 months as a learning period with a more modest budget, then scale with real data.

### How should the budget be split between LinkedIn and Instagram?

For companies selling to corporate and technical decision-makers, LinkedIn usually takes the bulk of the budget, often 70-80%, while Instagram is used mainly for retargeting. For companies selling to SMB owners and founders, or those with visual products, Instagram's share can rise to 40-50%. The exact ratio should be determined by performance data from the first 6-8 weeks.

### How much of the budget should go to testing?

For accounts with established campaigns, a common approach is to allocate about 10% of the budget to purely experimental ideas and 20% to scaling promising experiments. In newly opened accounts, however, most of the budget is effectively testing in the early period. What matters is that each test has enough budget to produce results you can base a decision on.

### How often should the ad budget be changed?

Performance should be monitored weekly, while major budget shifts should be made monthly. Frequent, large changes disrupt the platform algorithm's learning process and make results fluctuate. When increasing the budget, raising it gradually every few days delivers more stable results.

## Conclusion

Allocating a B2B social media ad budget correctly starts with a total budget calculated backward from your sales target, continues with a deliberate balance across funnel stages, platforms and a testing reserve, and is continuously improved through regular measurement. Budget allocation isn't a spreadsheet you build once and forget; it's a hypothesis you update with real data every month. Companies that don't neglect awareness, support their conversion campaigns with retargeting and bring sales team feedback into the decision-making process generate noticeably more qualified opportunities with the same budget.
