For B2B sales teams, LinkedIn is one of the few platforms where you can reach decision-makers directly. But that ease of access creates a problem of its own: every day, a procurement manager's or CTO's inbox fills up with dozens of near-identical messages. Messages that open with "Hi, I came across your company and believe we could add value" usually get deleted unread. Sending cold messages on LinkedIn isn't the problem. The problem is copy-paste messages sent without any regard for the buyer's perspective.

In this post, we walk through writing a LinkedIn cold sales message step by step. You'll find the preparation to do before you hit send, the building blocks of an effective message, templates for different scenarios, a follow-up sequence and how to measure your results. The goal is to write messages that make the recipient think, "It makes sense to talk to this person."

## Why Do Most Cold Messages Go Unanswered?

Before getting into how to write a good message, it's worth understanding why bad messages don't work. Most unanswered cold messages repeat the same mistakes:

- **Seller-centric language:** The message is packed with "we," "our solution" and "our team," while the buyer's problem barely gets a mention.
- **Fake personalization:** Lines like "I reviewed your profile, very impressive" could be sent to anyone. The buyer spots this immediately.
- **Asking for too much, too soon:** Requesting a 45-minute demo in the very first message is like expecting a marriage proposal from a stranger on first meeting.
- **Length:** If a message opened on mobile requires scrolling several times, the chances of it being read drop sharply.
- **Vague call to action:** Closings like "Let's talk if you're available" put the thinking burden on the buyer. When? For how long? About what?

What these mistakes have in common is this: the message was written from the sender's sales target, not from the buyer's world. An effective cold message flips that perspective.

## Before You Send: The Preparation Stage

Even the best-written message loses its impact when it's sent to the wrong person from a weak profile. Before you start writing, you need to get clear on three areas.

### 1. Narrow down your ideal customer profile

"Manufacturing companies in Turkey" isn't a target audience; it's just a market. Your ideal customer profile (ICP) should answer these questions:

- Which industry and sub-sector? (e.g., automotive suppliers)
- What company size? (e.g., 50 to 500 employees)
- Which job title will you target? (e.g., production manager, operations director)
- What concrete problem does this person face today? (e.g., unplanned downtime, supply delays)
- What event might trigger a purchase? (A new plant investment, a new executive appointment, a funding round)

The narrower the profile, the more on-target your message will be. Instead of sending the same message to a thousand people, sending a message tailored to a shared problem to a hundred people who have it usually delivers better results.

### 2. Optimize your own profile like a sales page

The first thing anyone who receives your message will do is look at your profile. So your profile should act as an extension of your message:

- **Headline:** Write who you help and how, not your job title. Instead of "Sales Manager," try something like "I help manufacturers reduce unplanned downtime."
- **About section:** Use the first two or three lines to describe the customer's problem and your approach. There's no need for a lengthy résumé.
- **Featured:** Add a case study, a short guide or an industry analysis.
- **Recent activity:** A profile that hasn't shared any content in the last 30 days struggles to build trust.

It also matters that individual profiles are aligned with the company page. To build a consistent presence on the corporate side, check out our post on [how to grow your brand with social media management](/blog/how-to-grow-your-brand-with-social-media-management).

### 3. "Warm up" the buyer before messaging

A completely cold message doesn't have the same impact as a message from someone whose name the buyer has already seen. One to two weeks before sending a message, you can:

- Visit the target person's profile. LinkedIn shows this as a notification.
- Leave a thoughtful comment on one of their posts rather than a passing "like."
- Follow the company page and note the company's latest news.
- Be visible in shared groups or events.

This warm-up process ensures your message is perceived not as "a sales pitch from a stranger" but as "a note from someone whose name I've seen before."

## Types of Cold Messages on LinkedIn

There are several ways to reach someone on LinkedIn, and each comes with its own rules.

- **Connection request note:** A short note attached to a connection request. The character limit is quite low (around 300 characters), and free accounts may be limited in the number of personalized notes they can send. The purpose of this note isn't to sell but to give a reason for accepting the connection.
- **First message after connecting:** The message sent to an accepted connection. This is where the sales process really begins.
- **InMail:** A message sent to people you're not connected with, via premium accounts or Sales Navigator. Because it includes a subject line, it's closer to email.
- **Follow-up messages:** Short reminders you send when you don't get a reply. A significant share of replies usually comes from follow-ups, not the first message.

You should set a different length and a different goal for each message type. The connection note aims for acceptance, the first message aims to spark interest, and follow-ups aim to make it easier to decide.

## Anatomy of an Effective Cold Message

A good LinkedIn cold message is usually made up of five parts. You don't need all of them in every message, but this structure gives you a framework to write from.

1. **Context (why now, why you):** A sentence that shows why you're sending the message to this person and why now. It could be a post, company news, a new role or a mutual connection.
2. **Observation or insight:** A brief point about a problem commonly faced in the buyer's industry or role. Sharing an observation is more effective here than making a claim.
3. **Value proposition:** A one-sentence explanation of how similar companies have tackled this problem. Talk about outcomes, not product features.
4. **Proof (optional):** A short, general statement showing you've worked with companies of a similar profile. Don't use made-up figures or customer names without permission.
5. **Low-friction call to action:** A clear question that's easy to say yes to. Instead of "a 15-minute call," try something like "Would you like me to send you the short analysis we've put together on this?"

As a rule of thumb, aim to keep your first post-connection message between **50 and 120 words**. A message that fits on a single mobile screen has an advantage when it comes to being read and answered. Limit paragraphs to one or two sentences and end the message with no more than one question.

## LinkedIn Cold Sales Message Examples

The examples below were written for different scenarios. Rather than copying them word for word, we recommend studying their structure and adapting them to your own industry. Fields in square brackets should be filled in with real information for each recipient.

### Example 1: A bad message and its improved version

**Bad example:**

> Hi Ahmet, I'm Mehmet from X Software. Our company has been an industry leader for 10 years and provides ERP solutions to many large companies. I believe our product would be very beneficial for you as well. Could we schedule a 45-minute demo at a time that suits you?

This message contains not a single piece of information about the recipient, includes unverifiable claims like "leader," and makes a big time request at first contact.

**Improved example:**

> Hi Ahmet, I saw your post last week about your new production line. Congratulations.
>
> In manufacturers that are scaling up capacity, stock and order data often ends up scattered across different spreadsheets in the first few months. We help planning teams bring that data into one place during this transition.
>
> Are you running into any issues on that front, or do you already have a settled process in place?

The improved message opens with real context, presents a problem as an observation rather than a claim, and ends with a question that's easy to answer.

### Example 2: Connection request note (under 300 characters)

> Hi Elif, I read your article on customer churn at B2B SaaS companies. Your insights on the first 30 days of onboarding reflect something I see often too. I'd love to connect.

There's no selling in this note. The only goal is to get the connection accepted. The sales conversation starts once the connection is accepted.

### Example 3: Trigger event-based message (new appointment)

> Hi Can, I saw you've been appointed Marketing Director. Wishing you every success in your new role.
>
> When I talk to marketing leaders who've just stepped into a new role, what I hear most is that in their first 90 days they struggle to see "what existing channels are actually delivering."
>
> We have a short channel assessment checklist that could be useful during this period. Would you like me to send it over?

New appointments, company investment news, job postings and new office openings are strong triggers. These events usually coincide with periods when budgets and priorities are being reset.

### Example 4: Content engagement-based message

> Hi Zeynep, the reply you wrote yesterday to the "sales and marketing don't look at the same metric" comment on your post was spot on.
>
> Working with B2B teams, we also see this disconnect show up most in how leads are defined. Marketing says "interested," sales says "not ready."
>
> Does your team use any method to align on that definition? I'm curious.

Messages like this aim to start a conversation more than to sell. Asking for the other person's opinion gets replies more easily than describing your product.

### Example 5: Message via a mutual connection

> Hi Burak, I was speaking last month with our mutual connection Selin about digital transformation projects in your industry, and your name came up.
>
> We're seeing that route planning at logistics companies is still largely done manually. I'd love to hear about your experience with this.
>
> Would a short 15-minute phone call next week work for you?

Only use a mutual connection if you've actually spoken with them and they've agreed to their name being mentioned. Otherwise, you'll permanently damage your credibility.

### Example 6: InMail (with subject line)

**Subject:** About the growth of [Company name]'s export team

> Hi Deniz,
>
> I noticed you've opened several positions in your export department over the past two months. That suggests you're planning to expand into new markets.
>
> The most common challenge for B2B companies entering a new market is reaching the right decision-makers in the target country. We provide support with building target lists and planning first touches in this process.
>
> If you share which markets you're focusing on, I can put together a short note on what kind of approach could work for that specific market. Would you be interested?

In InMail, the subject line is as critical as it is in email. Choose a subject line that's short, personalized and doesn't smell like a sales pitch. Avoid words like "opportunity," "offer" or "discount."

### Example 7: Value first (give first, then ask)

> Hi Okan, while looking through your company's LinkedIn page, I noticed that most of the content shared over the past three months has been product announcements.
>
> I quickly put together a one-page summary for you comparing the content mix of three of your competitors. I'd be happy to share it with no strings attached.
>
> Should I send it here, or would you prefer email?

This approach takes more effort, but it positions you in the other person's eyes as "a consultant who knows their stuff" rather than "a salesperson." It works especially well for high-budget services with long sales cycles.

## Follow-Up Sequence: What to Do If You Don't Get a Reply?

No reply to your first message doesn't mean no interest. Decision-makers are busy. They may have read your message, thought "I'll get back to this later" and forgotten. That's why a planned follow-up sequence is essential. A sample schedule might look like this:

1. **Day 0 – First message:** The main message, built using the structure above.
2. **Day 3-4 – Short reminder:** A one-sentence note that adds something new.
   > "Okan, you may be in a busy period. I'd still be happy to share the competitor comparison I mentioned. A simple 'yes' is all it takes."
3. **Day 7-8 – Value-adding follow-up:** Share an article, a short analysis or an observation about the industry.
   > "This week we put together a short note on LinkedIn content strategy in your industry and thought it might interest you. Would you like to take a look?"
4. **Day 14 – Closing message:** A polite, no-pressure exit.
   > "If this isn't a priority for you right now, I completely understand, and I won't message you again. If it comes up down the line, just reach out here."

The closing message can unexpectedly bring in replies, because it removes the pressure and gives the other person a chance to say "no" or "actually, now's a good time."

**What to avoid in follow-up messages:**

- Lines that add no value, like "Did you see my message?" or "Just checking in."
- Sending the same message again.
- Following up more than four or five times. That crosses the line between persistence and annoyance.

## What Not to Do in LinkedIn Cold Messages

Now that you've seen the examples, let's gather the most common mistakes into a checklist:

- **Sending a sales message the moment a connection is accepted:** A long sales pitch right after acceptance is perceived as a "connection trap." Wait a few hours and write your first message to open a conversation.
- **Piling on attachments and links:** Don't send PDFs, brochures or multiple links in your first message. Confirm interest first.
- **An overly formal or overly familiar tone:** In Turkish B2B communication, addressing people as "Bey/Hanım" (Mr./Ms.) is a safe starting point in most industries. If the other person addresses you by first name, you can switch to that tone too.
- **Unverifiable claims:** Unsourced phrases like "market leader," "the best" or "300 percent efficiency gain" erode trust.
- **Unchecked automation:** LinkedIn enforces strict policies against third-party tools that send automated messages and may restrict accounts. Keep your daily connection and message volume at a reasonable level.
- **Bulk, non-personalized sending:** Sending the same message to a hundred people may seem efficient in the short term, but it damages both your brand reputation and your account's credibility.

## How to Measure Cold Message Performance

You can't improve a process you don't measure. Track these metrics when evaluating your cold messaging campaigns:

- **Connection acceptance rate:** How many of the requests you sent were accepted? If it's low, work on your profile or connection note.
- **Reply rate:** How many of your first messages got a reply? Negative replies matter too, because they show whether your targeting is right.
- **Positive reply rate:** How many replies include a meeting, a request for information or expressed interest?
- **Meeting conversion rate:** How many positive replies turned into an actual meeting?
- **Opportunity and revenue contribution:** How many of those meetings turned into sales opportunities and revenue?

Run your tests in a controlled way. Change only one variable at a time: test the opening line first, then the call to action, then the message length. Send each variation to enough people to get a meaningful result. Drawing general conclusions from tests of just a few messages can be misleading.

## A 7-Step Checklist to Put It Into Practice

To put what we've covered into practice, you can follow this sequence:

1. Define your ideal customer profile by industry, company size, job title and problem.
2. Update your own LinkedIn profile with a buyer-focused headline and About section.
3. Start by building a narrow target list of 50 to 100 people and write a personalization note for each one.
4. Engage meaningfully with these people's content for one to two weeks before sending a message.
5. Write your sequence: a connection note, a first message and three follow-up messages.
6. Track acceptance, reply, positive reply and meeting rates weekly.
7. Improve your messages by testing one variable at a time, and scale the approach that delivers results.

Writing a cold sales message on LinkedIn is less about finding the right words and more about adopting the right perspective. A good message starts from the buyer's world. A planned follow-up sequence, a strong profile, warm-up work done before messaging and consistent measurement turn these messages from one-off attempts into a repeatable sales process.

## FAQ

### How long should a LinkedIn cold sales message be?

Aim to keep your first post-connection message between 50 and 120 words. A message that fits on a single mobile screen, uses one- or two-sentence paragraphs and ends with no more than one question has an advantage in being read and answered.

### What are the parts of an effective LinkedIn cold message?

A good cold message is usually made up of five parts: context, an observation or insight, a value proposition, optional proof and a low-friction call to action. You don't need all of them in every message, but this structure gives you a framework to write from.

### How many times should you follow up if you don't get a reply?

A planned follow-up sequence can run as a first message on day 0, a short reminder on day 3-4, a value-adding follow-up on day 7-8 and a closing message on day 14. Following up more than four or five times crosses the line between persistence and annoyance.

### Should you pitch in a connection request note?

No, the purpose of a connection request note isn't to sell but to give a reason for accepting the connection. The character limit for this note is quite low, at around 300 characters.
