LinkedIn Page analytics is a dashboard that shows your company page's **content**, **visitor**, **follower**, **competitor** and **lead** data. To read it correctly, you shouldn't look at impressions or likes in isolation. What really matters is your **engagement rate**, **click-through rate (CTR)**, **follower and visitor demographics**, and how these metrics change over time. For a B2B company, the most important question is this: is your content reaching decision-makers and moving them to the next step?

Many corporate teams treat LinkedIn analytics as a table of numbers they screenshot at the end of the month and paste into a report. In reality, this dashboard is a decision-making tool. It shows which content types work, which industries and seniority levels follow you, and where you stand against your competitors. In this article, we go through each section of LinkedIn Page analytics one by one. Using examples, we explain what each metric means, which ones can be misleading, and how to turn your data into a monthly routine.

## Where can you find LinkedIn Page analytics?

To view analytics data, you need to be assigned a **super admin** role or a role with permission to view analytics on your company page. The steps to access it are roughly as follows:

1. Log in to LinkedIn and switch to the admin view of the company page you manage.
2. Open the **Analytics** tab in the left-hand menu.
3. Select the section you want to review: Content, Visitors, Followers, Leads, Competitors. If your page has a newsletter, newsletter analytics also appear here.
4. Set the date range in the top right. If needed, download the data in Excel format using the **Export** option.

Because LinkedIn updates its interface from time to time, menu names and locations may change. The logic stays the same, though: each section answers a different question. We recommend getting into the habit of exporting your data. The amount of historical data you can see in the dashboard is limited, and you'll need to keep your own archive to make year-over-year comparisons.

## What sections make up the analytics dashboard?

Understanding how the dashboard is structured makes it clear where to look for the answer to each question:

- **Content:** How your posts perform. Impressions, clicks, reactions, comments, reposts and engagement rate are all found in this section.
- **Visitors:** Who is visiting your page and which tabs they view.
- **Followers:** How your follower count changes and the demographic breakdown of your followers.
- **Leads:** Submissions from lead forms connected to your page. This section is visible if you have activated the form.
- **Competitors:** A comparison of follower growth and organic content performance against the competitor pages you select.

Each of these sections tells its own story, but real insight emerges when you read them together. For example, if content engagement is rising while your target industry's share of your follower demographics is falling, your content may be attracting the wrong audience.

## Which content analytics metrics really matter?

The Content section is where teams spend most of their time. Here you can filter organic and sponsored content separately. When evaluating organic performance, always separate out the sponsored data; otherwise, you'll draw the wrong conclusions from numbers inflated by your ad budget.

### What is the difference between impressions and members reached?

**Impressions** refer to how many times your post was displayed on screens. If the same person sees a post three times, that counts as three impressions. **Members reached (unique impressions)**, on the other hand, is the number of unique people who saw the post at least once.

The ratio between these two metrics shows how often your content repeats in the same audience's feed. If a post received 5,000 impressions and reached 2,000 unique members, the average is 2.5 impressions per person. If this ratio keeps rising, your content may be circulating within a narrow circle instead of spreading to new people.

### Why does click-through rate (CTR) matter?

**Click-through rate** is calculated by dividing the number of clicks by the number of impressions. For example, if a post with 4,000 impressions received 120 clicks, its CTR is 3%.

On LinkedIn, clicks don't only include link clicks; they can also include interactions such as expanding the post ("see more") or clicking on the company name or logo. So don't confuse CTR with "traffic to your website." To measure how many people actually visit your website, add **UTM parameters** to your links and track the results in your web analytics tool.

### How is engagement rate calculated?

The **engagement rate** shown in LinkedIn Page analytics is calculated roughly using this formula:

**(Clicks + Reactions + Comments + Reposts + Follows) / Impressions**

Example: Suppose a post received 3,000 impressions. It got 60 clicks, 45 reactions, 8 comments, 4 reposts and 3 new follows. Total engagement is 120, so the engagement rate is 120 / 3,000 = **4%**.

There are two points to keep in mind here:

- **Not all types of engagement are equally valuable.** A comment, especially one from a decision-maker in your target audience, is worth more than dozens of reactions. Reposts are also particularly important because they carry your content into new networks.
- **A "good" engagement rate varies by industry, follower count and content type.** Instead of comparing yourself with other people's averages, use your own historical data as a benchmark. Posts that exceed your 90-day average are your "good" posts.

### What should you look at for video and document posts?

For video content, you'll see additional metrics such as **views**, **total watch time** and **average watch time**. On the B2B side, the most instructive metric is usually average watch time. If a 2-minute video has an average watch time of 12 seconds, the core of your message isn't reaching most of your audience. In that case, you need to move the main message into the first few seconds or shorten the video.

For document (carousel/PDF) posts, click-through and engagement rates indirectly reflect swiping behavior. Testing the same topic as a single image and as a document, then comparing the results, is a practical way to find out which format your audience prefers.

## What does visitor analytics tell you?

Content analytics shows performance in the feed. Visitor analytics, on the other hand, shows which people **actively come to your page and what they look at**. The key metrics in this section are:

- **Page views:** The total number of times your page was viewed.
- **Unique visitors:** The number of distinct people who visited your page.
- **Views by tab:** How many views each tab, such as Home, About, Jobs, People and Life, received individually.
- **Desktop vs. mobile breakdown.**
- **Visitor demographics:** Job function, seniority, industry, company size and location.

From a B2B perspective, the most valuable part is **visitor demographics**. Take an industrial software company, for example. Say its target audience is operations and IT managers in the manufacturing sector. If most of its visitors are students or entry-level profiles, the page traffic is probably driven by employer branding or job postings. That's not a bad thing in itself, but it shouldn't be directly linked to sales goals.

High view counts on the **Jobs** and **Life** tabs indicate that your page is strong at attracting candidates. Heavy traffic to the **About** tab, meanwhile, may point to visitors looking for company information, most likely in the research stage of the buying process.

## How does follower data show you're reaching the right audience?

In the Followers section, you'll find two main data sets: **follower growth** and **follower demographics**.

### How should you interpret follower growth?

The follower growth chart breaks down the new followers you gained in a given period into **organic** and **sponsored**. There are three things to look at:

1. **The source of growth:** Are most of your new followers coming from a follower campaign, or from the natural impact of your content?
2. **Sudden spikes:** A spike on a particular day is usually the result of a post, an event or an employee's share. Match that day against your content calendar.
3. **Net growth rhythm:** A steady monthly number of new followers is a healthier indicator than one-off surges.

### Why are follower demographics more important than follower count?

In B2B, a page with 50,000 followers but a scattered audience usually generates less business value than a page with 5,000 followers, a significant share of whom are decision-makers in the target industry. That's why you should review your follower demographics regularly:

- **Job function:** What share do target functions such as purchasing, IT, finance and operations represent?
- **Seniority:** What proportion are decision-making profiles such as managers, directors and C-level executives?
- **Industry:** Are the industries you serve at the top of the list?
- **Company size:** Do you sell to SMBs or to enterprises? Does your follower distribution match that?
- **Location:** Are the markets you sell in represented in your follower list?

These demographic insights are also a valuable starting point for the targeting setup of your ad campaigns. ## How do you interpret competitor analysis?

In the **Competitors** section, you can add a certain number of competitor company pages and compare them with your own. This section typically includes the following data:

- Total follower count
- New followers in the selected period
- Number of organic posts
- Total engagement on organic posts

When interpreting this section, focus on **ratios, not absolute numbers**. Your competitor may have three times as many followers as you, but if they're gaining a similar number of new followers over the same period, your growth rate is close to theirs.

A practical example: You publish 8 posts a month and get 600 total engagements. Your competitor publishes 20 posts a month and gets 900 engagements. On a per-post average, you get 75 engagements and your competitor gets 45. In other words, your content quality or audience fit is stronger, while your competitor is getting ahead through volume. In this case, the right move is to gradually increase your posting frequency while maintaining quality, and to monitor the results.

You can also visit competitor pages directly on LinkedIn to see which content types are working for them. But the goal should be to understand which topics the audience responds to, not to copy them.

## How do you track lead and conversion metrics?

If a lead form is active on your company page, you can see the submissions that come through it in the **Leads** section. This data shows how many page visits turn into concrete contact requests.

Let's do a simple conversion calculation. Suppose 1,200 unique people visited your page in a month and the lead form received 18 submissions. Your visitor-to-lead conversion rate is 1.5%. Tracking this rate every month lets you measure the impact of changes to your page (description text, button, featured content).

However, LinkedIn Page analytics alone doesn't show sales outcomes. To see how many incoming leads turn into sales meetings, proposals and closed deals, you need to match it against your CRM data. ## Which metrics can be misleading?

Not every number is equally meaningful. These are the most common interpretation mistakes in LinkedIn analytics:

- **Relying on impressions alone:** High impressions don't mean you're reaching the right people. A viral post may have spread outside your target audience.
- **Treating reaction counts as a measure of success:** Reactions are the lowest-effort form of engagement. Likes from your own employees also push this number up. The quality of comments and the identity of the people commenting are far more instructive.
- **Mixing sponsored and organic data:** Comparing an advertising period and an ad-free period using the same filter gives a false impression of organic performance.
- **Looking at periods that are too short:** A single week's data is subject to random fluctuations. To see trends, compare periods of at least 4–12 weeks.
- **Setting follower count as a goal:** Follower count is an outcome, not a goal. The goal should be to engage regularly with the right audience.

## A step-by-step monthly analytics routine

The best way to make your data meaningful is to review it in the same order and with the same questions every month. The following routine offers a practical framework for corporate teams:

1. **Set the date range.** Select last month and compare it with the previous month and, if possible, the same month last year.
2. **Apply the organic filter.** Evaluate organic performance first, then sponsored performance separately.
3. **List your top 3 and bottom 3 posts.** Rank them by engagement rate. For each one, note characteristics such as format, topic, day and time of posting, and copy length.
4. **Find the common threads.** Do your best posts cluster around a particular topic? A particular format? A category such as customer stories, industry analysis or team content?
5. **Check your follower demographics.** How has the breakdown by target industry and seniority changed compared to last month?
6. **Look at visitor data.** Which tabs got the most views? Has interest in sales-oriented tabs increased?
7. **Update your competitor table.** Compare your engagement per post and follower growth rate.
8. **Match leads and web traffic.** Add traffic from UTM-tagged links and form submissions to the report.
9. **Write 3 action items.** Every report should end with concrete decisions to implement in next month's content plan.

The last step is the most critical. If an analytics report doesn't lead to action, it's just an archive document. ## Example: Let's read a month's data together

To make this concrete, let's walk through a fictional scenario. Suppose a B2B logistics software company's LinkedIn page produced the following data in one month:

- 10 organic posts, 28,000 total impressions
- 1,120 total engagements, average engagement rate of **4%**
- 140 new followers, 25 of them sponsored
- 1,600 unique page visitors
- 14 submissions from the lead form

At first glance, the picture looks positive. When you dig into the details, however, the following emerges:

- **2 of the 3 posts with the highest engagement** are document posts describing concrete process improvements in customer operations. Their engagement rates are above 6%.
- **The lowest-performing posts** are general celebration messages and product announcements. Their engagement rates are below 2%.
- **In follower demographics**, the share of the logistics and supply chain industry has increased. However, the proportion of followers at director level and above has stagnated.
- **A significant portion of visitors** viewed the Jobs tab. This traffic coincides with the job postings published that month.

The action items from this data could be:

1. Increase the number of process improvement–themed document posts from 2 to 4 per month.
2. Reframe product announcements around the question "what operational problem does this feature solve?"
3. Launch a content series offering executive-level insights to reach audiences at director level and above, and test part of this series as sponsored content with a small budget.
4. Review the page description and featured posts to increase lead conversions.

This scenario shows how analytics moves from the question "what happened?" to "what do we do now?" ## How can you make reporting more efficient?

Monthly reporting can quickly become time-consuming when you manage multiple company pages or markets. A few practical tips:

- **Use a fixed report template.** Keep the same metrics, the same charts and the same comparison periods every month.
- **Export your data regularly.** Combine your monthly export files into a single spreadsheet for long-term trend analysis.
- **Tag your posts.** Tag each post by topic, format and objective (awareness, engagement, traffic, leads). This lets you calculate average performance by category.
- **Take advantage of automation.** Automating data collection and reporting frees up your team's time for interpretation and action. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most important metric in LinkedIn Page analytics?

There is no single "most important" metric, but for B2B companies, **engagement rate** and **follower/visitor demographics** together give the most meaningful picture. Engagement rate shows whether your content resonates with your audience, while demographics show whether that audience actually consists of the decision-makers you're targeting. If you have sales goals, you should add lead and web traffic data to these.

### How often should I review LinkedIn analytics?

You can do quick post-level checks weekly, but a **monthly** review is better for strategic decisions. Weekly data is more heavily affected by random fluctuations. Quarterly reviews, meanwhile, are well suited to planning major shifts in your content strategy.

### Are personal profile analytics and company page analytics the same?

No. Personal profiles offer more limited data, such as post impressions, profile views and follower information. Company page analytics provides a more comprehensive dashboard geared toward business needs, including visitor demographics, competitor comparisons and lead form data. To measure the impact of employee posts on the company, you need to evaluate both data sources together.

### What should I do if my engagement rate is low?

First, find the source of the drop: is a particular format or topic underperforming, or is there a general decline? Then identify the common traits of your best-performing posts and give them more room in your content plan. Run small tests changing posting times, opening lines and visual formats, and compare the results over a few weeks.

When read correctly, LinkedIn Page analytics is a powerful data source that informs content, targeting and sales decisions for B2B companies. The real difference comes from establishing a regular routine that turns this data into concrete action items every month.
