The best way to negotiate a sales job offer in Turkey is to negotiate the **total package** and not just the base salary. That package includes the base in net terms, the commission plan and how realistic it is, the inflation-adjustment schedule, and the benefits that protect your real income. Ask for the offer in writing, convert everything to a monthly net figure, test the commission plan against realistic quota attainment, and make one clear, specific counteroffer backed by your track record.

I have sat on both sides of this table: as a rep negotiating my own offers, and as a founder building sales teams at Corner Group and hiring dozens of people. The candidates who negotiate well are rarely the most aggressive ones. They understand how sales compensation works in Turkey, they ask precise questions, and they treat the negotiation like a well-run sales cycle. This guide shows you how to do that, step by step.

## Why is negotiating a sales offer in Turkey different?

Most negotiation advice online is written for markets with stable currencies and annual raises. Turkey works differently in a few ways that matter a lot for your offer.

- **Gross vs. net confusion.** Many Turkish employers discuss salaries in net terms (net maaş), while international companies and formal contracts often use gross (brüt). The gap between the two is significant once income tax brackets and social security deductions apply. As the year goes on, cumulative income tax brackets can lower your net pay in later months. If you don't clarify which number you're discussing, you can "win" a negotiation and still end up with less money than you expected.
- **Inflation changes the math.** A salary that looks generous in January can lose much of its purchasing power by December. How often and how much your salary is adjusted can matter more than the starting number.
- **Raise cycles are often semi-annual.** Many companies in Turkey review salaries in January and July, often in line with minimum wage updates. Some only adjust once a year. That difference compounds quickly.
- **Commission is often loosely defined.** Many local firms describe the prim system verbally ("our good reps earn a lot of commission") without a written plan. In sales, the variable part is often where most of your earning potential sits. A vague plan is a risk you need to reduce before you sign.
- **Currency matters for some roles.** If you're selling for an international company or into foreign markets, you may be able to tie part of your pay to USD or EUR. That changes your risk profile completely.

## What should you clarify before talking numbers?

Before you counter anything, get the facts straight. I recommend asking the hiring manager or HR for the following, ideally in writing:

1. **Is the base salary quoted gross or net?** Ask them to confirm the monthly net figure for the first month and roughly what it will be later in the year.
2. **When is the next salary adjustment, and how is it decided?** Is it tied to official inflation, minimum wage changes, company performance, or "management discretion"?
3. **What does the full commission plan look like?** Ask for the document, not a summary.
4. **What is the quota, and what percentage of the current team hits it?** You don't need exact numbers. A truthful range ("about half the team hits quota most quarters") is enough.
5. **Is there a ramp period?** New reps rarely close at full capacity in month one. Is there a guaranteed draw or reduced quota while you ramp?
6. **What benefits are included?** Private health insurance, meal card, transport, phone, laptop, car or fuel allowance, remote work.
7. **What is the probation period?** Under Turkish Labor Law it is generally up to two months. Collective agreements can extend it to four.

These questions also show the employer that you understand sales compensation. That alone often lifts the conversation.

## How do you find out what the role should pay?

Salary data in Turkey is fragmented. Public salary sites exist, but they are often outdated or skewed toward large corporates. Build your own picture from several sources:

- **Talk to people in similar roles.** Message three to five SDRs, AEs or account managers on LinkedIn at companies of a similar size. Ask for ranges, not exact salaries. Most people will share more than you expect if you ask respectfully.
- **Ask recruiters directly.** Recruiters working on sales roles know current market ranges, and they usually share them because it helps them place candidates.
- **Use the job posting and interview signals.** If the company mentions "competitive salary plus uncapped commission," ask what a top performer and a median performer earned last year in total.
- **Understand the employer's side.** It helps to know what a rep actually costs a company beyond the salary: taxes, SGK contributions, tools, management time. I've broken this down in [what a sales rep really costs in Turkey](/blog/what-does-a-sales-rep-cost-in-turkey). Once you see the employer's real budget, you can frame requests in ways that are cheap for them and valuable for you.

Your goal is a realistic range for **total monthly net earnings at target**: base plus commission when you hit 100% of quota. That number is your anchor for everything else.

## How should you evaluate the commission plan?

In sales, the commission plan is often worth more than the base salary negotiation, and candidates often barely read it. Go through each of these points.

### Is the quota realistic?

A plan that pays generously at 100% attainment is worthless if almost nobody gets there. Ask how the quota was set and whether it changes during the year. If you want to understand how good companies approach this, my practical guide on [how to set sales quotas](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quotas-for-sales-team) explains what a fair quota looks like from the manager's side.

### When and how is commission paid?

- Monthly, quarterly, or annually? In a high-inflation environment, commission paid six months late is worth noticeably less.
- Is it paid when the deal is signed or when the customer pays? Long payment terms in Turkey can push your commission back by months.
- Is commission calculated in TL even for deals priced in USD or EUR? If you close a contract in dollars, ask whether your commission uses the exchange rate on the signing date or the payment date.

### Are there caps, clawbacks or thresholds?

- **Caps** limit your upside. Try to remove them or move them higher.
- **Clawbacks** take commission back if a customer churns or doesn't pay. Ask for the time window. Something like 90 days is reasonable; open-ended clawbacks are not.
- **Thresholds** mean you earn nothing below a certain attainment level, such as 70%. These are common, but know where the line is.

### What happens during ramp?

New B2B reps typically take several months to reach full productivity, depending on deal complexity. I've written about [how long a new sales rep takes to ramp up](/blog/how-long-does-a-new-sales-rep-take-to-ramp-up). If the company expects full quota from month one, negotiate a reduced ramp quota or a guaranteed minimum commission for the first two or three months.

## Which benefits are worth negotiating in Turkey?

When a company can't move on base salary, benefits are often where you find room. Some are easy approvals for HR because they have fixed, predictable costs. In rough order of real-world value for most sales professionals:

- **Inflation-linked salary adjustments.** Getting "two adjustments per year, at least matching official inflation" written into your offer can be worth more than a 10% higher starting base.
- **Private health insurance (özel sağlık sigortası).** Especially valuable if it can cover your spouse or children.
- **Meal card (yemek kartı) value.** It's often tax-advantaged up to a daily limit, so a higher meal allowance can be cheaper for the employer than the same increase in salary.
- **Transport, fuel, or car allowance.** Essential for field sales roles in Istanbul, Ankara or İzmir. Clarify whether a company car is for personal use too.
- **Remote or hybrid days.** Saving two hours of Istanbul traffic three days a week has real value.
- **Phone, laptop and tools.** Small items, but they add up.
- **Training budget.** Sales courses, English classes, or certifications that grow your future earning power.
- **Title and review timeline.** A written six-month performance review with a clear path to promotion.

The last point is often underrated. A role with a clear promotion path is worth more than a slightly higher salary with no growth. Ask how the company has handled the move from SDR to AE, or AE to team lead, in practice. My articles on [building a sales career path](/blog/how-to-build-a-sales-career-path) and [moving from SDR to account executive](/blog/move-from-sdr-to-account-executive) cover what a healthy path looks like, so you can judge whether theirs is real.

## How do you actually run the negotiation, step by step?

Treat the negotiation like a deal. You qualify, discover needs, present value, handle objections and close.

1. **Thank them and ask for the offer in writing.** Say something like: *"Thank you, I'm genuinely excited about this role. Could you send me the full offer, including the commission plan, so I can review it properly?"* Never negotiate a verbal number on the spot.
2. **Convert everything into one comparable number.** Calculate monthly net base, plus expected monthly commission at a realistic attainment level (I use 80% of quota, not 100%), plus the cash value of benefits.
3. **Decide your three priorities.** For example: (1) base salary, (2) a guaranteed ramp commission, (3) a written July adjustment. Don't send ten requests. Three focused asks get taken seriously; ten look like a shopping list.
4. **Anchor with evidence, not need.** Employers don't pay more because your rent went up. They pay more because you'll bring more revenue. Use your numbers: meetings booked, pipeline generated, deals closed, quota attainment.
5. **Make one clear counteroffer.** Ask for a specific number, slightly above your target, with a reason. Avoid wide ranges, because the employer will pick the bottom.
6. **Handle objections like a seller.** If they say "the budget is fixed," ask: *"I understand. If the base can't move, could we look at the ramp guarantee or an earlier review date instead?"* This is the same skill as [handling price objections in B2B sales calls](/blog/how-to-handle-price-objections-in-b2b-sales-calls): don't fold, change the variable.
7. **Close in writing.** Once you agree, ask for an updated offer letter that reflects every change. Verbal promises about "a raise after probation" disappear when managers change.

## What does a good negotiation look like in practice?

Here is an illustrative example. The figures are made up purely to show the method.

Elif is moving from an SDR role into her first AE position at a mid-sized SaaS company in Istanbul. The initial offer is:

- 70,000 TL net monthly base
- Commission of 8% on new annual contract value, paid quarterly after customer payment
- Monthly quota equivalent to 250,000 TL in new contract value
- Salary review "once a year"
- Meal card and standard health insurance

Elif does the math. At 100% attainment, 8% of 250,000 TL is 20,000 TL per month in commission, so total target earnings are about 90,000 TL net. She asks around and learns the team usually lands around 70–90% of quota. At a realistic 80%, she'd earn about 16,000 TL commission, or 86,000 TL total. Quarterly payment after customer payment also means her first commission might not arrive until month five or six.

She counters with three focused asks:

1. Base salary of 78,000 TL net, supported by her record of consistently beating her SDR meeting targets.
2. A guaranteed 10,000 TL minimum monthly commission for the first three months while she ramps.
3. Salary adjustments in both January and July, written into the offer.

The company comes back with 75,000 TL base, the ramp guarantee, and the semi-annual adjustment. She "lost" 3,000 TL on base but secured 30,000 TL in guaranteed early commission and protected her income against inflation for the whole year. Over twelve months, that's a much better outcome than the initial offer, and arguably better than a higher base with annual reviews.

## What mistakes cost candidates the most?

- **Negotiating gross and net interchangeably.** Always confirm which one you're discussing.
- **Ignoring the commission plan's fine print.** Payment timing and clawbacks can quietly cut your real earnings.
- **Accepting "we'll review after probation" verbally.** If it isn't in writing, assume it won't happen.
- **Using competing offers as threats.** Mention them as context ("I have another offer I'm considering"), but never bluff. Turkey's sales community is smaller than you think.
- **Negotiating too long.** One or two rounds is normal. Five rounds signals that you'll be difficult to manage.
- **Forgetting your notice period.** If you're employed, your current employer is legally entitled to notice (generally two to eight weeks depending on tenure). Agree on a realistic start date upfront.

## FAQ

### Should I negotiate the first sales job offer I receive in Turkey?

Yes, but negotiate politely and with focus. Even entry-level candidates can usually improve something, such as the ramp guarantee, review timing, or benefits, even if the base salary is fixed. Most employers expect a thoughtful counteroffer and respect candidates who ask good questions about the commission plan.

### Is it better to ask for a higher base salary or a better commission?

It depends on your confidence in the product and the quota. If the quota is realistic and the team regularly hits it, a stronger commission plan usually pays more over time. If the product is new, the territory is unproven, or you're in ramp, prioritize base salary and guaranteed minimums to protect yourself.

### Can I ask for a salary in USD or EUR in Turkey?

You can ask, and it's realistic for roles at international companies or roles selling into foreign markets. A common middle ground is a TL base salary with commission tied to foreign-currency deal values. Make sure the contract clearly states which exchange rate and date are used for calculations.

### How much higher should my counteroffer be?

There's no universal percentage. Base it on your market research and what you can prove with your results. Ask for a specific number slightly above your real target so there's room to meet in the middle, and always explain why you're worth it with concrete performance data.

## Conclusion

Negotiating a sales job offer in Turkey comes down to seeing the whole picture: net base salary, a commission plan you can realistically earn, inflation protection, ramp support and benefits that cost the employer little but matter to you. Get everything in writing, focus on three priorities, back your asks with numbers, and run the conversation the way you'd run a good deal. Calm, prepared and specific.
