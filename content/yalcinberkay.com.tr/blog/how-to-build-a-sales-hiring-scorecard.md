A sales hiring scorecard is a one-page document that lists the 5–7 competencies a sales role actually requires, defines what weak, acceptable, and excellent look like for each, and assigns every interviewer a specific competency to score on a fixed scale. **To build one, you start from the outcomes the hire must deliver in their first 12 months, translate those outcomes into observable behaviors, weight them by importance, and map each one to a specific interview stage.** Done well, it replaces "I liked her energy" with evidence you can compare across candidates.

I've hired salespeople across several companies in the Corner Group. The most expensive mistakes I've made came from interviews where everyone walked out "feeling good" and nobody could say why. A scorecard doesn't make hiring decisions for you. It forces your team to argue about evidence instead of impressions. Below is the exact process I use and a template you can copy and adapt.

## What is a sales hiring scorecard, and why do you need one?

Most sales interviews fail the same way. Five people meet the candidate. Each asks a version of "tell me about yourself" and "how do you handle rejection." Each forms an overall gut feeling. Then the debrief turns into a popularity contest, and the most confident person in the room wins.

Salespeople are, by profession, good at being liked in a 45-minute conversation. That's exactly why gut-feel hiring is so dangerous in sales. A scorecard protects you from this in three ways:

- **It defines success before you meet anyone.** You decide what matters while you're still objective, not after a charming candidate has anchored you.
- **It splits the work.** Each interviewer goes deep on two competencies instead of shallow on ten.
- **It makes candidates comparable.** When you're choosing between three finalists, you compare scores and evidence, not memories.

It also helps later. The same competencies feed straight into your onboarding plan, your coaching conversations, and, if it comes to that, your performance discussions.

## Step 1: Start with outcomes, not traits

Don't open a blank document and write "hungry, coachable, resilient." Those words mean something different to everyone on your team. Start instead by writing down what this person must *achieve*.

Ask yourself: if this hire is a clear success 12 months from now, what will have happened? Be specific. For example, for a mid-market Account Executive:

1. Reached full quota by month 6 and maintained at least 80% attainment through month 12.
2. Self-sourced at least 30% of their own pipeline through outbound.
3. Ran a clean CRM with accurate stages and next steps, so the forecast is trustworthy.
4. Closed deals with at least two stakeholders involved, including finance or procurement.
5. Contributed at least one improvement to the playbook (a new objection response, a better discovery question, a vertical-specific pitch).

Write 3–6 outcomes. If you can't, you're not ready to hire yet. You don't actually know what the job is. This is especially common when founders hire their first reps. If that's you, read my guide on [building an outbound sales team from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-outbound-sales-team-from-scratch) before writing the job description.

Your ramp expectations also shape the outcomes. Expecting full quota in month 2 for a complex B2B sale will push you toward the wrong profile. Check realistic benchmarks in [how long a new sales rep takes to ramp up](/blog/how-long-does-a-new-sales-rep-take-to-ramp-up).

## Step 2: Translate outcomes into 5–7 competencies

Now ask: what does someone need to be good at to deliver those outcomes? Each outcome usually maps to one or two competencies. Using the AE example above:

- **Prospecting discipline:** consistently builds their own pipeline without being chased (outcome 2).
- **Discovery and qualification:** uncovers real pain, budget, timeline, and decision process (outcomes 1 and 4).
- **Multi-threading and deal control:** manages several stakeholders and keeps deals moving (outcome 4).
- **Pipeline and CRM hygiene:** organized, honest about deal status (outcome 3).
- **Coachability:** takes feedback and changes behavior quickly (outcomes 1 and 5).
- **Written communication:** clear, short, persuasive emails and follow-ups (outcomes 2 and 4).
- **Motivation fit:** what drives them matches what the role offers (all outcomes).

Stop at seven. Every competency you add dilutes attention from the ones that matter. If you have ten, merge or cut. "Resilience," for example, is usually just evidence you'll already see in prospecting discipline.

## Step 3: Write behavioral anchors for a 1–4 scale

This is the step most teams skip, and it's the one that makes a scorecard work. A score of "3" is meaningless unless everyone agrees what a 3 looks like.

I use a **4-point scale** on purpose. With 5 points, tired interviewers default to "3, it was fine." With 4, they have to lean one way or the other.

- **1, Strong no:** clear evidence the candidate can't do this.
- **2, Lean no:** some ability, but below what the role requires.
- **3, Lean yes:** meets the bar, with solid evidence.
- **4, Strong yes:** exceeds the bar, with specific and repeatable evidence.

Then write anchors for each competency. Here's an example for **Discovery and qualification**:

- **1:** Pitches the product within the first two minutes of the role-play. Asks no follow-up questions. Can't describe how they qualified a past deal.
- **2:** Asks standard questions but accepts surface answers ("we want to grow revenue"). Doesn't uncover the decision process.
- **3:** Asks layered follow-ups. Identifies a quantified pain and at least one other stakeholder. Can explain why a past deal was disqualified.
- **4:** Does everything in 3, and also reframes the buyer's problem, confirms timeline and budget naturally, and ends with a concrete, agreed next step.

Notice that every anchor describes something an interviewer can *observe or hear*. "Seems smart" isn't an anchor. "Asked three follow-up questions before presenting" is.

## Step 4: Weight the competencies and set must-haves

Not every competency matters equally. Assign weights that add up to 100%. For the AE example:

| Competency | Weight | Must-have? |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery and qualification | 25% | Yes (min. 3) |
| Prospecting discipline | 20% | Yes (min. 3) |
| Multi-threading and deal control | 15% | No |
| Coachability | 15% | Yes (min. 3) |
| Pipeline and CRM hygiene | 10% | No |
| Written communication | 10% | No |
| Motivation fit | 5% | No |

Then set two decision rules:

1. **Weighted score of 3.0 or higher** to make an offer.
2. **No must-have competency below 3**, regardless of the total.

The second rule matters more than people think. A candidate who scores 4 on everything except coachability, where they score 2, will average out to a "hire." In practice, an uncoachable rep with great talk-track skills is one of the hardest people to manage. The must-have rule catches that.

Here's a quick example calculation. Candidate A scores Discovery 4, Prospecting 3, Multi-threading 3, Coachability 3, CRM 2, Writing 3, Motivation 4:

- (4 × 0.25) + (3 × 0.20) + (3 × 0.15) + (3 × 0.15) + (2 × 0.10) + (3 × 0.10) + (4 × 0.05)
- = 1.00 + 0.60 + 0.45 + 0.45 + 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.20 = **3.20**

All must-haves are at 3 or above, so Candidate A clears the bar. The CRM score of 2 becomes a coaching focus for the first 30 days, not a rejection reason.

## Step 5: Map each competency to an interview stage

Now decide *where* you'll gather evidence for each competency. Each competency should be covered by at least one stage, ideally two. No interviewer should own more than two or three.

A typical structure for an AE role:

1. **Recruiter or founder screen (30 min):** Motivation fit, basic career story, compensation expectations.
2. **Hiring manager interview (45–60 min):** Prospecting discipline and pipeline hygiene, through behavioral questions about past results.
3. **Discovery role-play (45 min):** Discovery and qualification, coachability.
4. **Written exercise or take-home (async):** Written communication, prospecting approach.
5. **Peer or cross-functional interview (30–45 min):** Multi-threading and deal control, team fit.

### How do you test coachability in an interview?

The most reliable method I've found: run the role-play, give one specific piece of feedback ("You jumped to the demo before confirming who else is involved in the decision. Try it again and slow down there."), then run a short second round. You're not scoring the first attempt. You're scoring how much they change in the second. Someone who nods and then repeats the exact same behavior has just shown you your next six months.

### What questions should each interviewer ask?

Give every interviewer 2–3 behavioral questions tied to their competency, and require them to probe for specifics. For prospecting discipline:

- "Walk me through your outbound activity in a typical week at your last job. How many touches, through which channels, and how did you decide who to target?"
- "Tell me about a quarter when your pipeline was too thin. What did you do in the first two weeks?"
- "What percentage of your closed revenue last year was self-sourced? How do you know?"

Strong candidates answer with numbers and names of tools and processes. Weak ones answer with adjectives.

If you use a take-home exercise, make it reflect real work: a cold email sequence to a realistic persona, or a short account plan. I've written about this from the candidate side in [how to ace a sales take-home assignment](/blog/how-to-ace-a-sales-take-home-assignment). It's also useful reading for hiring managers designing one, because it shows what good candidates will try to demonstrate.

## The sales hiring scorecard template

Copy this into a doc or spreadsheet and adapt it per role.

**Role:** ________ **Candidate:** ________ **Interviewer:** ________ **Stage:** ________ **Date:** ________

**12-month outcomes this role must deliver:**
1. ________
2. ________
3. ________

**Competencies assigned to you in this stage:** ________

For each assigned competency:

| Competency | Weight | Score (1–4) | Evidence (quotes, numbers, behaviors) | Concerns / follow-up for next interviewer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ________ | __% | __ | ________ | ________ |
| ________ | __% | __ | ________ | ________ |

**Must-have check:** Any must-have below 3? Yes / No

**Overall recommendation:** Strong no / Lean no / Lean yes / Strong yes

**Submitted before debrief?** Yes / No

The "evidence" column is the heart of the scorecard. If an interviewer writes a 4 with no evidence, the score doesn't count in the debrief. That one rule improves interview quality more than any training session.

## How do you run a fair scoring and debrief process?

The template is only half the system. The other half is how people use it.

- **Score independently, before the debrief.** Every interviewer submits their scorecard before talking to anyone else. If the founder speaks first and says "I loved him," everyone else's scores mysteriously rise.
- **Hand off concerns.** If the hiring manager isn't sure about the candidate's multi-threading experience, they write that in the "follow-up" column so the next interviewer can probe it.
- **Debrief competency by competency, not person by person.** Go through each competency and ask who has evidence. Discuss disagreements by comparing evidence, not opinions.
- **Let the lowest-ranked person speak first.** In small teams especially, juniors defer to the founder. Reverse the order.
- **Calibrate every few hires.** After 3–5 hires, look back. Did people who scored 4 on prospecting actually build pipeline? If not, your anchors or your questions need work.

## Should an SDR scorecard differ from an AE or sales manager scorecard?

Yes, significantly. The structure stays the same, but the competencies and weights shift.

- **SDR:** Weight coachability, work ethic and activity consistency, written communication, and curiosity heavily (together 70–80%). Prior experience matters little. You're hiring for raw material and learning speed.
- **AE:** Weight discovery, deal control, and self-sourced pipeline heavily. You need proof they've closed, not just that they can talk.
- **Sales manager or Head of Sales:** Add hiring ability, coaching, forecasting accuracy, and process design. Reduce weight on individual selling. Ask them to walk through how *they* built a scorecard, and listen for whether they've ever let someone go. ## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Copying a generic scorecard from the internet.** Use templates (including this one) as structure, but the outcomes and anchors must come from your sale, your buyers, and your deal cycle.
- **Too many interviewers.** Four to five stages is enough. Beyond that, strong candidates drop out, especially in tight markets where good reps have multiple offers.
- **Scoring "culture fit" without defining it.** Undefined culture fit becomes "similar to us." Define specific values with behavioral anchors, or leave it out.
- **Ignoring the scorecard when someone is charming.** If the numbers say lean no and the room says yes, that's precisely the moment to trust the process.
- **Throwing it away after the offer.** The candidate's lowest scores are your onboarding priorities. Hand the scorecard to whoever runs their first month and build it into your [30-day onboarding plan for new sales reps](/blog/how-to-onboard-a-new-sales-rep-first-30-days).

## FAQ

### How many competencies should a sales hiring scorecard include?

Five to seven competencies is the right range for most sales roles. Fewer than five usually misses something important, while more than seven spreads interviewer attention too thin and makes scores less reliable. If your list is longer, merge overlapping items or cut the ones with the lowest weight.

### Should I use a 1–5 or a 1–4 rating scale?

A 1–4 scale works better for hiring because it removes the neutral middle option. Interviewers have to commit to "lean yes" or "lean no," which produces clearer signals in the debrief. Whatever scale you choose, define behavioral anchors for every point so scores mean the same thing to everyone.

### Can a small startup with one founder use a hiring scorecard?

Yes, and it arguably matters even more in that case, because there's no second opinion to catch a founder's bias. A solo founder can still define outcomes, write anchors, and score each stage separately before deciding. Bringing in one trusted outsider for a role-play stage adds a valuable independent score.

### How often should I update the scorecard?

Review it after every 3–5 hires, or whenever the role changes meaningfully, such as moving upmarket or adding a new product. Compare interview scores with actual performance at 6 and 12 months. Adjust the anchors or weights where the scorecard predicted poorly.

## Conclusion

A sales hiring scorecard is simple to build. You define 12-month outcomes, turn them into 5–7 weighted competencies, write observable anchors on a 1–4 scale, assign each competency to an interview stage, and require evidence for every score. The hard part is the discipline to use it when a charismatic candidate tempts you to skip it. Teams that stick with it make fewer expensive hiring mistakes, onboard faster, and build sales cultures where decisions are based on evidence.
