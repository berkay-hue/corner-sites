The fastest way to land your first 10 B2B customers is for the founder to sell personally to a narrow, well-defined group of companies that share one painful problem. In practice that means hand-picking about 100 target accounts, starting with your warm network, running founder-led outbound, and treating every early deal as a learning partnership instead of a transaction. Paid ads, a big sales team, and a polished brand matter far less at this stage than talking to enough of the right people each week.

I've watched many founders try to skip this phase by hiring a salesperson too early, buying a large contact list, or waiting for inbound to "kick in." It rarely works. Your first 10 customers are not a scale problem. They are a learning problem, and only the founder can solve it. Below is the process I recommend, with concrete numbers you can plan around.

## Why are the first 10 customers so different?

Customer number 100 buys because you have proof. They have seen case studies, they have heard from peers, and your product has been refined by dozens of earlier buyers. Customer number one buys because they believe *you*. They are taking a risk on an unproven company, and they do it because they trust that the founder understands their problem and will personally make it work.

That changes how you should sell:

- **You are selling a relationship, not a feature list.** Early buyers want access, speed, and influence over the roadmap.
- **Every deal is a research project.** The objections you hear in these conversations become your future sales playbook.
- **Speed of learning beats volume.** Ten deep conversations a week where you learn something new are better than 50 shallow ones.

If you go in with this mindset, the rest of the process makes sense.

## Step 1: Define a painfully narrow ideal customer

The most common mistake I see is a target market like "SMEs that need better sales" or "companies in manufacturing." That is not a market. It's a category, and you cannot write a compelling message to a category.

A useful early ICP has four parts:

1. **Industry or segment:** for example, B2B SaaS companies selling to HR departments.
2. **Size:** for example, 20–100 employees, post-seed, with a small sales team.
3. **Trigger or situation:** for example, they just raised funding, hired a new sales leader, or entered a new country.
4. **The specific pain:** for example, the founder is still doing all the demos and pipeline is unpredictable.

When you get this specific, three things happen. Your list becomes easy to build, your messaging writes itself, and prospects recognize themselves immediately when they read your email.

A simple test: can you name 100 real companies that fit your ICP? If you can name 10,000, it's too broad. If you can only name 15, it's too narrow for now.

## Step 2: Build a list of 100 target accounts by hand

For your first 10 customers, you don't need a database of 20,000 contacts. You need roughly 100 well-researched accounts and the right two or three people inside each one.

Here's how I'd approach it:

- **Start with a prospecting tool** such as [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) to filter by industry, headcount, location, and job title. This gives you a raw list quickly.
- **Then verify manually.** Visit each company's website and LinkedIn page. Are they actually in your segment? Do they show the trigger you care about, like a recent funding announcement or open sales roles?
- **Pick two to three contacts per account.** Usually the economic decision-maker (founder, CEO, VP), plus the person who feels the pain daily.
- **Write one line of context per account.** "Hiring their second SDR," "just launched in Germany," "founder posts about pipeline problems." This line becomes your personalization later.

This takes time. Expect to spend 10–15 hours building a solid list of 100 accounts. That investment pays off because every message you send afterwards is relevant.

## Step 3: Start with your warm network first

Before you send a single cold email, go through your existing network. Founders consistently underestimate how much warm opportunity they already have.

Make three lists:

1. **People who could buy directly:** former colleagues, past clients, people you've worked with who now run teams in your ICP.
2. **People who could introduce you:** investors, advisors, former managers, friends in adjacent industries.
3. **People who know the problem well:** even if they won't buy, they can sharpen your pitch and refer you.

Then send short, honest messages. Something like:

> "I've started a company that helps [ICP] solve [pain]. I'm looking for 5 early partners to work closely with. Do you know anyone dealing with this right now, or would you be open to a 20-minute call to tell me if I'm missing something?"

Asking for advice instead of a sale lowers the pressure, and advice calls often turn into buying conversations. In my experience, a founder's first two or three customers very often come from this warm circle.

## Step 4: Run founder-led outbound

Once your warm network is exhausted, outbound becomes your main engine. As the founder, you have an advantage no SDR has: credibility. A message from the person who built the company carries more weight than a message from a junior rep.

### Cold email

Keep emails short (under 100 words), specific to the account, and focused on one problem. A simple structure:

- **Line 1:** The context you found (their hiring, expansion, a post they wrote).
- **Line 2:** The problem companies in that situation usually face.
- **Line 3:** One sentence on how you help.
- **Line 4:** A low-friction question, not a meeting demand.

Run a sequence of three to four touches over two to three weeks. If you want to go deeper on subject lines, personalization, and follow-ups, I've covered it in detail in my guide on [how to improve your cold email reply rate](/blog/how-to-improve-cold-email-reply-rate).

### LinkedIn

Combine email with LinkedIn. View the profile, connect without a pitch, engage with their content once or twice, then send a short message referencing something real. Posting regularly yourself also helps: when prospects check your profile after receiving your email, they should find a founder who clearly understands their world. ### Phone

Don't ignore the phone, especially for local markets. A short, polite call that references your email often gets a meeting faster than another follow-up.

## How many conversations do you need to land 10 customers?

Every business is different, so treat these as planning numbers rather than benchmarks. But working backwards from your goal forces discipline.

Here's an illustrative funnel for a founder selling a mid-priced B2B service:

- **Target:** 10 customers
- **Assume** roughly 1 in 4 qualified opportunities closes in the early days → you need about **40 qualified opportunities**
- **Assume** about half of first meetings turn into real opportunities → you need about **80 first meetings**
- **Assume** a small percentage of contacted people book a meeting → depending on your reply rate, that could mean reaching out to **several hundred to a thousand contacts** across your accounts over a few months

Then run your own numbers after the first 100 outreaches. If your meeting rate is far lower than expected, the problem is usually the ICP or the message, not the volume. Fix that before scaling activity.

A realistic weekly rhythm for a founder:

- 50–100 new personalized outreaches
- 5–10 first meetings
- 2–3 follow-up or proposal calls
- One hour reviewing what you learned and updating your messaging

## Step 5: Run discovery, not demos

Founders love to show their product. Resist it in the first call. Your goal is to understand whether this company has the problem badly enough to act now.

Questions I find useful:

- "What made you take this call today?"
- "How are you handling this right now?"
- "What does it cost you when it goes wrong?"
- "Who else would be involved in deciding to fix it?"
- "If we solved this, what would need to be true for you to move forward this quarter?"

Listen for urgency, ownership, and budget. If you want a framework for structuring this, my comparison of [BANT vs MEDDIC for qualifying B2B leads](/blog/how-to-qualify-b2b-leads-bant-vs-meddic) explains which questions matter at which deal size. For early-stage founders, a lightweight version is usually enough. Just make sure you know who signs, why now, and what success looks like.

Disqualifying is just as valuable as qualifying. Every hour spent chasing a prospect who will never buy is an hour taken from one who would.

## How should you price your first deals?

This is where many founders make a costly mistake: giving the product away for free. Free pilots attract people who are curious, not committed. When the pilot ends, they often disappear.

Better options:

- **Paid pilot:** a defined scope, a fixed timeframe (for example, 60–90 days), and a clear success metric. Charge something meaningful, even if discounted.
- **Design partner pricing:** a lower price in exchange for regular feedback calls, a testimonial if results are good, and permission to use their logo.
- **Founding customer discount with a time limit:** for example, a reduced rate locked in for the first year, which rewards early risk without devaluing your product forever.

Always attach your discount to something you get back: feedback, a case study, referrals. ## Step 6: Deliver obsessively and turn customers into references

Customers four through ten are much easier to win if customers one through three are thrilled. Early on, delivery *is* sales.

- **Over-communicate.** Weekly check-ins, quick responses, and visible progress.
- **Measure results** against the success metric you agreed on in the pilot.
- **Ask for referrals at the moment of success,** not months later. "You mentioned this saved your team hours each week. Who else in your network deals with the same problem?"
- **Turn wins into short case studies.** Even a few paragraphs with the problem, approach, and result give your next prospects something concrete.

A single strong reference in a tight industry can open more doors than hundreds of cold emails.

## What about selling to companies in Turkey?

If your first customers are Turkish companies, a few points are worth keeping in mind:

- **Relationships matter a lot.** Expect more in-person meetings and more time spent building trust before a decision.
- **Referrals carry significant weight.** A warm introduction often shortens the process considerably.
- **Decision-making is often centralized.** In many mid-sized firms, the owner or general manager makes the final call, so reach them early.
- **Payment terms deserve attention.** Agree on them clearly upfront, especially for services.

None of this changes the core process. It just means your warm network and in-person presence may play a bigger role.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Hiring a salesperson before you've closed anything yourself.** A rep cannot sell what the founder hasn't figured out how to sell.
- **Targeting everyone.** Broad messaging gets ignored.
- **Building more features instead of having more conversations.** Most early "product problems" are actually positioning problems.
- **Free pilots with no success criteria.**
- **Not writing anything down.** Record objections, winning phrases, and lost-deal reasons. This becomes the foundation of your future playbook.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to land the first 10 B2B customers?

It depends on your deal size, sales cycle, and how much time the founder dedicates to selling. For many founders selling mid-priced B2B services with consistent weekly outreach, it takes several months. Larger enterprise deals can take considerably longer.

### Should the founder do sales, or hire someone?

The founder should close the first customers personally. Early sales conversations are how you learn your ICP, messaging, and pricing, and that knowledge cannot be outsourced. Hire once you have repeatable patterns to hand over.

### Is it okay to offer free pilots to get first customers?

It's usually better to offer a paid pilot, even at a discount. Payment signals real commitment and filters out prospects who are only curious. If you discount, ask for something in return, such as feedback, a testimonial, or referrals.

### Is cold outreach still effective for early-stage B2B startups?

Yes, when it's targeted and personalized. Short, relevant messages sent by the founder to a narrow list of well-researched accounts still open conversations. Mass, generic emails to large purchased lists do not.

## Conclusion

Your first 10 B2B customers won't come from a clever growth hack. They come from a narrow ICP, a hand-built list of the right accounts, your warm network, consistent founder-led outreach, honest discovery, and obsessive delivery that turns early buyers into references. Track your numbers, learn from every conversation, and write down what works. That record becomes the playbook your future team will run.
