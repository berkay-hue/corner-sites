The most reliable way to get paid on time by B2B clients in Turkey is to treat payment as part of the sale. Agree on terms, invoicing details and approval steps before the contract is signed, then follow up on every invoice with a fixed, polite and persistent cadence. Late payment in Turkey is rarely about bad intent. It usually comes from long default payment terms (*vade*), monthly payment runs, missing paperwork and inflation, which makes waiting attractive for the buyer. If you design around those realities, most of your collection problems disappear before they start.

I learned this the expensive way. In the early days of Corner Group, I focused on closing deals and assumed money would follow. Some of our most "successful" months on paper were our tightest months in the bank account, because we had signed clients who paid 60, 90 or even 120 days later. This post is the playbook I wish I had back then: what to negotiate, how to invoice, how to follow up, and what to do when a client simply stops paying.

## Why do B2B clients in Turkey pay late?

Before fixing the problem, it helps to understand why it happens. In my experience, late payment in Turkey usually comes from one of five sources:

- **Vade culture.** Many Turkish companies treat 60–90 day terms as normal, especially larger corporates and holding companies. If you don't state your terms clearly, theirs will apply by default.
- **Monthly payment runs.** Many finance departments pay suppliers once or twice a month, often on fixed days. If your invoice misses the cut-off by one day, you can lose a full month.
- **Paperwork gaps.** A missing purchase order number, a wrong tax office, a supplier registration that was never completed, or an e-invoice sent to the wrong address can all stop a payment silently.
- **Inflation incentives.** When prices rise quickly, every month a buyer delays a Turkish lira payment reduces the real value of what they pay. Some finance teams know this very well.
- **No internal owner.** Your champion signed the deal, but they don't control payments. Unless someone inside the client pushes the invoice through, it sits.

Notice that only the last two involve any real "choice" by the client, and even those can be managed. Most delays are process failures, so the fix is mostly process.

## Payment terms are a sales conversation, not a finance afterthought

The biggest mistake founders make is negotiating price hard and then accepting whatever payment terms the client proposes. A 10% discount with 30-day terms is often better for you than full price with 90-day terms, especially in a high-inflation environment.

Bring payment terms into the sales process early:

1. **Mention them in discovery.** When you ask about budget and decision process, also ask: "How does supplier payment usually work on your side? What terms do you typically work with?" You'll learn whether you're dealing with a 15-day startup or a 90-day corporate before you invest weeks in a proposal.
2. **Put them in the proposal.** Payment terms should appear on the pricing page, not hidden in a PDF annex. If you want a refresher on structuring that document, I wrote about [how to write a B2B sales proposal that gets signed](/blog/how-to-write-a-b2b-sales-proposal-that-gets-signed).
3. **Trade, don't give.** If a client asks for 60-day terms, ask for something back: a longer contract, an upfront first payment, or a price adjustment. Terms have value, so treat them that way.
4. **Get the finance contact before signing.** Ask your champion to introduce you to the person in accounting or procurement who handles supplier payments. This one step prevents a large share of future delays.

## What payment terms should you ask for?

There is no single right answer, but here is a framework I use, depending on client type and service model.

### For retainers and monthly services

- **Invoice at the start of the month, payable within 7–15 days.** You're delivering work throughout the month, so you shouldn't also be financing it for two more months.
- **First month paid before work starts.** This filters out clients who aren't serious and protects your onboarding effort, which is usually the most expensive part of the engagement.

### For project-based work

- **Milestone billing**, for example 40% at kickoff, 30% at a midpoint deliverable, 30% at completion.
- Avoid "100% on delivery" for anything longer than a few weeks. Scope creep and "final approval" delays will push your payment back indefinitely.

### For large corporates with fixed policies

Sometimes you won't win the argument. A large company may tell you their system only supports 60 or 90 days. In that case:

- Price the delay in. If you're waiting 90 days for Turkish lira, your effective price is lower than the invoice amount.
- Consider pricing in USD or EUR, or with an indexation clause, paid at the exchange rate on the payment date rather than the invoice date. Discuss the legal and accounting setup with your accountant first.
- Ask whether they have early-payment options or supplier finance programs. Some larger companies do.

I go deeper on currency choices and pricing structures in [how to price B2B services for Turkish companies](/blog/how-to-price-b2b-services-turkish-companies).

## How much does late payment actually cost you?

Founders often underestimate this, so let's put numbers on it.

Imagine you invoice a client 300,000 TL for a quarter of work. If annual inflation is running at, say, 40%, the purchasing power of that money falls by roughly 3% per month. Payment in 90 days instead of 15 means you lose around 7–8% of the real value. That's 20,000–25,000 TL gone, often more than your margin on smaller deals.

Then add the hidden costs:

- Time spent chasing: a founder or account manager spending 3–5 hours per month per slow client.
- Cash-flow stress: delayed salaries, delayed tax payments, or expensive short-term credit to cover the gap.
- Opportunity cost: money you couldn't invest in hiring or growth.

When you see late payment as a real cost line, it becomes much easier to negotiate terms firmly. If cash management in a high-inflation economy is new to you, my post on [the importance of financial literacy in Turkey](/blog/the-importance-of-financial-literacy-in-turkey) is a good starting point.

## How do you make sure your invoice actually gets paid?

Getting the invoice "into the system" is half the battle. Before sending the first invoice to a new client, confirm the following in writing:

1. **Legal entity and tax details.** Exact company title (*ticari unvan*), tax office (*vergi dairesi*), tax number and address. Holding structures often have several entities, and invoicing the wrong one means starting over.
2. **E-invoice or e-archive status.** Check whether the client is registered for e-fatura so your invoice goes through the right channel and lands in the right inbox.
3. **Purchase order or reference number.** Many corporates will not pay an invoice without a PO number on it. Ask for it before you invoice, not after.
4. **Supplier registration.** Some companies require you to be set up as a vendor in their system, with signed forms, bank details and sometimes documents like a tax certificate or signature circular. Do this during onboarding.
5. **Payment run dates.** Ask directly: "Which days of the month do you process supplier payments, and what's the cut-off for invoices?" Then time your invoices accordingly.
6. **Who approves the invoice.** Often your champion needs to confirm "the work was delivered" before finance releases funds. Make sure they know this is expected of them.

I keep a simple onboarding checklist with these six items for every new client. Once it's complete, the invoicing process tends to run on its own.

## A follow-up cadence that gets invoices paid

Most founders either don't follow up at all (because it feels awkward) or follow up angrily after the money is already very late. Neither works. What works is a predictable, friendly cadence that starts **before** the due date.

Here's the sequence I recommend, assuming 30-day terms:

1. **Day 0, invoice sent.** Send the e-invoice plus a short email to your finance contact and champion: invoice number, amount, due date, PO reference, bank details.
2. **Day 20, pre-due reminder.** "Just confirming invoice #123 is in your system and scheduled for the payment run before the 30th. Let me know if anything is missing." This catches paperwork problems while there's still time to fix them.
3. **Day 31, due date passed.** Polite email to finance: "Invoice #123 was due yesterday. Could you share the expected payment date?" Always ask for a date, not just "an update."
4. **Day 38, phone call.** Call the finance contact. Turkish business culture is relationship-driven, and a friendly phone call often achieves in five minutes what three emails didn't.
5. **Day 45, champion escalation.** Message your champion: "We're two weeks past due on #123 and I want to make sure it doesn't affect the project. Could you help me check internally?"
6. **Day 60, founder-to-senior call.** At this point the founder or a senior person calls their counterpart at the client. Keep it calm and factual.
7. **Day 60+, service decision.** Formally decide whether to pause work until payment arrives (more on this below).

You can automate steps 1–3 with your accounting software or a simple workflow tool. Keep the personal steps (4–6) human. If you want to automate parts of this cadence, the same principles apply as in my guide on [how to automate B2B follow-up emails with AI safely](/blog/how-to-automate-b2b-follow-up-emails-with-ai): automate the predictable parts, keep the sensitive conversations personal. At [Corner Group, where we build automation for our own operations](https://cornergroup.co), invoice reminders were one of the first workflows we automated, because they're repetitive, rule-based and easy to forget.

## What should you do when a client is seriously late?

Sometimes the cadence doesn't work. When an invoice is 60+ days overdue and promises keep slipping, escalate in clear stages.

### Stage 1: Pause the work

If your contract allows it, and it should, send a written notice that services will pause on a specific date unless payment is received. Be professional, not emotional: "As per section X of our agreement, we'll pause delivery on [date] until invoices #123 and #124 are settled." Many clients pay within days of receiving this message, because now there's a business consequence.

This is why your contract needs a clause allowing suspension for non-payment, plus a late-payment interest clause. Have a lawyer draft these once and reuse them.

### Stage 2: Formal notice

If pausing doesn't work, the next step in Turkey is usually a formal notice sent through a notary (*ihtarname*). It documents the debt, sets a deadline and signals that you're serious. It's relatively inexpensive and often enough to trigger payment.

### Stage 3: Legal routes

Beyond that, options typically include enforcement proceedings (*icra takibi*) and, for many commercial monetary claims, a mandatory mediation step (*arabuluculuk*) before a lawsuit. The rules, costs and timelines depend on the specifics, so work with a lawyer at this stage rather than improvising. Also be realistic: legal routes take time and money, and they almost always end the relationship. For smaller amounts, a negotiated payment plan may be the smarter outcome.

### Stage 4: Learn and adjust

Every serious late payment should change your process. Was there a warning sign in discovery? Did you skip the finance contact? Did you accept terms you shouldn't have? Write it down and update your checklist.

## Which clients are most likely to pay late?

Over time you'll recognize patterns. Watch for:

- Clients who push hard on payment terms but barely negotiate price.
- Companies where nobody can tell you who handles supplier payments.
- Prospects who want to "start next week" but won't sign or pay a first invoice.
- Businesses with visible financial stress: layoffs, delayed projects, frequent leadership changes.

None of these are automatic deal-breakers, but they should change your terms. Ask for more upfront, shorter terms or smaller initial scope. When you're chasing [your first 10 B2B customers](/blog/first-10-b2b-customers), it's tempting to accept anyone. Even then, a client who doesn't pay is worse than no client, because you're spending delivery costs on revenue that never arrives.

## How do you build a collections habit in a small team?

Collections shouldn't depend on the founder remembering. Set up a few simple habits:

- **Weekly receivables review.** Fifteen minutes every Monday: list every open invoice, its age, and the next action. Assign an owner to each.
- **Track days sales outstanding (DSO).** Roughly, how many days on average it takes to get paid. If it creeps up from 25 to 45 days, you've found a problem before it becomes a crisis.
- **Make account managers co-owners.** The person with the client relationship should know when that client is late. Some companies even tie part of sales commission to *collected* revenue rather than signed revenue, which is worth considering.
- **Separate relationship from collection when needed.** If your account manager is uncomfortable chasing money, let finance or an operations person send the formal reminders while the account manager stays "the friendly face."

## FAQ

### What are normal B2B payment terms in Turkey?

Terms vary widely by company size and sector. Smaller companies and startups often pay within 15–30 days, while large corporates commonly default to 60–90 days. The key is to state your own terms clearly in the proposal and contract, because the client's default will apply if you don't.

### Should I invoice Turkish clients in USD or EUR instead of TL?

Foreign-currency pricing or an indexation clause can protect you from inflation and long payment terms, especially on large or long-term contracts. However, not every client will accept it, and there are legal and accounting considerations. Discuss the setup with your accountant and lawyer before making it your standard.

### Is it acceptable to stop working for a client who hasn't paid?

Yes, provided your contract includes a clause allowing you to suspend services for non-payment and you give clear written notice. Pausing work is often the single most effective way to get an overdue invoice paid. Without that clause, you risk a dispute, so add it to your standard agreement.

### How can I ask for payment without damaging the relationship?

Start reminders before the due date, keep the tone factual and friendly, and always ask for a specific payment date. Involve your finance contact first and your champion second, so the relationship owner isn't the only person chasing. Most clients respect a supplier who manages payments professionally.

## Conclusion

Getting paid on time by B2B clients in Turkey isn't about being aggressive. It's about being clear and organized. Negotiate terms as part of the sale, collect the right invoicing details before the first invoice, follow up on a predictable cadence, and know your escalation steps before you need them. In a high-inflation environment, every day of delay costs real money, so treating collections as a core business process is one of the highest-return habits a founder can build.
