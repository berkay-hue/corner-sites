The honest answer is that **most new B2B sales reps take three to six months to ramp up, and reps selling complex, high-value deals often need six to nine months or more.** The single best predictor of ramp time is your sales cycle. A rep can't be fully productive until they've worked at least one full cycle from first contact to signed contract, plus the few weeks it takes to learn your product, market and process. If your average deal takes 90 days to close, expect a new rep to need roughly four to five months before they hit full quota consistently.

That is the short version. In practice, I've seen two reps join the same team in the same month and ramp at very different speeds. The difference was rarely talent. It was usually onboarding, the quality of the pipeline they inherited, and how clearly the company defined what "ramped" means. This article breaks down how long ramp-up really takes, what speeds it up, how to set quotas during the ramp period, and how to tell early whether a new hire is on track.

## What does "ramped" actually mean?

Before you can measure ramp time, you need a definition. Many founders say "ramped" when they really mean "has stopped asking me basic questions." That isn't a useful benchmark.

I use three levels:

- **Activity-ramped:** The rep runs the full daily workflow on their own. They prospect, book meetings, run discovery calls and update the CRM without hand-holding. This usually takes 3 to 6 weeks.
- **Pipeline-ramped:** The rep consistently generates or progresses enough qualified pipeline to support a full quota. If your pipeline coverage target is 3x quota, a pipeline-ramped rep has that 3x in their own name. This often takes 2 to 4 months.
- **Revenue-ramped:** The rep closes at or near full quota for two or three consecutive months or quarters. This is the real finish line, and it depends heavily on your sales cycle.

When someone asks "how long does ramp take?", they almost always mean the third level. Tracking the first two matters because they are your early warning system.

## How long does ramp-up take for different sales motions?

Ramp time varies a lot with what you sell and who you sell to. These are general ranges I've seen across B2B teams. Treat them as starting assumptions, not guarantees.

- **Transactional SMB sales (deal cycle under 30 days, low price point):** 2 to 3 months to full productivity. The rep gets many repetitions quickly, so learning compounds fast.
- **Mid-market B2B (30 to 90 day cycles, a few stakeholders):** 4 to 6 months. This is where most B2B service and SaaS companies sit.
- **Enterprise or complex sales (90+ day cycles, procurement, multiple decision-makers):** 6 to 12 months. The rep may close only a handful of deals in their first year, so each one carries a lot of weight.
- **SDR / outbound appointment-setting roles:** 1 to 2 months to full meeting quota. The feedback loop is daily, so ramp is much shorter.

A simple rule of thumb: **ramp time ≈ onboarding time + one full sales cycle + a buffer of a few weeks.** If onboarding takes 4 weeks and your cycle is 60 days, plan for about 3.5 to 4 months.

## Why does the sales cycle set the floor?

A new rep's first deals almost always come from pipeline they create after they start, unless you hand them existing opportunities. If a deal takes 90 days to close, a rep who starts prospecting in week three won't see their first closed deal until month four at the earliest. No amount of hustle compresses the buyer's procurement process.

That's why founders who expect a new hire to "pay for themselves in 60 days" in a 90-day cycle business set everyone up for disappointment. The rep feels like a failure, the founder feels misled, and a perfectly good hire walks out the door in month five, just before their pipeline would have converted.

If you want to shorten ramp, you have two real levers. You can give the rep a starter book of warm opportunities, or you can work on the cycle itself. I've written separately about [practical methods to shorten the B2B sales cycle](/blog/how-to-shorten-b2b-sales-cycle). Every week you remove from the cycle comes off ramp time too.

## What slows a new sales rep down?

In my experience, slow ramp has the same handful of causes over and over:

1. **No documented process.** If the "playbook" lives in the founder's head, every new rep reinvents it. They spend months discovering objections, qualification criteria and messaging your best rep already knows.
2. **Empty or poor-quality territory.** A rep handed a CRM full of dead leads and outdated contacts will spend their first month cleaning data instead of selling.
3. **Unclear ideal customer profile.** Reps who don't know exactly who to target chase everyone and qualify no one.
4. **Too much product training, too little selling.** Some companies keep new hires in classroom mode for six weeks. Reps learn faster from real calls than from slide decks.
5. **No structured coaching.** Throwing a rep into the deep end and checking in at the end of the month is how you lose a quarter.
6. **Wrong hire for the motion.** An enterprise seller in a high-velocity SMB role, or the reverse, will struggle no matter how good the onboarding is.

Most of these are fixable before the rep even starts.

## How do you build a ramp plan that actually works?

Here's the structure I recommend for a mid-market B2B rep. Adjust the timeline to your cycle length.

### Before day one

- Write down your sales process, ideal customer profile, qualification framework and top ten objections. If you haven't done this yet, my guide on [how to write a sales playbook for your team](/blog/how-to-write-a-sales-playbook) walks through it step by step.
- Prepare a clean territory or account list. Verify contact data before the rep inherits it. A tool like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) can help build and refresh B2B contact lists, but someone still needs to check the output against your ICP.
- Set up CRM access, email, calendar, call recording and any sequences the rep will use.
- Pick 3 to 5 "starter" opportunities, warm leads or inbound inquiries, that the rep can work in month one.

### Weeks 1–2: Learn by listening

- Spend no more than 40% of the time on product and market training. The rest goes to shadowing.
- Listen to at least 10 to 15 recorded calls: good ones, bad ones, discovery, demos and negotiations.
- Shadow live calls with your best rep or the founder.
- By the end of week two, the rep should be able to deliver your pitch and answer the top five objections in a role-play.

### Weeks 3–4: Start selling with a safety net

- The rep begins prospecting and running discovery calls, with a manager or senior rep joining the first few.
- Set daily activity targets. For example: 40 to 60 outbound touches per day, or whatever your proven benchmark is.
- Run a short debrief after every live call in week three. Shift to daily or every-other-day reviews in week four.

### Months 2–3: Build their own pipeline

- The rep owns their pipeline fully. The manager reviews it weekly.
- Focus on pipeline-ramp metrics: qualified opportunities created, stage progression, meeting-to-opportunity conversion.
- The first deals from starter opportunities should start closing here.

### Months 4–6: Close and stabilize

- Deals created in month two begin to close.
- Quota steps up toward 100%.
- Coaching shifts from "how to run a call" to deal strategy, negotiation and forecasting.

The coaching cadence matters more than most founders think. Too little and the rep flounders. Too much and they never build confidence. I've covered the balance in [how to coach sales reps without micromanaging them](/blog/how-to-coach-sales-reps-without-micromanaging).

## How should you set quotas during the ramp period?

Giving a new rep a full quota in month one is demoralizing and pointless. Giving them no quota at all removes urgency. The answer is a **ramped quota**: a stepped target that rises as they gain experience.

A common structure for a 4 to 6 month ramp:

- **Month 1:** 0% revenue quota. Activity and learning targets only.
- **Month 2:** 25% of full quota.
- **Month 3:** 50% of full quota.
- **Month 4:** 75% of full quota.
- **Month 5 onward:** 100% of full quota.

For a team where full quota is ₺500,000 per month in new revenue, that would look like ₺0, ₺125,000, ₺250,000, ₺375,000, then ₺500,000. The numbers are illustrative; use your own.

Two important points:

1. **Pair the revenue quota with leading-indicator targets** in the first 2 to 3 months. A rep at 50% revenue quota who has built 3x pipeline coverage is in good shape. A rep at 50% with no pipeline behind them is not.
2. **Protect commission during ramp.** Many companies offer a guaranteed draw or a higher base for the first few months so the rep isn't financially punished for the learning curve.

For a deeper look at quota design, see my practical guide on [how to set sales quotas for a sales team](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quotas-for-sales-team).

## Which leading indicators show a rep is on track?

You shouldn't have to wait six months to find out whether a hire is working. These are the metrics I watch, roughly in the order they become meaningful:

- **Weeks 1–4:** Activity volume, call quality in reviews, CRM hygiene, speed of learning objection handling.
- **Months 1–2:** Meetings booked, discovery-to-opportunity conversion rate, quality of qualification notes.
- **Months 2–3:** Pipeline value created relative to target coverage, average deal size compared to team average, stage progression speed.
- **Months 3–6:** Win rate, actual revenue against ramped quota, forecast accuracy.

Compare each new hire against how your best existing reps performed at the same stage, not against their current numbers. If your top rep had 8 qualified opportunities at the end of month two and your new hire has 7, they're doing well, even though they're nowhere near the top rep's current pipeline.

## How much does a slow ramp actually cost?

This is where founders often underestimate the stakes. Let's take a simple illustrative example.

Say a rep costs you ₺90,000 per month fully loaded (salary, taxes, tools, overhead) and is expected to generate ₺500,000 in monthly revenue once ramped. If they ramp in 4 months instead of 7, you gain three extra months of full productivity. That's ₺1,500,000 in revenue capacity, against the same salary cost.

## When should you worry that a rep won't make it?

Not every hire works out, and the kindest thing for everyone is to recognize it early. Warning signs I take seriously:

- **Low activity after week four**, without a clear reason. Skill can be coached. Effort is much harder.
- **No improvement between call reviews.** The same feedback comes up three or four times in a row.
- **Pipeline below 50% of target at the end of month three**, with no clear external cause such as a bad territory.
- **Defensiveness toward coaching.** Reps who argue with every piece of feedback rarely ramp quickly.

If you see two or more of these signals, have a direct conversation. Set a specific 30-day improvement plan with measurable targets. Sometimes this resets things. Sometimes it confirms what you suspected. Either way, it's better than drifting into month eight with no clarity.

## FAQ

### What is a good ramp time for a B2B sales rep?

For most mid-market B2B roles, four to six months to full quota is a healthy ramp time. Transactional sales can be faster, around two to three months, while enterprise roles often take six to twelve months. The best benchmark is your own sales cycle length plus about one month of onboarding.

### Can you shorten sales rep ramp time?

Yes. The biggest levers are a documented sales playbook, a clean and well-defined territory, a handful of warm starter opportunities, and structured weekly coaching. Shortening your overall sales cycle also shortens ramp, because a rep can't be fully productive until they've completed at least one full cycle.

### Should new sales reps have a quota in their first month?

Most new reps shouldn't carry a revenue quota in month one, but they should have clear activity and learning targets. A stepped quota that starts at 0% and rises to 100% over four or five months keeps urgency high without punishing the natural learning curve.

### Does previous experience shorten ramp time?

Relevant experience helps, especially if the rep has sold to the same buyer persona or in a similar sales motion. Even experienced reps still need to learn your product, market and process, so expect a shorter ramp, not an instant one. Reps switching between very different motions, such as enterprise to SMB, can take as long as less experienced hires.

## Conclusion

A new sales rep's ramp time isn't a mystery. It's mostly the sum of your onboarding quality and your sales cycle length. Define what "ramped" means at each stage, prepare the territory and playbook before day one, use a stepped quota, and track leading indicators from the first month. Do those things and most mid-market reps will reach full productivity in four to six months. You'll also know early which hires need more support and which aren't the right fit.
