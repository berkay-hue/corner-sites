The best way to ask for a raise as a sales rep is to treat it like a deal. Build a business case from your numbers, ask at a moment when your value is easy to see, name a specific figure, and walk your manager through it the way you'd walk a buyer through a proposal. Reps who show up with quota attainment, pipeline data and a clear ask get better outcomes than reps who show up with "I feel underpaid."

I've sat on both sides of this conversation, first as a rep and later as a founder approving (and declining) raise requests at Corner Group. The requests that worked were almost never the most emotional or the most aggressive. They were the most prepared. Below is the process I'd follow if I were a rep today, plus word-for-word scripts for every stage, from booking the meeting to handling a "no."

## Why is asking for a raise different in sales?

Sales is one of the few roles where your contribution shows up as a number. That works for you and against you.

**It works for you** because you don't have to argue about vague impact. If you closed 118% of quota for three straight quarters, that's a fact, not an opinion.

**It works against you** because many managers believe the comp plan already rewards performance. Their reasoning goes: "You hit 118%, so you earned more commission. Why would we also raise your base?" You need an answer for that before you walk in. We'll get to the script below.

There's also a structural point. In sales, a "raise" can mean several different things:

- A higher **base salary**
- A higher **OTE** (on-target earnings), usually through a bigger variable component
- Better **commission rates** or accelerators above quota
- A **title change** that moves you into a new pay band
- A **better territory or account list**, which raises your realistic earnings without changing the plan

Knowing which lever you want, and which ones you'd accept, gives you room to negotiate instead of hitting a single yes/no wall.

## When is the right time to ask for a raise?

Timing matters more in sales than almost anywhere else, because your perceived value moves with the quarter.

The best windows are:

1. **Right after a strong quarter closes.** Your number is fresh, verified and hard to dispute. Asking two to three weeks after quarter end, once the results are in the system, is ideal.
2. **Four to eight weeks before budget planning.** If your company sets compensation budgets in November for the following year, a conversation in late September can still shape the numbers. A conversation in January asks your manager to find money that's already been allocated.
3. **After you've taken on more scope.** Maybe you're mentoring two new hires, running a key account or covering a second territory. Any of these changes what your role is actually worth.
4. **During formal review cycles**, as long as you prepare ahead and don't let the review happen *to* you.

The worst windows are the last two weeks of a quarter (your manager is focused on the forecast), right after you missed quota, or in the middle of a company-wide cost-cutting push.

A note for reps in Turkey: many companies run salary adjustments once or twice a year, often tied to inflation. Understand the difference between an **inflation adjustment** and a **merit raise**. An inflation adjustment keeps your purchasing power roughly flat. A raise is what you get on top of that for increased value. Don't let the first be presented as the second.

## How do you build your case?

Before any conversation, put together what I call an evidence pack. It should fit on one page, because your manager may need to forward it to finance or the founder.

### Your core performance numbers

- Quota attainment for the last 3–4 quarters (e.g., 104%, 112%, 121%, 109%)
- Your rank on the team, if it's favorable
- Average deal size and how it has changed
- Win rate and sales cycle length compared with the team average
- Revenue retained or expanded from your accounts

### Your leading indicators

Managers trust results more when the activity behind them is visible. Include pipeline coverage, meetings booked per month, and conversion between stages. If your company has clearly defined pipeline stages, use the same language your manager uses in forecasting.

### Your contribution beyond the number

This is where most reps leave money on the table. List things like:

- Onboarding or shadowing support for new reps
- Playbooks, email templates or objection-handling docs others now use
- Product feedback that shaped a feature or pricing decision
- Strategic logos you brought in that help marketing or future sales

### Your market data

Find out what similar roles pay. Talk to recruiters, peers at other companies, and job postings that list salary ranges. You're looking for a defensible range for your role, seniority and city, not a single magic number. If you've recently gone through the hiring market, the same research I describe in [negotiating a sales job offer in Turkey](/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-sales-job-offer-in-turkey) applies here.

## How much should you ask for?

Pick a specific number, not a vague range, and anchor slightly above what you'd happily accept.

A practical approach:

1. Define your **target**, the number that would make you feel fairly paid.
2. Define your **walk-away point**, the minimum below which you'd start looking elsewhere.
3. Ask for a figure roughly 5–10% above your target, so there's room to meet in the middle.

**Example:** Your base is 60,000 per year (in whatever currency you're paid). Market data suggests reps at your level earn 66,000–72,000 base. Your target is 70,000, your walk-away is 65,000, and you ask for 74,000. If your manager counters at 70,000, you've landed on your target. Both of you can call that a fair outcome.

If you're asking for OTE changes, be precise about the split. "I'd like my OTE to move from 100,000 to 115,000, with base going from 60,000 to 66,000 and variable from 40,000 to 49,000" is much easier to act on than "I'd like more commission."

## The scripts

These are starting points. Adjust them to your voice and your relationship with your manager. The structure matters more than the exact wording.

### Script 1: Requesting the meeting

Don't ambush your manager at the end of a 1:1 about pipeline. Ask for a dedicated slot.

> "Hi Elif, I'd like to book 30 minutes in the next two weeks to talk about my compensation and growth here. I'll bring a short summary of my results from the past year. Would Thursday or next Tuesday work?"

This signals you're serious and prepared, and it gives your manager time to check budget before the meeting. That's a good thing.

### Script 2: Opening the conversation

Start with commitment and evidence, then the ask. Don't apologize and don't over-explain.

> "Thanks for making time. I want to start by saying I like working here and I want to keep growing with the team. Over the past four quarters I've hit 104, 112, 121 and 109% of quota, and I've closed three of our five largest new logos this year. I've also been onboarding our two newest reps, which wasn't part of my original role.
>
> Based on that and on what I'm seeing in the market for similar roles, I'd like to discuss increasing my base from 60,000 to 74,000. I've put the details on one page here."

Then stop talking. Silence after the ask is uncomfortable, but it's your manager's turn.

### Script 3: "Your commission already rewards your performance"

This is the most common pushback in sales. Have the answer ready.

> "I agree the commission plan rewards hitting the number, and I've earned that. What I'm asking about is the role itself. My base reflects the job I was hired into: a rep with my own territory. Today I'm also onboarding new hires and handling our largest accounts. That's a bigger role, and I think the base should reflect the role, not just the results."

The distinction between *paying for results* (variable) and *paying for the role* (base) is the key argument. Most managers accept it once it's spelled out.

### Script 4: "There's no budget right now"

Sometimes this is true. Sometimes it's a reflex. Either way, move from "yes/no" to "how and when."

> "I understand budget is tight. Could we agree on what it would take, and by when? For example, if I hit 110% again this quarter and keep supporting onboarding, could we commit to an increase to 70,000 effective at the start of next quarter? I'd like to have that in writing so we're both clear."

Alternatively, ask for non-salary levers:

> "If base isn't possible right now, could we look at a higher accelerator above 100%, or moving me onto the enterprise account list?"

### Script 5: "Let's revisit in six months"

A delay without criteria is usually a soft no. Turn it into a plan.

> "I'm open to that. To make sure we're aligned, can we define what success looks like by then? I'd suggest three things: quota attainment above 105% for the two quarters, both new reps fully ramped, and one documented playbook for the team. If I deliver those, can we agree the increase happens at the six-month mark?"

Then put it in a follow-up email (Script 7).

### Script 6: When you have a competing offer

Only use this if the offer is real and you'd actually consider taking it. Bluffing works until it doesn't, and then you're the rep everyone knows was bluffing.

> "I want to be transparent. I've received an offer from another company at a base of 75,000. I'd prefer to stay. I like the team, the product and where we're going. I'm not trying to start a bidding war. I'm sharing it because it gives a clear market reference. Is there a way we can close some of that gap?"

### Script 7: The follow-up email

Send this within 24 hours, whatever the outcome.

> "Hi Elif, thanks for the conversation today. To summarize what we agreed: my base will move from 60,000 to 70,000, effective 1 April. We'll review OTE again at mid-year, with the target of quota attainment above 105% and full ramp for both new hires. Let me know if I've missed anything. Appreciate your support."

Written follow-up protects you if your manager changes roles or forgets. It also makes it easier for them to push the approval through.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Comparing yourself to a named colleague.** "Ahmet earns more than me" makes it personal and puts your manager in an awkward spot. Use market data instead.
- **Leading with personal needs.** Rent and inflation are real, but the business pays for value, not expenses. Lead with value and mention cost-of-living only as context.
- **Asking after a bad quarter** and hoping your track record carries you. Wait until the latest data supports you.
- **Accepting a vague promise.** "We'll take care of you" is not a commitment. Get numbers and dates.
- **Threatening to leave without meaning it.** It may get you a short-term raise and a long-term reputation as a flight risk.
- **Going over your manager's head first.** Even if your manager lacks final authority, they should be the one carrying your case upward.

## What if the answer is still no?

A "no" is information. Ask what it would take, get the criteria in writing, and give the company a reasonable window to act. One or two quarters is fair.

If nothing changes after you've delivered against agreed criteria, that tells you something about how the company values sales talent. Good sales organizations have clear, visible paths. If you're curious what that looks like from the management side, I wrote about [building a sales career path reps actually follow](/blog/how-to-build-a-sales-career-path). Compare your company against it honestly.

Use the time to sharpen your materials. Your evidence pack is most of a strong CV already, and the advice in [writing a sales CV that gets interviews](/blog/how-to-write-a-sales-cv-that-gets-interviews) will help you turn it into one.

If you're a manager reading this, the lesson runs the other way. Make raise criteria transparent, talk about compensation regularly in your [sales 1:1 meetings](/blog/how-to-run-sales-1-1-meetings-that-work), and don't make your best reps negotiate from a position of mystery.

## FAQ

### How often can a sales rep ask for a raise?

Once a year is the norm for a formal raise request. Asking after a major change in scope, like taking on a new territory or a team-lead role, is also reasonable. Asking more than twice a year without a significant change in your role or results usually weakens your position.

### Should I ask for a higher base salary or a higher commission?

It depends on your risk tolerance and how predictable your pipeline is. A higher base rewards the role and gives stability. Better commission or accelerators reward top performance and can be easier for a company to approve because they're tied to revenue. Many reps get the best outcome by asking for a moderate base increase plus a better accelerator above quota.

### Is it okay to ask for a raise if I didn't hit quota?

It's harder but not impossible, especially if the miss was caused by factors outside your control, like a territory change, product issues or a market downturn. In that case, focus on leading indicators, relative performance versus the team, and contributions beyond your number. Often the smarter move is to agree on clear criteria now and make the formal ask once you've hit them.

### How do I ask for a raise in a small startup with limited budget?

Be direct about the numbers, but flexible on the form. Startups can sometimes offer equity, a title change, better accounts, or a guaranteed increase tied to a funding milestone or revenue target. Get any future commitment in writing with a specific date or trigger.

## Conclusion

Asking for a raise as a sales rep is a sales conversation, and you already know how to run those. Do the discovery (market data, your numbers, your expanded scope). Prepare the proposal (a one-page evidence pack and a specific figure). Handle objections calmly (base versus variable, budget, timing). Close with clear next steps in writing. Most reps who follow this process walk away with either a raise or a concrete, dated plan to get one, and both are far better than staying quiet and getting resentful.
