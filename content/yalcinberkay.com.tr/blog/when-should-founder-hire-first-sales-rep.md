Hire your first sales rep when you have proven that selling works, not when you are tired of selling. You should know who buys and why, be able to write the process down, show the numbers, and see that your own capacity has become the limit on growth. Before that point, a rep is a costly experiment.

Every founder I talk to reaches the same moment. Sales calls fill your calendar, product decisions wait in Slack, investors want updates, and you start thinking: *"I need to hire a salesperson so I can get back to building the company."* The instinct is understandable. But hiring your first sales rep at the wrong time is one of the most expensive mistakes an early-stage B2B company can make. You lose money and months. Worse, you can come away believing that "sales doesn't work for us" when the real problem was timing and preparation.

I've built outbound teams for my own companies and worked with many founders on the same question. My answer is not a date on the calendar or a revenue number. It is a set of conditions. When those conditions are in place, your first sales hire can multiply what you've built. When they're missing, even a very talented rep will struggle. Here is how I think about it.

## The Short Answer: Later Than You Want, Earlier Than You Think

Most founders get the timing wrong in one of two ways.

The first group hires **too early**. They have a handful of friendly customers, no clear idea why those customers bought, and they hope a "real salesperson" will find the answers. That rarely happens. A sales rep is good at running a process. They are usually not the right person to *invent* the process, especially without the founder's authority, vision, and product knowledge.

The second group hires **too late**. They have a working motion and steady demand. But they keep closing every deal themselves because "nobody can sell it like I do." Growth stalls at the founder's personal capacity, and the company misses its window.

The sweet spot sits between these two. You have proven that the product can be sold repeatedly, but you haven't yet become the bottleneck that slows the whole business down.

## Signs You Are Not Ready Yet

Before we look at the green lights, be honest about the red ones. If several of these describe your company, keep selling yourself for now.

- **You can't explain why customers buy.** If you asked your last five customers why they chose you, would you hear five different answers? Then you don't have a repeatable story yet.
- **Most deals came from your personal network.** Warm introductions and former colleagues are a great start. They don't prove that strangers will buy.
- **Your pricing changes with every deal.** A rep needs a price list and clear discount rules, not a negotiation from scratch each time.
- **The product changes a lot after each sale.** If every closed deal triggers custom development, you are still doing discovery, not sales.
- **You have fewer than 10 paying customers from outside your network.** This isn't a magic number. It's a practical minimum for spotting patterns.
- **You don't have six months of runway set aside for the hire.** A new rep needs time to ramp. If the hire has to pay for itself in 60 days, the pressure will distort everything.

None of this is a failure. It means the founder's job right now is to keep learning directly from the market. Listening closely to buyers is the foundation for everything else. I wrote more about this in my piece on [why customer feedback is so powerful in B2B](/blog/the-power-of-customer-feedback-in-b2b).

## Signals That You Are Ready

Now the green lights. When most of these are true, it's time to start the hiring process.

1. **You've closed at least 10–20 deals with a consistent pattern.** Similar company size, similar buyer title, similar pain point, similar sales cycle length.
2. **You can describe your ideal customer profile in two sentences.** For example: "Logistics companies with 50–300 employees that run their own fleet and still track deliveries in spreadsheets. The buyer is the operations director."
3. **You know your sales cycle.** You can say with reasonable confidence that a typical deal takes, say, 30–60 days from first call to signature, with roughly three to four meetings.
4. **Your win rate on qualified opportunities is stable.** It doesn't matter much whether it's 20% or 35%. What matters is that it's predictable.
5. **You're spending more than 50% of your week on sales activities.** Product, hiring, and fundraising are starting to suffer.
6. **You're leaving pipeline on the table.** Leads go uncontacted. Follow-ups slip. Demo requests wait days for a reply.
7. **You can afford the ramp period.** You can fund the role for at least two quarters before expecting it to break even.

The sixth signal matters most to me. If demand is clearly bigger than your capacity, a rep fills a real gap. If you're hoping the rep will *create* demand from nothing, you're placing a much riskier bet.

## Write the Playbook Before You Hire

The biggest favor you can do your first rep is to hand them a written playbook on day one. It doesn't need to be beautiful. A shared document of 10–15 pages is enough. It should cover:

- **Ideal customer profile and buyer personas:** industries, company size, titles, and the triggers that make someone a good prospect.
- **The problem statement:** the pain you solve, in the words your customers actually use.
- **Discovery questions:** the 8–10 questions that reliably qualify or disqualify a lead.
- **Common objections and your best answers:** price, timing, "we're already using X," "we'll build it ourselves."
- **Pricing and discount rules:** who can approve what, and when.
- **Sales stages and exit criteria:** what has to be true before a deal moves from "discovery" to "proposal."
- **Two or three call recordings:** your best calls, so the rep can hear the motion, not just read about it.
- **Prospecting sources:** where leads come from, which channels work, and which don't.

If you're running outbound, include your list-building method too. Many founders build their first lists themselves with a tool like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya), filtering by industry, headcount, and title. Write down the exact filters and messaging that got replies. Your rep can then start from proven ground instead of guessing.

If you struggle to write this document, that's useful information. It usually means the motion isn't repeatable yet. For a deeper walkthrough of building the full system, see my guide on [how to build an outbound sales team from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-outbound-sales-team-from-scratch).

## Who Should Your First Sales Hire Be?

Founders often ask whether they should hire an SDR, an account executive, or a VP of Sales first. My general view:

### Don't hire a VP of Sales first

A senior sales leader is excellent at scaling and managing a team that already exists. Most are not excited to cold-call, run demos, and build lists by hand. Many are also no longer sharp at it. Hiring a VP to "figure out sales" before there is anything to manage often ends with an expensive, frustrated executive and very little pipeline.

### Be careful with a pure SDR as the first hire

An SDR books meetings. If the founder still has to run every meeting and close every deal, you've only moved the bottleneck. An SDR-first approach can work when you have plenty of closing capacity but weak top-of-funnel. That situation is less common than founders think.

### The best first hire is usually a "full-cycle" builder

For most early B2B companies, the right profile is a **full-cycle account executive with startup DNA**. This person can prospect, qualify, demo, and close, and is comfortable working without a perfect process. Look for:

- Experience selling to a similar buyer, even if the product was different
- Evidence of building something from scratch: a new territory, a new segment, a first sales role elsewhere
- Curiosity about the product and the customer, not only the commission plan
- Comfort with ambiguity and the habit of writing things down

Some founders hire two reps at the same time instead of one. The logic is sound. With one rep, you can't tell whether a bad result comes from the person or the process. With two, you have a comparison. If you can afford it, this is worth considering.

## Do the Math Before You Sign the Offer

Here is a simple way to check whether the hire makes financial sense. The numbers below are an illustration, not a benchmark. Plug in your own.

Suppose your average contract value is **$12,000 per year**. Your rep's on-target earnings are **$80,000**, split 50/50 between base and commission. Add taxes, tools, and benefits, and the fully loaded cost is about **$100,000 per year**.

A common rule of thumb is that a fully ramped rep should bring in new annual revenue of roughly **three to five times their fully loaded cost**. In this example, that means $300,000–$500,000 in new ARR per year. That works out to about **25–40 deals a year**, or roughly **two to three deals per month** once ramped.

Now ask yourself:

- Does your pipeline support two to three closed deals per month for one person, given your win rate? With a 25% win rate, that means about **8–12 qualified opportunities per month** for that rep.
- How long will ramp take? For a 30–60 day sales cycle, expect **three to six months** before the rep reaches full productivity.
- Can you cover that ramp period without panic?

If the math only works under perfect assumptions, wait. If it works under conservative assumptions, move forward.

## Set Up the First 90 Days

A good hire can still fail without structure. Here is a simple 30/60/90 framework I like:

**Days 1–30: Learn and shadow**
- Read the playbook, use the product, and talk to at least three existing customers.
- Sit in on 10+ of the founder's sales calls.
- Start light prospecting with founder review of messaging.

**Days 31–60: Run with support**
- Run discovery calls and demos, with the founder joining the important ones.
- Own a defined segment or lead source.
- Hit activity and meeting targets, not yet revenue targets.

**Days 61–90: Own the cycle**
- Run deals end-to-end, with the founder as a closing resource only when needed.
- Have a first revenue target, even a modest one.
- Contribute improvements back to the playbook.

Throughout this period, hold a short weekly pipeline meeting. It's the best way to coach, spot problems early, and keep your forecast honest. I've shared the format I use in [how to run a weekly pipeline review that actually works](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-guide).

## What About Outsourcing or AI?

Not every company needs a full-time rep as its first move. If your main constraint is top-of-funnel and not closing, there are other options:

- **An outsourced outbound partner** can test messaging, segments, and channels before you commit to a permanent hire. - **AI tools** can now handle research, list enrichment, first-draft emails, and call summaries. They can stretch a founder's capacity for a while. They don't replace human judgment in complex B2B deals. I covered where the line sits in [my practical answer on whether AI can replace SDRs](/blog/can-ai-replace-your-sdrs).

Treat these options as ways to *learn faster and hire better*, not as permanent excuses to avoid building a team.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Hiring a friend or a "great personality" without a sales track record.** Likeability matters. Proof of performance matters more.
- **Disappearing after the hire.** The founder must stay close to sales for at least the first six months.
- **Unclear territory or lead ownership.** Disputes over "whose deal is it" destroy trust fast.
- **Commission plans that are too complex.** If the rep can't calculate their own paycheck, the plan won't drive behavior.
- **Judging too early.** Evaluate activity and learning in the first 60 days, and revenue after the ramp.
- **Judging too late.** If a rep is clearly not working after six months with good support, act decisively.

## Conclusion

The right time to hire your first sales rep isn't when you're tired of selling. It's when you have proven that selling works. You know who buys and why. You can write it down. You can show the numbers. And your own capacity has become the limit on growth. Before that point, a rep is a costly experiment. After it, a rep is a growth engine.

So keep selling until the patterns are clear. Then document everything, do the math honestly, and hire a builder rather than a manager. Stay close for the first 90 days. Get those pieces right, and your first sales hire can become the foundation of a real sales team.

## FAQ

As a practical minimum, you should have at least 10 paying customers from outside your personal network before hiring your first sales rep. Ideally, you've closed 10–20 deals with a consistent pattern: similar company size, similar buyer title, similar pain point and similar sales cycle length. This isn't a magic number, but it's roughly what you need to spot patterns a rep can repeat.

### Should a startup's first sales hire be an SDR, an AE or a VP of Sales?
