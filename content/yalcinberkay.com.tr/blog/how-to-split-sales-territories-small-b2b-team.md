For a small B2B team, the best way to split sales territories is to build a complete list of target accounts, score those accounts by potential value, and divide them so each rep gets **roughly equal opportunity** rather than an equal number of accounts or an equal slice of the map. With two to six reps, use one primary dimension (industry, company size, or named accounts) instead of complex hybrid models. Then write clear rules for who owns what, and review the split every six to twelve months. A good territory plan prevents reps from fighting over the same prospects, makes quotas defensible, and lets you see quickly whether a rep or a territory is underperforming.

I've watched small teams skip this step because "we're only three people, we'll just talk to each other." That works for about a quarter. Then two reps email the same CFO in the same week, a big inbound lead gets claimed by whoever answered Slack first, and your best rep quietly cherry-picks the easy accounts while the newer hire grinds through leftovers. This guide is the process I'd use to avoid all of that, with numbers and a worked example you can adapt.

## Do you actually need territories with only two or three reps?

Yes, although they should be lightweight. Territories are not bureaucracy. They answer three questions that cause real friction:

- **Who is allowed to contact this account?**
- **Who gets credit (and commission) if it closes?**
- **Is each rep's quota achievable given what they've been given?**

If you can't answer those clearly, you'll pay for it in duplicated outreach, damaged prospect relationships, and arguments at commission time. Prospects notice when two people from the same company pitch them with different messages. It looks disorganized, and in markets where relationships matter a lot (Turkey being a strong example) it can cost you the deal.

The one exception is a team in a pure discovery phase. If you've just moved past [founder-led sales](/blog/when-to-stop-founder-led-sales) and don't yet know which segment converts best, a short period of shared, round-robin coverage can help you learn. Put an end date on that period, usually one or two quarters, and then assign territories.

## What are the main ways to split sales territories?

There are five common models. A small team should usually pick one as the primary split.

### 1. Geography

Reps own regions, cities, or countries. This is the classic model, and it makes sense when you sell in person or when language and time zones matter. A team selling into Turkey and the Gulf, for example, might split by country because of language, working week, and buying culture.

**Downside:** Opportunity is rarely spread evenly across a map. In Turkey, Istanbul alone holds a large share of corporate headquarters. A "Marmara vs. rest of Turkey" split almost always produces one overloaded rep and one starving rep.

### 2. Industry or vertical

Reps own sectors such as logistics, manufacturing, fintech, or healthcare. This is often the strongest model for small B2B teams because reps build real expertise. They learn the jargon, the common objections, and the reference customers, and their conversion rates improve as a result.

**Downside:** Verticals differ in size and buying speed, so balancing takes work.

### 3. Company size or segment

One rep handles SMB, another mid-market, another enterprise. This works well when deal sizes and sales cycles differ dramatically between segments. A 20-employee company might buy in two weeks, while a 2,000-employee company takes six months.

**Downside:** It creates handoff friction when a company grows, and enterprise reps can go months without a close, which is hard on morale and cash flow.

### 4. Named accounts

Each rep gets a specific list of companies, often 50–300 depending on deal size and sales motion. This is the most precise model and works very well for account-based selling with a defined total market.

**Downside:** It needs good data upkeep. Accounts get acquired, go out of business, or change their buying team.

### 5. Round-robin or pooled

Leads are distributed in rotation, and no one owns a segment. This is fine for high-volume inbound with short cycles, and as a temporary learning phase.

**Downside:** No one builds deep ownership of an account, and outbound becomes chaotic.

**My default recommendation for a small outbound B2B team:** use named accounts grouped by vertical. Reps get the specialization benefit of verticals and the precision of named lists.

## How to split sales territories step by step

### Step 1: Build your total account universe

You can't divide what you haven't counted. Start with your ideal customer profile (industry, employee range, region, tech stack, or other qualifying signals) and pull every company that fits.

For most small B2B teams this means exporting from a data provider into your CRM. A tool like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) lets you filter by industry, headcount, and location and export the matching accounts with their key contacts. If you'd rather not build and clean these lists yourself, a team like [Sales Corner, which specializes in data-driven B2B lead generation](https://salescorner.co), can build the account universe for you.

Aim for a single, de-duplicated list. Let's say you end up with **1,200 accounts** that match your ICP.

### Step 2: Score every account by potential

Not all 1,200 accounts are equal. Give each one a simple score. A practical small-team approach is three tiers:

- **Tier A (high potential):** Strong ICP fit, larger expected deal, active buying signals (hiring for relevant roles, recent funding, expansion). Weight = 3.
- **Tier B (medium):** Good fit, average deal size. Weight = 2.
- **Tier C (low):** Fits on paper, but smaller or with no signals. Weight = 1.

Don't overengineer this. A tiering you can finish in a day beats a 15-variable model that never gets built. If you have historical data, look at which closed-won deals looked like at the start and score toward that profile.

### Step 3: Choose your primary split dimension

Ask yourself one question: **what knowledge most improves a rep's win rate?** If prospects in different industries have completely different problems, split by vertical. If the difference that matters is deal complexity, split by company size. If you sell in person across distant regions, geography may matter most.

### Step 4: Balance by weighted opportunity, not account count

This is the step most teams get wrong. Giving each rep 400 accounts sounds fair, but if one rep's 400 includes 80 Tier A accounts and another's includes 20, you've decided their results before the quarter starts.

Calculate a **weighted score** for each territory:

> Territory score = (Tier A × 3) + (Tier B × 2) + (Tier C × 1)

Then adjust the boundaries until the scores are within roughly 10% of each other. You'll see a full example below.

### Step 5: Write the rules of engagement

Territory lines only work if the edge cases are decided in advance. Write down rules for:

- **Inbound leads:** Does an inbound lead from a rep's territory go to that rep, or to a separate inbound owner?
- **Subsidiaries and groups:** In Turkey, large holding companies own businesses across many sectors. If the logistics arm and the energy arm of the same holding sit in different reps' verticals, who owns the relationship at group level?
- **Referrals:** If a customer in Rep A's territory refers a company in Rep B's territory, does Rep A get a referral credit?
- **Existing customers:** Does the closing rep keep the account for expansion, or does it move to an account manager?
- **Dormant accounts:** If a rep hasn't touched an account in 90 days, can someone else claim it?

That last rule matters more than people expect. Without it, reps hoard accounts they'll never work.

### Step 6: Encode it in your CRM

Every account should have a single owner field in your CRM. Ownership should never live in a spreadsheet next to the CRM, and never only in someone's head. Set up assignment rules so new accounts and inbound leads route automatically. If your current system can't do basic owner-based routing, that's a sign it may be time to revisit [how to choose a CRM for a small sales team](/blog/how-to-choose-crm-for-small-sales-team).

### Step 7: Tie quotas and compensation to the territory

Once territories are balanced, set quotas from the territory's potential rather than from a top-down number divided equally. A rep with a 15% smaller territory score should not carry the same quota as everyone else. I cover the mechanics in [this practical guide to setting sales quotas](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quotas-for-sales-team). Make sure your [commission plan](/blog/how-to-design-a-sales-commission-plan) states clearly how split deals, referrals, and territory changes are paid.

## A worked example: three reps selling B2B software in Turkey

Imagine a company selling operations software to mid-sized Turkish businesses. They have three reps and the 1,200 accounts from Step 1, split across four verticals:

| Vertical | Tier A | Tier B | Tier C | Weighted score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing | 60 | 140 | 200 | 620 |
| Logistics | 40 | 90 | 120 | 420 |
| Retail & e-commerce | 30 | 110 | 160 | 410 |
| Healthcare | 20 | 60 | 70 | 250 |
| **Total** | **150** | **400** | **550** | **1,700** |

With three reps, each territory should carry roughly **567 points** (1,700 ÷ 3).

The naive version gives one vertical to each rep and the leftover to whoever has space. That leaves one rep with manufacturing (620) and another with healthcare (250). The manufacturing rep would have more than twice the opportunity of the healthcare rep.

A balanced version looks like this:

- **Rep 1: Manufacturing (620).** Slightly above target. Move about 15 Tier B manufacturing accounts in a specific sub-sector, such as packaging, to Rep 3. New score: about 590.
- **Rep 2: Logistics + half of healthcare (420 + 125 = 545).** Logistics and healthcare distribution overlap naturally, so the split makes sense to the rep.
- **Rep 3: Retail & e-commerce + remaining healthcare + packaging accounts (410 + 125 + 30 = 565).**

The final scores of 590, 545, and 565 all fall within about 8% of each other. Each rep still owns a coherent story they can learn deeply, and no one starts the year at a structural disadvantage.

If Rep 1 is your most experienced seller, you might deliberately leave them with the largest territory and a proportionally higher quota. That's a valid choice as long as it's intentional and the quota reflects it.

## How should you handle a new rep joining the team?

Adding a rep means redrawing lines, and that's where trust gets tested. A few principles:

- **Never take active pipeline away.** Any account with an open opportunity stays with its current rep until the deal closes or is lost.
- **Give the new rep a mix of tiers.** Handing them only Tier C accounts sets them up to fail. Give them a fair share of Tier A accounts that haven't been worked recently.
- **Account for ramp time.** A new hire won't produce at full capacity for months. Their quota should reflect [how long a new sales rep takes to ramp up](/blog/how-long-does-a-new-sales-rep-take-to-ramp-up), even if their territory is fully balanced.
- **Explain the reasoning openly.** Show the weighted scores. Reps accept smaller territories far more readily when they can see the math.

## How often should you redraw territories?

For most small teams, **a light review every six months and a full rebalance once a year** works well. Trigger an earlier review if:

- a rep's territory has shown much lower pipeline creation than the others for two consecutive quarters, despite solid activity levels
- you add or lose a rep
- you launch a new product or enter a new vertical or country
- your ICP changes based on win/loss data

When you review, compare **pipeline created per territory**, **win rate**, and **activity levels**. If activity is high but pipeline is low, the territory is likely the problem. If activity is low, it's more likely a coaching conversation. Tracking these consistently is part of [the metrics a founder should watch in sales](/blog/what-metrics-should-founder-track-sales).

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Splitting by alphabet.** "A–M for you, N–Z for me" is balanced by luck at best and teaches reps nothing about their buyers.
- **Letting reps pick their own accounts.** Your strongest or loudest rep will take the best accounts, and the rest of the team will notice.
- **Ignoring account quality.** Equal counts don't mean equal opportunity.
- **Too many territory dimensions.** A vertical × size × region matrix for four reps produces confusion and constant exceptions.
- **No dormancy rule.** Unworked accounts sitting in someone's name are pure waste.
- **Changing territories mid-quarter without warning.** Nothing damages trust faster, especially when commission is involved.

## FAQ

### How many accounts should each B2B sales rep own?

It depends on deal size and sales motion. Reps doing high-touch, account-based selling into larger companies typically work well with roughly 50–150 named accounts. Reps selling smaller deals with shorter cycles can handle several hundred. A good test is whether a rep can meaningfully touch every Tier A account at least once a quarter.

### Should territories be based on geography or industry?

For most small B2B teams, industry is the better primary split because reps build expertise that directly improves win rates. Geography makes more sense when you sell in person, operate across countries with different languages, or have significant time zone differences. You can still use geography as a secondary rule within a vertical when needed.

### What happens to accounts when a rep leaves?

Reassign the departing rep's open opportunities immediately to the rep best placed to close them, and tell the prospects about the change personally. Distribute the remaining accounts using the same weighted scoring you used originally, or hold them in a pool until a replacement is hired. Document everything in the CRM so no context is lost.

### How do I make territory splits feel fair to the team?

Make the method transparent. Share the account list, the tier definitions, and the weighted scores for each territory. Set rules for edge cases in writing before conflicts happen. When reps can see that the split was done with math rather than favoritism, they argue far less about it.

## Conclusion

Splitting territories for a small B2B team doesn't need a consultant or complex software. Count your full account universe, score it with a simple tier system, choose one primary dimension, balance by weighted opportunity, and write down the rules for edge cases before they cause arguments. Put ownership in your CRM, connect quotas to territory potential, and revisit the split at a regular cadence.

Done well, territories give each rep a clear mandate and give you a fair way to compare performance. They also protect your company's reputation with prospects.
