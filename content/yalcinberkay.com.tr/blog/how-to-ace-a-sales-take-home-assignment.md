A sales take-home assignment is a practical task, such as building a prospect list, writing an outbound sequence, or preparing an account plan, that shows a hiring manager how you think and work before they hire you. **To ace it, clarify the brief, research the company's real customers, deliver a focused piece of work that fits the scope, and explain the reasoning behind every choice.** Candidates who win are rarely the ones who produce the most pages. They are the ones whose work looks like something the team could use on Monday morning.

I've reviewed a lot of take-home assignments while building outbound teams at Corner Group, and the patterns are consistent. Strong submissions are specific, honest about their assumptions, and easy to read in five minutes. Weak ones are generic, padded, or obviously written to impress rather than to sell. This guide walks through what hiring managers look for, how to structure your work step by step, and how to defend it when you present it live.

## What is a sales take-home assignment, and why do companies use them?

A take-home assignment is a work sample. Instead of asking "How would you prospect into mid-market logistics companies?" in an interview, the company asks you to actually do it, usually with a deadline of two to seven days.

Companies use them because sales interviews reward people who talk well, and talking well is only part of the job. A take-home reveals things a conversation can't:

- **Whether you research before you write.** Generic outreach is the fastest way to burn a market.
- **Whether you can prioritise.** Picking 10 good accounts is harder than listing 100 random ones.
- **Whether you follow instructions.** If the brief says "three emails" and you send seven, that tells the reader something.
- **Whether you can explain your thinking.** Reps who can explain why something works can be coached. Reps who can't usually plateau.

For the employer, it lowers the risk of a bad hire. For you, it's a chance to stand out from candidates with similar CVs. ## What types of take-home assignments will you see?

Most assignments fall into five categories. Knowing which one you have helps you understand what's really being tested.

### 1. Prospect list and outbound sequence

This is the most common one for SDR, BDR and junior AE roles. You'll be asked to identify target accounts and contacts, then write a multi-touch sequence of emails, LinkedIn messages and sometimes call scripts. The real test is your judgement about who to target and what to say to them.

### 2. Account plan

Typical for AE and account manager roles. You choose one or two target companies, map the buying committee, find likely pain points and outline how you'd move from first contact to closed deal. This tests your strategic thinking and your understanding of complex B2B buying.

### 3. Discovery or demo preparation

You get a fictional or real prospect and have to prepare a discovery call plan or a short demo flow. Sometimes this turns into a live role-play. The test is whether you lead with questions or with features.

### 4. Written case or objection response

You might get a scenario like "A prospect says your price is 40% higher than a competitor's. Write your reply." This tests commercial instinct and tone.

### 5. 30-60-90 day plan or territory plan

These are common for senior and management roles. You lay out how you'd ramp up, which segments you'd focus on and which metrics you'd own. If you're interviewing for a leadership role, pair this with our guide on [how to ace a sales manager interview](/blog/how-to-ace-a-sales-manager-interview), because the plan will almost certainly be discussed there.

## What are hiring managers actually evaluating?

When I score a take-home, I'm not grading it like a school essay. I'm asking myself four questions:

1. **Did they understand our customer?** Is the targeting close to our real ideal customer profile, or did they guess?
2. **Is the work usable?** Could a rep on the team send these emails tomorrow with minor edits?
3. **Is the reasoning visible?** Do I understand why they chose these accounts, this angle, this sequence length?
4. **Did they respect the brief?** Right format, right length, on time.

Notice what's missing from that list: design polish, slide count and buzzwords. A clean Google Doc with sharp thinking beats a 25-slide deck full of stock imagery every time.

## How do you approach a sales take-home step by step?

Here is the process I'd follow if I were a candidate today.

### Step 1: Read the brief twice and ask clarifying questions

Before you do anything, write down every assumption you're making. Then send a short, professional email with two or three clarifying questions. For example:

- "Should I focus on a specific region or company size, or choose the segment I think fits best?"
- "Are there accounts I should avoid because they're current customers?"
- "Would you prefer the deliverable as a document or a short deck?"

Asking good questions is a sales skill in itself. Many hiring managers notice it, and some quietly count it. Don't send ten questions, though. Two or three focused ones show judgement. Ten show anxiety.

### Step 2: Research the company like it's your first week

Spend real time understanding the business before you produce anything:

- Read their website, case studies and pricing page if it's public.
- Look at their customer logos and work out the common thread: industry, size, geography.
- Check the LinkedIn profiles of current salespeople to see what they post about and who they target.
- Read recent news, funding announcements or product launches.

Write a one-paragraph summary of their ideal customer in your own words. If you can't, you're not ready to build the deliverable.

### Step 3: Define your target and justify it

If the assignment involves prospecting, resist the urge to go broad. Pick a tight segment, for example "B2B SaaS companies in Turkey with 50 to 200 employees that recently hired their first sales leader," and explain why. Narrow targeting with a clear trigger shows you understand that relevance drives reply rates.

To build the actual list, use whatever tools you have access to. A free tier of a database like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) is often enough to find accounts, filter by headcount and industry, and identify the right titles. Check each contact manually, though. Sending a list full of people who left the company a year ago is an easy way to lose points.

### Step 4: Build the core deliverable

Keep the scope tight. For an outbound assignment, a strong submission usually includes:

- **10 to 20 target accounts**, each with a one-line reason it fits
- **2 to 3 personas** (for example Head of Sales, Founder, RevOps Manager) with their likely priorities
- **A 4 to 6 touch sequence** over 2 to 3 weeks, mixing email, LinkedIn and phone
- **One fully personalised example** for a named account, alongside the template version

For an account plan, focus on the stakeholder map, two or three hypotheses about pain points, and a realistic path to a first meeting. Don't try to forecast the whole deal down to the euro.

### Step 5: Show your reasoning

This is where most candidates fall short. Add a short "Why I made these choices" section, half a page at most, that covers:

- Why this segment over others
- Why this sequence length and channel mix
- What you would test first and how you'd measure it (for example, "I'd A/B test subject lines on the first 100 sends and judge by reply rate, not open rate")
- What you'd change once you had real data

This turns a static document into evidence of how you'd operate on the team.

### Step 6: Package it cleanly

Put a short executive summary at the top: three to five bullet points that someone could read in 60 seconds. Use clear headings. Proofread twice, and check that you've spelled the company's name and product correctly everywhere. It sounds basic, but mistakes here happen more often than you'd expect.

## What does a strong outbound sequence look like in practice?

Let's make this concrete. Say you're applying for an SDR role at a company that sells sales-training software, and your target persona is a Head of Sales at a 100-person SaaS company.

A weak first email looks like this:

> "Hi Ayşe, I hope you're well. Our platform helps sales teams improve performance with AI-powered coaching. Do you have 30 minutes next week for a demo?"

It's generic, focused on the product and asks for too much too early.

A stronger version:

> "Hi Ayşe, saw that you've posted four AE roles in the last month. Ramping that many reps at once usually means your best people end up doing the onboarding instead of selling. We help sales leaders shorten that ramp with structured call coaching. Worth a quick look at how teams your size handle it?"

Why it works: it uses a real trigger (hiring activity), names a pain the persona actually feels, and makes a small ask. In your reasoning section, you'd explain that ramp time is a major concern for sales leaders who are scaling, which is a topic we cover in depth in [how long a new sales rep takes to ramp up](/blog/how-long-does-a-new-sales-rep-take-to-ramp-up).

Then outline the rest of the sequence:

1. **Day 1:** Personalised email (above)
2. **Day 3:** LinkedIn connection request, no pitch
3. **Day 5:** Follow-up email with a relevant insight or short resource
4. **Day 8:** Phone call attempt, with a 20-second opener written out
5. **Day 12:** Email with a different angle, such as manager time saved
6. **Day 17:** Short breakup email

That's six touches over about two and a half weeks. Clear, realistic and easy to evaluate. If you've worked in agency-style outbound before, for example on campaigns like the ones [SAAS Corner runs for B2B SaaS companies](https://saascorner.co), say so and draw on that experience in your reasoning.

## How much time should you spend on a sales take-home?

A reasonable range for most assignments is **3 to 6 hours** of focused work. Some companies specify a limit. If they do, respect it, and mention in your submission how long you spent.

If there's no limit, use this rough split:

- **30 to 60 minutes:** reading the brief and researching the company
- **60 to 90 minutes:** building the target list or account map
- **60 to 90 minutes:** writing the core deliverable
- **30 minutes:** writing the reasoning section
- **30 minutes:** editing and formatting

Spending 20 hours on an unpaid assignment usually doesn't pay off, and it can signal poor prioritisation. If an employer asks for something that would clearly take several days, such as a full go-to-market strategy, it's fair to ask politely whether a narrower version would work.

## What are the most common mistakes?

After reviewing many submissions, these are the mistakes I see most:

- **Going too broad.** "I'll target all B2B companies in Europe" is not a strategy.
- **Product-first messaging.** Leading with features rather than the prospect's problem.
- **Ignoring the brief.** Wrong format, wrong length or missing sections.
- **No reasoning.** Great emails with no explanation leave the reviewer guessing.
- **Copy-paste AI output.** Writing that is obviously unedited, full of filler phrases and generic personalisation.
- **Fake precision.** Inventing reply rates or pipeline numbers you can't support. Saying "I'd aim to validate the reply rate in the first two weeks" is more credible than a made-up figure.
- **Late submission without notice.** If something comes up, tell them before the deadline, not after.

## Can you use AI tools on a sales take-home?

In most cases, yes, and many hiring managers expect you to. Modern sales teams use AI for research, first drafts and list enrichment. What matters is how you use it.

Good uses:

- Summarising a company's case studies to find patterns in their customers
- Generating subject-line variations that you then filter and edit
- Drafting persona profiles that you check against real LinkedIn profiles

Bad uses:

- Submitting unedited output as your own thinking
- Letting AI invent facts about prospects

My advice: be open about it. A line like "I used AI to speed up initial research and drafted variants, then edited everything by hand" signals that you're current and honest. That's a better look than pretending you didn't use it, or hoping nobody notices.

## How do you present your take-home in the follow-up interview?

Most take-homes lead to a 20 to 45 minute presentation or discussion. Prepare for it as seriously as the document itself.

1. **Open with a 60-second summary.** What you targeted, why, and what you'd do first.
2. **Walk through one example in depth** instead of skimming everything.
3. **Name your weak spots before they do.** "With more time I'd validate these personas with two or three discovery calls."
4. **Invite pushback.** When they challenge a choice, don't fold immediately and don't get defensive. Explain your reasoning, then show you can adapt. This is exactly how a good rep handles objections.
5. **Ask what they would have done differently.** It shows you're coachable, and you'll learn how the team thinks.

## FAQ

### Should I do a sales take-home assignment if it isn't paid?

For a reasonable assignment of a few hours, yes. It's one of the best ways to stand out from candidates with similar backgrounds. If the task looks like real consulting work that would take days, it's fair to ask politely whether a shorter version is acceptable.

### Is it okay to contact real prospects as part of the assignment?

Only if the brief explicitly asks you to. Otherwise, build the list and write the outreach without sending anything, because contacting a company's real market on its behalf without permission can cause problems for them. If you're unsure, ask the hiring manager before you start.

### How long should the final document be?

For most SDR and AE assignments, 3 to 8 pages or 5 to 10 slides is plenty. The reviewer should be able to understand your main points in under five minutes from the executive summary alone. Length is never a substitute for clarity.

### What if I have no direct experience in the company's industry?

Say so openly, then show how quickly you learned. Strong research, a clear ideal customer profile and thoughtful questions often matter more than industry background, especially for junior roles. Many hiring managers would rather hire a fast learner than someone with the right logos and weak thinking.

## Conclusion

Acing a sales take-home assignment isn't about producing the most impressive document. It's about showing that you understand the customer, can prioritise, write outreach that people will actually reply to, and explain your thinking clearly. Clarify the brief, research properly, keep the scope tight, make your reasoning visible, and prepare to defend your choices with confidence and openness.

Treat the assignment as your first week on the job, because that's exactly how the hiring manager is reading it.
