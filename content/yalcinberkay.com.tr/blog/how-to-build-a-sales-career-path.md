A sales career path is a written map of the levels a rep can reach, what each level demands, and exactly how someone moves from one to the next. To build one your reps will actually follow, define four to six clear levels, attach measurable promotion criteria to each, offer both a management track and a senior individual contributor track, and review progress against the map every quarter. When reps can see the next step and know precisely what earns it, they stop guessing, stop job-hunting, and start working toward something.

Most sales teams don't have this. They have job titles, a vague promise that "top performers get promoted," and a manager who decides based on feel. That works until your best SDR gets a LinkedIn message offering an AE title somewhere else. Over the years of building outbound teams at Corner Group, I've learned that a career path is one of the cheapest retention tools you have. It doesn't cost much to design, but it changes how people show up every day. Here is how to build one properly.

## Why do good reps leave when there's no clear path?

Salespeople are wired to chase targets. Give them a quota and they'll hit it. Give them a fuzzy future and they'll fill the gap with their own assumptions, usually pessimistic ones.

When there is no defined path, a few predictable things happen:

- **Reps compare themselves to the outside market instead of your internal ladder.** A competitor offering a bigger title looks like progress, even if the job is worse.
- **Promotions feel political.** If one rep moves up and another doesn't, and nobody can explain the difference in concrete terms, trust erodes across the whole team.
- **Managers make inconsistent decisions.** Two managers on the same team might promote on completely different standards.
- **High performers plateau.** Without a next goal beyond "hit quota again," even your best people get bored.

A career path solves these problems by replacing opinion with criteria. It also makes your job as a leader easier, because the hard conversations ("Why wasn't I promoted?") now have objective answers.

## What levels should a sales career path include?

Keep it simple. Too many levels and promotions feel meaningless; too few and people wait years for any sign of progress. For most B2B teams under 50 people, this structure works well:

1. **SDR I (Sales Development Representative):** Learning the product, the ICP, and the outbound motion. Focus is activity quality and booking first meetings.
2. **SDR II / Senior SDR:** Consistently hits meeting targets, books higher-quality meetings, starts mentoring new SDRs.
3. **Account Executive I:** Runs discovery, demos, and closes smaller or mid-market deals with support.
4. **Account Executive II:** Owns a full cycle independently, handles larger deal sizes and multi-stakeholder buying committees.
5. **Senior AE / Strategic AE:** Handles the most complex accounts, contributes to playbooks, influences pricing and process.
6. **Team Lead → Sales Manager:** Responsible for other people's results rather than only their own.

If you're a smaller company, you can compress this. A startup with four reps might only need three levels: SDR, AE, Senior AE. If you're still at the stage of figuring out [how to build an outbound sales team from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-outbound-sales-team-from-scratch), design the path early anyway. It's far easier to set expectations with your first hires than to retrofit a structure later.

## How do you define promotion criteria reps can trust?

This is where most career paths fail. Leaders write "demonstrates leadership" or "shows strong closing ability" and call it done. Nobody can measure that, so it collapses back into opinion.

Every level should have criteria in three categories: **results, skills, and behaviors.** Here is an example of what promotion from SDR I to SDR II might look like:

### Results (measurable, usually 50–60% of the decision)

- Hit at least 100% of the meeting target in three of the last four months
- Maintain a meeting-to-opportunity conversion rate at or above team average for two consecutive quarters
- Minimum tenure of six months in role

### Skills (observable, assessed by manager)

- Can run a cold call without a script and handle the five most common objections
- Writes personalized outbound sequences that a manager approves without major edits
- Correctly qualifies leads using the team's framework before handing them to an AE

### Behaviors (how they work)

- Keeps CRM data clean and up to date, with no deals missing next steps
- Shares wins and lessons in team meetings
- Has onboarded or shadowed at least one new hire

Notice the numbers. "Three of the last four months" is something a rep can track on their own dashboard. "Meeting-to-opportunity conversion at or above team average" protects you from promoting someone who books lots of low-quality meetings. Your exact thresholds will depend on your market and deal size; if you need help setting realistic targets in the first place, my guide on [how to set sales quotas for a sales team](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quotas-for-sales-team) walks through the math.

For the SDR-to-AE jump specifically, the skill criteria matter more than the results. A great SDR isn't automatically a great closer. I've written a full piece on [how to move from SDR to Account Executive](/blog/move-from-sdr-to-account-executive) that you can share directly with reps who are aiming for that step.

## Should every great rep become a manager?

No. This is one of the most expensive mistakes in sales leadership. You take your best closer, promote them to manager because that's "the next step," and you lose a top producer and gain a struggling manager.

Selling and managing are different jobs. A great AE is motivated by personal wins, competition, and control over their own deals. A great manager is motivated by other people's success and is patient with repetitive coaching.

That's why your career path needs **two tracks after the AE level:**

- **Individual contributor (IC) track:** AE II → Senior AE → Strategic or Principal AE. These people handle bigger accounts, earn higher commission ceilings, and get recognition without managing anyone.
- **Management track:** AE II → Team Lead (managing two or three reps while still carrying a reduced quota) → Sales Manager → Head of Sales.

The Team Lead role is critical. It's a low-risk trial. A rep can test whether they enjoy coaching while still selling, and if it isn't for them, they can return to the IC track without anyone treating it as a failure. Make that explicit in writing.

For anyone moving into management, the biggest early challenge is learning to develop people without hovering over them. My article on [how to coach sales reps without micromanaging them](/blog/how-to-coach-sales-reps-without-micromanaging) is a good starting point for new team leads.

## How long should each step take?

Reps want to know timelines, and they deserve a realistic answer. Rough ranges that work for most B2B teams:

- **SDR I → SDR II:** 6–9 months
- **SDR II → AE I:** 12–18 months total time as an SDR
- **AE I → AE II:** 12–18 months
- **AE II → Senior AE or Team Lead:** 18–24 months

Two rules make timelines fair. First, **set a minimum tenure, but never a guaranteed one.** Time in seat qualifies you to be considered; it doesn't promote you. Second, **allow exceptions in both directions.** An SDR who crushes every criterion in five months shouldn't wait four more just because the document says so. Let your top performers move faster. That's exactly the signal you want the rest of the team to see.

## How should compensation change at each level?

A title change without a pay change feels hollow, especially in sales, where people track money closely. Each level should come with a visible change in at least one of these:

- **Base salary:** a defined band per level, so reps know the range before they get there
- **Variable pay:** higher on-target earnings, a larger share of variable pay, or better commission rates
- **Accelerators:** increased payouts above 100% of quota for senior levels
- **Account access:** better territories, larger accounts, or inbound lead allocation

Publish the bands internally, at least as ranges. You don't need to share individual salaries, but a rep should be able to see "AE I base is between X and Y." Transparency here removes a huge amount of anxiety and negotiation drama.

A practical example: an SDR I might have a 70/30 base-to-variable split. At AE I, that might move to 60/40, and at Senior AE to 50/50 with accelerators above quota. The message is clear: as you grow, your upside grows with your performance.

## How do you build the career path step by step?

Here's the process I'd follow for a team of 5 to 30 reps. It can realistically be done in about four weeks.

1. **Week 1: Audit what you have.** List every current title, what each person actually does, and what they earn. You'll often find two people with the same title doing very different work, or different titles doing the same job.
2. **Week 1: Interview your top performers.** Ask what they did in their first year that made them successful. Their answers become your skill and behavior criteria.
3. **Week 2: Draft levels and criteria.** Use the results/skills/behaviors format for each level. Keep each level to one page.
4. **Week 2: Set compensation bands.** Check local market rates for each level, then align your bands so each promotion means a real increase.
5. **Week 3: Test with managers.** Have each manager map their current reps to the new levels. If you can't place someone clearly, your criteria are too vague. Rewrite them.
6. **Week 3: Get feedback from two or three reps.** Not everyone; just a few trusted people. Ask: "Is anything unclear? Would this motivate you?"
7. **Week 4: Launch with individual conversations.** Don't just send a document. Sit down with each rep, show them where they sit today, and agree on what they need to do to reach the next level.
8. **Ongoing: Review quarterly.** Every quarter, each rep and their manager score progress against the criteria. Promotions happen at set points (for example, twice a year), which keeps the process predictable.

The quarterly review fits naturally alongside your regular operating rhythm. If you already run a [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-guide), you'll have most of the data you need for the results section.

## What's different when building a sales career path in Turkey?

Since much of my work happens here, a few local realities are worth mentioning.

**Titles carry significant weight.** In Turkish business culture, *unvan* matters, both inside the company and to family and peers. A rep moving from "Satış Temsilcisi" to "Kıdemli Satış Temsilcisi" or "Müşteri Yöneticisi" can mean a lot, even before the salary change. Use this thoughtfully, but don't inflate titles to avoid paying people properly. Reps see through that quickly.

**Inflation makes salary bands go stale fast.** A band you set in January can look very different in real terms a few months later. Review compensation bands at least twice a year, and separate cost-of-living adjustments from promotion increases so a promotion still feels like a real step up. For a fuller picture, see my breakdown of [what a sales rep really costs in Turkey](/blog/what-does-a-sales-rep-cost-in-turkey).

**English and international skills are a lever.** Many Turkish B2B companies sell abroad. Building "can run discovery calls in English with international buyers" into AE II or Senior AE criteria gives reps a concrete skill to develop and increases their value to the business.

## What are the most common mistakes to avoid?

- **Writing criteria nobody can measure.** If a rep can't self-assess, the criteria are too vague.
- **Promoting only to retain.** Promoting someone who isn't ready because they threatened to leave teaches the whole team that threats work.
- **Forgetting about the middle performers.** The path should motivate solid, steady reps too, not only your stars.
- **Launching and never updating.** Your sales motion will change as you add AI tools, new segments, or new products. Revisit the path once a year.
- **Keeping it secret.** A career path that lives in a manager's head isn't a career path. Put it in writing and make it accessible to everyone.

## FAQ

### How many levels should a sales career path have?

Most B2B sales teams work well with four to six levels, from entry-level SDR up to Senior AE and Sales Manager. Smaller startups can start with three levels and add more as the team grows. The goal is for reps to see meaningful progress every 12 to 18 months.

### What's the difference between a career path and a promotion policy?

A promotion policy explains when and how promotions are decided, while a career path shows the full set of levels and what each one requires. A good career path includes the promotion policy inside it, covering criteria, timelines, and review cycles. Reps need both to trust the system.

### Should top-performing salespeople always be promoted to management?

No. Selling and managing require different strengths, and promoting a top closer into management often loses a great rep and creates a struggling manager. Offering a senior individual contributor track lets strong sellers grow their income and influence without managing people.

### How often should reps be reviewed against the career path?

Quarterly progress reviews work best for most teams, with actual promotion decisions made once or twice a year. This gives reps regular feedback while keeping promotions predictable and fair. Reviews should use the written criteria rather than general impressions.

## Conclusion

A sales career path isn't a nice-to-have HR document. It's a performance tool. When reps know exactly what the next level looks like, what it pays, and what it takes to get there, they work with more focus and stay longer. Start with clear levels, attach measurable criteria to each one, give your best sellers a track that doesn't force them into management, and review progress every quarter. You can build the first version in a month, and it will pay you back with every rep you don't lose to a competitor.
