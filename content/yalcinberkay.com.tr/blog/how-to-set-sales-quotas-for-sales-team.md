Sales quotas for a team are set by **working backwards from the company's revenue target and comparing the result with each rep's real selling capacity**. A healthy quota rests on historical performance data, average deal size, win rate, the length of the sales cycle and the ramp period for new reps. In short, a good quota reflects both what the company needs and what is actually happening in the field. Simply saying "we did 10 last year, let's do 15 this year" doesn't count as setting a quota. At best, it's a wish.

One of the problems I see most often when working with sales teams is that the quota gets set in a spreadsheet at the start of the year, and afterwards nobody can explain where that number came from. If a rep finds the quota unreachable, their motivation drops. If the quota is too easy, the company misses growth opportunities and the best salespeople perform below their potential. In this post I'll go through quota setting step by step, with a worked example in numbers and the mistakes I see most often in the field.

## What is a sales quota and why is it so critical?

A sales quota is the target a rep or a team is expected to reach within a given period. This target is usually expressed as a measurable metric such as revenue, number of closed deals, number of meetings or value of pipeline created.

A quota isn't just a performance measure. It is also:

- **The foundation of financial planning.** The company budget, hiring plan and cash flow all rest on the sum of the sales quotas.
- **The backbone of the commission system.** A rep's variable pay is calculated according to how much of their quota they achieve.
- **A driver of behaviour.** Whatever you measure is what the team focuses on. If you set only a revenue quota, reps may lean towards small but fast deals. If you set only an activity quota, lots of calls get made but quality may drop.
- **An influence on team culture.** Quotas perceived as fair build trust. Quotas perceived as arbitrary lead to resentment within the team and to staff turnover.

That's why setting quotas is one of the most consequential decisions a sales leader makes during the year.

## The three basic approaches to setting quotas

In practice, three methods stand out. Which one you choose depends on your company's maturity and the quality of the data you have.

### Top-down

Management or investors set the annual revenue target, and that target is divided by the number of people on the team. It's fast and aligned with the company target. But it doesn't take capacity in the field into account. The logic of "the target is 20 million, there are 4 of us, so 5 million each" never asks whether the reps can actually sell that much.

### Bottom-up

Each rep's capacity is calculated from pipeline, win rate, deal size and time, and then these capacities are added up. It's realistic and reps accept it more easily. The downside is that on its own it may not meet the company's need for growth.

### Hybrid approach

In my view, this is the healthiest method. First the company target is set from the top, then a bottom-up capacity calculation is done. The difference between the two numbers is treated as a **"gap analysis"**. If the gap is large, either the target is revised or the gap is closed with levers such as hiring, pipeline generation or pricing.

## How do you set a quota step by step?

You can apply the process below to newly formed teams as well as mature ones. With new teams there is simply more estimation involved, that's all.

### 1. Clarify the company target

The first question is this: how much **new** revenue is expected from the sales team this year? Separate renewal revenue from existing customers, expansion (upsell) revenue and new customer revenue. In most B2B companies these three are the responsibility of different teams or different quotas. If you lump them all into one number, it becomes unclear who is responsible for what.

### 2. Pull your historical data

Data is the fuel of quota calculation. At a minimum, you need these metrics:

- **Average deal size:** The average of deals closed in the last 12 months.
- **Win rate:** How many qualified opportunities were won?
- **Sales cycle:** The average time from the first qualified meeting to signature.
- **Qualified opportunities per rep per month:** The volume of opportunities a rep can handle well at the same time.
- **Seasonality:** In which quarters do sales cluster?

If you can't pull this data reliably, the real problem isn't the quota, it's your infrastructure. A win rate taken from a CRM where opportunity stages aren't defined consistently will be misleading. If you haven't set up this infrastructure yet, the guide I wrote on [choosing a CRM for a small sales team](/blog/how-to-choose-crm-for-small-sales-team) can be a good starting point.

### 3. Calculate rep capacity

This is the heart of it. The annual capacity of a rep working at full productivity, meaning one who has completed their ramp period, is roughly calculated like this:

**Annual capacity = Qualified opportunities per month × 12 × Win rate × Average deal size**

This formula is simple but a solid starting point. More mature teams go further and work from "active selling days", taking into account the time a rep spends on holidays, training and internal meetings.

### 4. Account for the ramp period

Expecting a full quota from a newly hired rep from their first month is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes. The ramp period is directly related to the length of your sales cycle. If your cycle is 90 days, it will most likely take a new rep 3 to 4 months to close their first deal.

A commonly used ramp structure looks like this:

- **First quarter:** 25% of the full quota
- **Second quarter:** 50% of the full quota
- **Third quarter:** 75% of the full quota
- **Fourth quarter:** Full quota

How long should the ramp be? A simple rule: **ramp length = onboarding time + average sales cycle length**. Transactional SaaS (2 weeks of onboarding, 30-day cycle) usually needs 2 to 3 months. Mid-market B2B software (4 weeks, 90-day cycle) needs about 4 months. Enterprise or complex services (6 weeks, 6-month cycle) need 6 to 8 months. In the first one or two months, measure activities, meetings and pipeline created instead of revenue. Revenue expectations should rise only as fast as the rep's pipeline can mature.

Shortening the ramp period depends on two things: hiring the right person and integrating them into the system quickly. On the hiring side, the framework in my post on [questions to ask in a sales rep interview](/blog/sales-rep-interview-questions) helps you screen out early the candidates who would drag out the ramp period.

### 5. Add an over-assignment buffer

Not everyone on the team will hit their quota. If you ignore this reality, you'll miss the company target. That's why the sum of rep quotas is usually set **10% to 20%** higher than the company's sales target. How large this buffer should be depends on how quota attainment has historically been distributed across your team. If half the team has missed quota in the past, you need to keep the buffer high. But first you should ask why the quotas were missed.

### 6. Distribute the quota by segment, territory and period

Giving everyone the same quota looks fair, but most of the time it isn't. A rep managing the enterprise segment in Istanbul and a rep managing the SME segment in Anatolia don't have the same market potential. When distributing quotas, look at:

- The number of target accounts in the territory or segment
- The average budget size of those accounts
- The existing pipeline handed over to the rep
- The rep's seniority and ramp status

When splitting the annual quota into quarters, don't forget seasonality either. Budget periods, the summer months and year-end closes noticeably affect the rhythm of sales.

### 7. Tie the quota to the commission plan

A quota means nothing on its own. It works together with the reward system. Things to watch here:

- **On-target earnings (OTE):** The total of base plus variable pay a rep earns when they hit 100% of quota.
- **Accelerators:** A higher commission rate on sales above quota. This is one of the most effective ways to retain your top performers.
- **Threshold:** No commission paid below a certain percentage of quota. If this threshold is set too high, it damages team morale.
- **No cap:** Capping commission is the same as telling your best salespeople "stop after a certain point".

## A worked example: how do you calculate a quota?

Let's make the theory concrete. The numbers are for illustration, and you can swap in your own data.

Say you're a company selling B2B software and this year you're targeting **20 million TL** in revenue from new customers. Your historical data looks like this:

- Average deal size: **400,000 TL**
- Win rate (qualified opportunity to signature): **20%**
- New qualified opportunities a fully productive rep can handle per month: **6**
- Sales cycle: **around 90 days**

**Rep capacity:**
6 opportunities × 12 months = 72 opportunities
72 × 20% = about 14.4 deals
14.4 × 400,000 TL = **about 5.76 million TL**

**Total quota with over-assignment:**
20 million TL × 1.15 = **23 million TL**

**Number of fully productive reps needed:**
23 million TL / 5.76 million TL ≈ **4 reps**

If the team has 4 fully productive reps, the quota per person comes to about **5.75 million TL**, which is in line with capacity. But if one of those 4 is a new hire starting at the beginning of the year, the picture changes. With the ramp structure, that person's effective annual capacity is:

5.75 million × (0.25 + 0.50 + 0.75 + 1.00) / 4 = **about 3.6 million TL**

That leaves the team with a **gap of about 2.15 million TL**. To close this gap, you have these options:

- Hire a fifth rep at the start of the year
- Increase pipeline generation to raise the number of opportunities per rep
- Make sales process improvements that raise win rate or deal size
- Revise the target together with management

Ignoring the gap and quietly raising the quotas of the other three is the worst option.

**Quarterly split:** Let's assume your sales have historically been distributed as 20%, 25%, 20% and 35%. A fully productive rep's annual quota of 5.75 million TL is split across the quarters like this:

- Q1: 1.15 million TL
- Q2: about 1.44 million TL
- Q3: 1.15 million TL
- Q4: about 2.01 million TL

**Pipeline coverage ratio:** If your win rate is 20%, you theoretically need qualified pipeline worth **5x** the quota to hit a quarterly quota. For a Q2 quota of 1.44 million TL, about 7.2 million TL of qualified pipeline needs to be in place at the start of the quarter. Many teams apply the "3x pipeline is enough" rule by rote. But the right coverage ratio should be derived from your own win rate.

## Revenue quota or activity quota?

The answer to this question depends on the role and on how experienced the rep is.

A **revenue quota** measures the outcome and is the core metric for Account Executive (AE) roles responsible for closing. But revenue is a lagging indicator. In a 90-day sales cycle, you see the result of the work you do today three months later.

An **activity quota** (daily calls, emails sent, LinkedIn messages and so on) measures leading indicators. It's useful for reps in their ramp period and for SDRs. But used on its own, it makes the team optimise for volume rather than quality.

My recommendation is to build a layered structure:

- **For AEs:** Revenue quota as the main metric, qualified pipeline created as the secondary metric.
- **For SDRs:** A qualified meeting or qualified opportunity quota as the main metric, with the activity target only as a steering tool.
- **For everyone in their ramp period:** Activity and learning goals in the first 1 to 2 months, then a gradual move to outcome metrics.

## How do you set an SDR quota?

In an outbound team, the SDR quota is calculated backwards from the AE quota. Let's continue with the example above:

- Each AE needs **6 qualified opportunities** a month.
- About **60%** of the meetings SDRs book turn into qualified opportunities.
- That means **10 meetings** per AE per month.
- For 4 AEs, that's **40 meetings** a month.
- If there are 2 SDRs on the team, the monthly quota per person is **20 qualified meetings**.

To see whether this number is realistic, you also need to look at the SDR's activity funnel. How many replies come from how many people reached, and how many meetings from how many replies? If there's a problem at the top of the funnel, for example if your email reply rates are low, raising the quota isn't the solution. You need to improve your messaging and targeting first. ## When should a quota be revised?

Quotas are usually set annually and tracked quarterly. But they aren't sacred numbers that must never be touched during the year. Situations that call for a revision are:

- **The territory or account portfolio has changed:** If a large account has been moved from one rep to another, the quota should be adjusted accordingly.
- **The product or pricing has changed:** A price increase or a new package directly affects deal size.
- **Market conditions have changed significantly:** For example, sharp moves in the exchange rate, a contraction in the sector or a major competitor entering the market.
- **Quota attainment is distributed abnormally:** If 80% of the team exceeds quota in the first quarter, or nobody comes close, the problem isn't the individuals, it's the quota.

If there's going to be a revision, the rules should be clear from the start. If it's written down in which situations, by whom and with what data a quota will be revised, reps will trust that the process is fair. Raising a rep's quota when they're about to exceed it sends your team the message that "the reward for working harder is a higher quota". Never do this.

## What are the most common mistakes when setting quotas?

These are the mistakes I come across most often in the field:

1. **Setting the quota by adding a percentage to last year.** The "last year was 4 million, let's grow 30% this year" approach doesn't account for market potential, the state of the pipeline or capacity.
2. **Ignoring the ramp period.** Giving a new rep a full quota from day one burns them out and sinks your target.
3. **Giving everyone the same quota.** Different segments and territories carry different potential.
4. **Not checking pipeline coverage.** If there isn't enough pipeline at the start of the quarter, hitting the quota is mathematically impossible. You should notice this at the start of the quarter, not at the end.
5. **Announcing the quota without discussing it with the rep.** Sharing the logic behind the quota calculation significantly increases acceptance. If a rep understands where the number comes from, they take ownership of it.
6. **Not accounting for the sales cycle.** In long-cycle sales, opportunities opened in the last quarter of the year will most likely slip into the following year. The quota plan should include this slippage. Shortening the cycle directly increases capacity. I'd suggest considering the recommendations in my post on [practical ways to shorten the B2B sales cycle](/blog/how-to-shorten-b2b-sales-cycle) alongside your quota planning.
7. **Focusing on a single metric.** If only revenue is measured, discounting increases. If only activity is measured, quality drops. Build a balanced set of metrics.

## How should you communicate the quota to the team?

Setting the quota is half the job. The other half is communicating it properly. This is the approach I use:

- **Hold one-to-one meetings.** Don't send the quota by email. Sit down with each rep and explain the logic of the calculation.
- **Share the assumptions openly.** Which win rate, which deal size and which number of opportunities were used? Listen to the rep's objections, and if they have valid points, make corrections.
- **Turn the quota into a monthly work plan.** "5.75 million a year" is an abstract number. "This month you need to open 6 qualified opportunities and close 3 to 4 deals by the end of the quarter" is an actionable plan.
- **Set up a regular follow-up rhythm.** Weekly pipeline meetings and monthly quota reviews let you spot problems well before the end of the quarter.

Building a data-driven sales organisation turns quota conversations from arguments into a shared planning session. In teams that adopt this approach, reps start to see the quota not as "a number imposed on us" but as "a target we calculated together". ## FAQ

### Should a sales quota be set annually or quarterly?

Setting the quota annually and breaking it down into quarterly and monthly targets is the most common and healthiest approach. The annual target keeps you aligned with financial planning, while the quarterly slices make tracking and, when needed, intervention possible. In businesses with a short sales cycle, a monthly quota can also be used.

### What percentage of the team should normally hit quota?

There is no exact figure, but in a healthy quota structure the majority of the team is expected to hit quota or come very close to it. If only a small part of the team hits quota, the problem most likely lies with the quota itself. If almost everyone exceeds it easily, the quota has been set too low.

### How do you set a quota in a new team with no historical data?

If there's no historical data, start from industry experience, the signals from the founders' first sales and conservative assumptions. In the first 1 to 2 quarters, put the weight on activity and qualified opportunity targets instead of a revenue quota. As data builds up, move to a revenue quota and update your assumptions every quarter.

### What should you do with a rep who misses quota?

First you need to understand the reason: is it not enough pipeline, a low win rate or small deal sizes? If the problem is pipeline, prospecting support helps. If it's win rate, coaching and call analysis help. If there's no improvement despite support over several quarters, a structured performance plan should be put in place.

## Conclusion

A good sales quota is set by calculation, not by guesswork. Start from the company target, calculate rep capacity with historical data, add the ramp period and an over-assignment buffer, then put the remaining gap openly on the table. Distribute the quota by segment and period, tie it properly to the commission plan, and communicate it to each rep by sharing the logic behind it. Once you've set up this process, quota conversations become shorter, less tense and far more accurate every year.
