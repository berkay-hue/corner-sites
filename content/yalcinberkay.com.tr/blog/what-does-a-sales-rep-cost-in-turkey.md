The fully loaded cost of a sales rep in Turkey is usually **1.6 to 2.2 times their gross salary** in the first year once you add employer social security contributions, benefits, commission, tools, recruiting, management time and severance accrual. The lost productivity during ramp-up comes on top of that. A rep you hire at a certain gross salary will likely cost you around double that figure before they close their first meaningful deal. If you budget only for the salary, you will run out of patience, cash, or both, long before the hire has a fair chance to succeed.

I have hired, trained, and lost sales reps across several companies in our group, and the pattern is always the same. Founders compare a candidate's salary expectation to their monthly revenue and decide it is affordable. Six months later, they are surprised by the real number. This article breaks down every cost line, walks through a worked example in Turkish lira, and gives you a simple process to budget a sales hire honestly.

## Why is salary the wrong number to look at?

Salary is the most visible cost, so it gets all the attention. In Turkey, it is also the most misunderstood. Candidates usually talk in **net** terms ("I want X in hand"), while your cost is based on **gross** salary plus employer contributions. The gap between net and gross depends on the income tax bracket, which also shifts as cumulative earnings rise through the year. A rep who asks for a specific net amount in January may cost you noticeably more per month by the autumn if you have agreed to keep their net pay flat.

Salary also ignores time. A sales rep is an investment that pays back with a delay. You are paying full cost from day one, but revenue arrives months later, if it arrives at all. That is why the real question is not "Can I afford this salary?" but "Can I fund this person for nine to twelve months before they pay for themselves?"

If you are still deciding whether you are ready for this step at all, start with my post on [when a founder should hire their first sales rep](/blog/when-should-founder-hire-first-sales-rep). This article assumes you have decided to hire and want to know what it will really cost.

## What goes into the fully loaded cost of a sales rep?

Here is every line I include when I budget a new sales hire in Turkey.

### 1. Gross salary

This is the base. Always negotiate and budget in gross terms, even if you discuss net with the candidate. Write the gross figure into the contract and make sure both sides understand how net pay may change through the year.

### 2. Employer SGK and unemployment insurance contributions

On top of gross salary, the employer pays social security and unemployment insurance contributions. As a working assumption, I budget **roughly a fifth of gross salary** for these. The exact rate depends on current regulations, the contribution ceiling, and whether you qualify for incentives. Your accountant (mali müşavir) will give you the precise figure. Do not skip that conversation.

### 3. Benefits

Most sales candidates in Turkey expect at least some of the following:

- **Meal card** (yemek kartı) or a meal allowance
- **Transportation support** or a fuel allowance for field reps
- **Private health insurance**, increasingly standard for white-collar roles in larger cities
- **Mobile phone and line**
- For field sales, a **company car** or car allowance, which can easily become one of your largest cost lines

### 4. Commission and bonuses

Variable pay is a real cost, even if it is "only paid when they sell." Budget for your on-target commission, not zero. If your plan pays 20% of base at target and the rep hits target, that money leaves your account. Some companies also pay one or more annual bonuses (ikramiye), which should be included.

### 5. Tools and data

A modern outbound rep needs a stack. That typically means:

- A CRM seat
- A prospecting and contact data tool such as [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya)
- LinkedIn Sales Navigator or a similar tool
- An email sequencing or calling tool, if not included elsewhere
- AI assistants for research and call preparation

Most of these tools are priced in **US dollars**, which matters a lot in Turkey (more on that below). If you are setting up your stack from scratch, my guide on [choosing a CRM for a small sales team](/blog/how-to-choose-crm-for-small-sales-team) will help you avoid paying for features you do not need.

### 6. Equipment

You will need a laptop, headset, and possibly a monitor and phone. This is mostly a one-off cost in the first year, but it is real cash.

### 7. Recruiting

If you use an agency, expect a fee calculated as a percentage of the rep's annual gross salary. If you recruit yourself, the cost is your time: writing the job post, screening CVs, interviewing, running case exercises, and checking references. Founder time is not free, even if it never appears on an invoice. A clear interview structure saves a lot of hours. These [sales rep interview questions](/blog/sales-rep-interview-questions) are the ones I rely on.

### 8. Management and onboarding time

A new rep needs coaching, call reviews, pipeline reviews, and someone to answer questions. In the first three months, this can take 10–20% of a manager's or founder's week. Put a number on it.

### 9. Severance accrual (kıdem tazminatı)

Turkish labour law requires severance pay in many termination scenarios once an employee has completed at least one year of service. It is calculated as roughly one month of gross salary per year worked, subject to a legal ceiling. Many founders forget this entirely. I recommend **accruing one month of gross salary per year** for every employee from day one, so a future termination does not become a cash shock. Notice periods (ihbar süresi), which increase with tenure, are another cost to keep in mind.

## How much does a sales rep cost per year? A worked example

Let's make this concrete. The figures below are **illustrative**, not market benchmarks. Salaries in Turkey change quickly with inflation, so plug in your own numbers. What stays useful is the structure and the multiplier.

Assume you hire a B2B inside sales rep at a **gross salary of 70,000 TRY per month**.

| Cost line | Annual cost (TRY) |
|---|---|
| Gross salary (70,000 × 12) | 840,000 |
| Employer contributions (~20% assumption) | 168,000 |
| Meal card and transport (6,000/month) | 72,000 |
| Private health insurance | 30,000 |
| On-target commission (20% of base) | 168,000 |
| Tools and data (USD-priced, converted) | 100,000 |
| Equipment (laptop, headset, phone) | 60,000 |
| Recruiting (agency fee or equivalent time) | 126,000 |
| Severance accrual (one month gross) | 70,000 |
| Management time (share of manager's cost) | 150,000 |
| **Total first-year cost** | **~1,784,000** |

That is roughly **2.1 times the annual gross salary**. Even without the agency fee and the management time, you are still at around 1.8 times.

This example also leaves out the **mid-year raise**. Many Turkish companies adjust salaries more than once a year because of inflation. If you give a meaningful raise in July, your second-half costs rise with it. Budget for it in advance rather than treating it as a surprise.

## What does the ramp period really cost?

The table above shows what you spend. It does not show what you get back, and in the early months, that is very little.

A new B2B rep typically needs time to:

1. Learn the product and the market (weeks 1–4)
2. Build pipeline through outbound and follow-ups (months 2–3)
3. Move deals through the sales cycle (months 3–6+, depending on your cycle length)

If your average sales cycle is three months, a rep who starts prospecting in month two will rarely close a meaningful deal before month five. In our example, that means **roughly 700,000–900,000 TRY spent before the first real revenue arrives**.

Now look at break-even. Suppose your gross margin is 60%. To cover a fully loaded cost of ~1.78 million TRY, the rep needs to generate about **3 million TRY in revenue** in the first year just to pay for themselves, not to make you a profit. If most of that has to happen in the second half of the year, the monthly target in months 7–12 is steep.

This is why quotas for new reps must be ramped, not flat. I explain how to structure that in [how to set sales quotas for a sales team](/blog/how-to-set-sales-quotas-for-sales-team). Anything that shortens your sales cycle also directly shortens your payback period, so the ramp math is a strong argument for tightening your process.

## How do inflation and currency change the math?

Doing business in Turkey adds two cost variables that founders in more stable economies rarely think about.

**Inflation drives salary pressure.** If your rep's purchasing power erodes quickly, they will either ask for a raise or start taking calls from recruiters. Losing a rep after eight months means you paid for the entire ramp and got none of the payback. Retention is a cost line too, and underpaying is often the most expensive option.

**Your tools are in dollars, your revenue may be in lira.** If you sell to Turkish companies in TRY but pay for your CRM, data, and LinkedIn tools in USD, every currency move squeezes your margin. Some practical responses:

- Audit tool seats every quarter and remove unused ones
- Prefer annual plans only when you are confident about the headcount
- Consider pricing some contracts in foreign currency or indexing them, which I cover in [how to price B2B services for Turkish companies](/blog/how-to-price-b2b-services-turkish-companies)

## Should commission be part of the cost or part of the reward?

Both. Founders often design commission plans thinking, "If they don't sell, I don't pay." That is true, but it misses two points.

First, a rep with a very low base and high commission will chase easy, small deals, or leave quickly when the pipeline is thin. In Turkey, where many candidates prioritise income stability, a heavily commission-weighted offer will shrink your candidate pool.

Second, you should *want* to pay commission. If your plan is designed well, every lira of commission corresponds to profitable revenue. A common starting structure for B2B roles is a **60/40 or 70/30 split** between base and variable at target, with accelerators above quota. Then model three scenarios: 50%, 100%, and 150% of target. Make sure you can afford the 150% scenario.

## Is it cheaper to outsource than to hire?

Sometimes. The honest answer depends on what you are trying to learn.

**Hiring in-house makes sense when:**
- You already have repeatable sales motions and need capacity
- Your product requires deep, ongoing customer relationships
- You can fund a 9–12 month ramp without stress

**Outsourcing makes sense when:**
- You have not yet validated which segment, message, or channel works
- You need pipeline in weeks, not months
- You want a predictable monthly cost without severance, recruiting fees, or tool overhead

An outsourced team comes with its own tools, data, and process, so many of the hidden lines in the table above disappear. ## How to budget a sales hire: a step-by-step process

Here is the exact process I use.

1. **Set the gross salary range** based on the role (SDR, AE, field sales) and city. Istanbul usually costs more than Anatolian cities.
2. **Ask your accountant** for the precise employer cost at that gross level, including any incentives you qualify for.
3. **List all benefits** you will offer and price them annually.
4. **Model commission** at 50%, 100%, and 150% of target.
5. **Price the tool stack** in USD, then convert at a conservative exchange rate. Assume the lira will be weaker than today.
6. **Add one-off costs**: equipment and recruiting.
7. **Accrue severance** at one month of gross per year.
8. **Estimate management time** and assign it a cost.
9. **Add an inflation buffer** for mid-year raises.
10. **Calculate break-even revenue**: total cost ÷ gross margin. Then check whether that revenue is realistic given your sales cycle and ramp.
11. **Confirm runway**: make sure you can fund at least 9–12 months of fully loaded cost without the hire producing anything.

If step 10 or step 11 fails, you are not ready to hire. Fix your pricing, shorten your cycle, or start with an outsourced model.

## FAQ

### What is the total cost of an employee in Turkey compared to salary?

For a sales rep, the total first-year cost is typically 1.6 to 2.2 times the gross salary. This includes employer social security contributions, benefits, commission, tools, equipment, recruiting, severance accrual, and management time. The exact multiplier depends on your benefits package and how you recruit.

### How long does it take for a sales rep to pay for themselves?

For most B2B companies, a new rep takes 6 to 12 months to cover their fully loaded cost. The main factors are your sales cycle length, average deal size, and gross margin. Shorter cycles and higher margins bring the break-even point forward considerably.

### Do I need to pay severance to a sales rep in Turkey?

Under Turkish labour law, severance pay (kıdem tazminatı) generally applies in many termination scenarios once an employee has worked for at least one year. It is calculated as roughly one month of gross salary per year of service, up to a legal ceiling. Confirm the specifics with your accountant or a labour lawyer, and accrue for it from day one.

### Is it better to hire an SDR or an account executive first?

If you, as founder, are still closing deals, an SDR who books meetings for you is usually the cheaper and faster win. If you have plenty of pipeline but no time to close, an experienced account executive makes more sense, though they cost more. Either way, budget the fully loaded cost, not just the salary.

## Conclusion

A sales rep in Turkey costs far more than the number on their payslip. Once you add employer contributions, benefits, commission, dollar-priced tools, recruiting, management time, and severance accrual, expect to spend roughly double the gross salary in the first year. Then add the ramp period, during which you pay full cost for very little revenue.

None of this means you should not hire. A good rep is one of the best investments a B2B company can make. But it should be a funded, deliberate investment with a clear break-even target, not a hopeful bet based on a monthly salary figure.
