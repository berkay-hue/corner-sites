A B2B sales proposal that gets signed is not a sales pitch. **It is a written summary of agreements you have already reached with the buyer**: their problem, the outcome they want, the scope, the price, and the next steps. If the buyer reads your proposal and finds nothing surprising, only a clear plan they already said yes to in conversation, signing becomes the natural next step rather than a new decision.

Most proposals fail because they arrive too early, say too much about the seller, and leave the buyer to work out the "why" on their own. Over the years, through building outbound teams and closing deals across Corner Group's companies, I have seen strong opportunities stall at the proposal stage for reasons that had nothing to do with price or product. This guide covers what to do before you write, the structure that works, how to present pricing, and how to deliver and follow up so the document actually gets signed.

## Why do most B2B proposals fail?

Proposals rarely lose because the formatting was poor. They lose because of what happened, or didn't happen, before they were written. The most common failure patterns I see:

- **The proposal was sent instead of a conversation.** A rep finishes a first discovery call and says "I'll send you a proposal." There is not yet enough information to write a good one, so the document fills up with generic capabilities.
- **It talks about the seller, not the buyer.** Three pages of company history, team photos and service lists before the buyer's problem is even mentioned.
- **The economic buyer was never involved.** The champion loved the solution, but the person who signs never heard the business case and has never met you.
- **Pricing is a single take-it-or-leave-it number.** That turns the buyer's decision into "yes or no" rather than "which option."
- **There is no clear next step.** The document ends with "We look forward to working with you" instead of a date, a signature block, and a kickoff plan.

Each of these is fixable, and most of the fixes happen before you open a document.

## When should you send a proposal?

Send a proposal only when you can answer these questions confidently:

1. **What problem are we solving, in the buyer's own words?**
2. **What does it cost them to leave the problem unsolved?** (lost revenue, wasted hours, missed targets)
3. **Who signs, and have we spoken with them or at least confirmed their priorities?**
4. **What budget range have they indicated?**
5. **When do they need this to be live, and why that date?**
6. **What does their approval process look like?** (legal review, procurement, finance sign-off)

If you can't answer at least five of these six, you are not ready to write a proposal. You need another conversation. A solid qualification framework helps enormously here; if your team isn't using one consistently, read my breakdown of [how to qualify B2B leads using BANT vs MEDDIC](/blog/how-to-qualify-b2b-leads-bant-vs-meddic).

A useful rule for your team: **no proposal goes out without a verbal "yes, if."** Before writing, ask the buyer directly: "If the proposal reflects what we discussed, scope X, timeline Y, investment in the range of Z, is there anything that would stop you from moving forward?" Their answer tells you what objection to address in the document. Sometimes it tells you the deal isn't real yet.

## What should a B2B sales proposal include?

Here is the structure I recommend. It works for services, SaaS and project-based work, and it keeps the buyer at the centre of every page.

### 1. Executive summary (half a page)

This is the most-read section, and often the only section the economic buyer reads. Write it as if the CFO will read nothing else.

It should cover four things in four short paragraphs:

- **The situation:** "Your sales team of 6 reps currently books around 8 qualified meetings per month, and your target for next year requires roughly 25."
- **The goal:** "You want a predictable outbound pipeline that doesn't rely on founder referrals."
- **The recommendation:** "We propose a 6-month outbound programme covering list building, multichannel sequences and meeting booking."
- **The expected outcome and investment:** "Target: 20–25 qualified meetings per month by month three. Investment: from €X per month."

Use the buyer's language. If they called it "the pipeline problem" in your call, call it that. When buyers see their own words reflected back, it signals that you listened.

### 2. Current situation and cost of inaction

Restate what you learned in discovery: their current process, what isn't working, and what it is costing them. Be specific and use numbers *they* gave you.

For example: "With an average deal value of €15,000 and a close rate of about 20% from qualified meetings, each month at 8 meetings instead of 25 represents roughly 17 missed meetings, or around 3 to 4 deals that never happen."

This section justifies the price before you mention the price. If you skip it, your fee is compared to zero. If you include it, your fee is compared to the cost of the problem.

### 3. Proposed solution tied to outcomes

Describe what you will do, but connect every element to a result the buyer cares about. A simple format is a table or list with three columns: **what we do → why it matters → how we measure it.**

- Build and verify a target account list of 2,000 companies → ensures reps only spend time on fit accounts → measured by bounce rate and ICP match
- Run 3-step email + LinkedIn sequences → creates consistent top-of-funnel volume → measured by reply rate and meetings booked
- Weekly reporting call → keeps both sides aligned → measured by attendance and actions completed

Resist the urge to list every capability you have. Include only what solves the problem discussed.

### 4. Scope, deliverables and timeline

Ambiguity here is where deals get delayed in legal review and where projects go wrong after signing. Be precise:

- **In scope:** what you will deliver, how many, how often
- **Out of scope:** what you will not do (this protects both sides)
- **Client responsibilities:** access, approvals, a point of contact, turnaround times
- **Timeline:** week-by-week for the first month, then monthly milestones

Example: "Week 1: kickoff and ICP workshop. Week 2: list built and approved. Week 3: messaging approved. Week 4: first sequences live."

A concrete timeline also creates urgency naturally. If kickoff takes four weeks and they want results by the end of the quarter, the start date matters.

### 5. Investment (pricing)

I'll cover pricing in detail below. For now: call it "investment," place it *after* the value and scope sections, and present options rather than a single number.

### 6. Proof

One or two short, relevant examples beat a logo wall. Describe a similar situation, what you did and the result, anonymised if necessary. Match the proof to the buyer's industry or problem. A case from a company of similar size and sales motion is worth more than a famous name in an unrelated sector.

Never invent results or exaggerate them. Buyers check, and a single inflated claim undermines everything else in the document.

### 7. Next steps and signature

End with a clear, dated action plan:

1. Sign the proposal by [date] to secure a kickoff on [date]
2. Kickoff call scheduled for [date and time]
3. Invoice issued on signature; first payment due within [X] days

Include a signature block directly in the document or use an e-signature tool. Every extra step between "I agree" and "signed" is a chance for the deal to stall.

## How long should a B2B proposal be?

Shorter than you think. For most mid-market deals, **4 to 8 pages** is enough. Enterprise deals with complex scopes may need more, but even then, put the detail in appendices and keep the core document tight.

A good test: can the economic buyer understand the problem, the solution, the price and the next step in under five minutes? If not, cut. Case studies, detailed methodology, team bios and terms and conditions can all live in an appendix for those who want them.

## How should you present pricing in a proposal?

Pricing is where most buyers jump first, so it deserves careful thought.

### Offer three options

Three tiers change the buyer's question from "should we do this?" to "which version should we do?" A typical structure:

- **Option A, Essential:** solves the core problem with a narrower scope. For example, email-only outbound, 1 market, 10–15 meetings/month target.
- **Option B, Recommended:** the scope you actually discussed and believe is right. Email + LinkedIn, 2 markets, 20–25 meetings/month target.
- **Option C, Accelerated:** more coverage or speed. Adds a dedicated SDR, call outreach and additional markets.

Mark Option B clearly as your recommendation and explain why in one sentence. Most buyers will choose the middle option or anchor their negotiation around it.

### Make the pricing logic transparent

Show what's included in each option and what drives the difference in price. Hidden fees or vague line items invite suspicion and procurement pushback.

### Address the obvious objection in advance

If you know the buyer is comparing you with a cheaper alternative or hiring in-house, address it directly. A short "How this compares to hiring internally" note that sets out recruitment time, salary, tools and ramp-up period can neutralise the objection before it's raised. For handling this live, see my guide on [how to handle price objections in B2B sales calls](/blog/how-to-handle-price-objections-in-b2b-sales-calls).

### Set a validity date

"This proposal is valid until [date, typically 14–30 days]." It's a reasonable business practice, it protects you from future price changes, and it gives a natural reason to follow up.

## How do you deliver a proposal so it gets signed?

**Never just email a proposal and wait.** The single biggest improvement most teams can make is to present the proposal live.

Here's the process I recommend:

1. **Book the proposal review meeting before you write.** At the end of your final discovery call, say: "I'll put this together. Can we book 30 minutes on Thursday to go through it together?" Get the economic buyer invited.
2. **Walk through it on screen share.** Spend most of the time on the situation and solution sections, confirming at each step: "Did we capture this correctly?"
3. **Present pricing last and pause.** Let them react. Ask which option feels closest to what they need.
4. **Handle concerns in the room.** Revisions can be made the same day while the momentum is still there.
5. **Send the document right after the meeting**, with a short summary email and the agreed next step.

When you present live, you see hesitation, hear objections and can correct misunderstandings instantly. When you email a PDF, the buyer interprets it alone, often forwards it to colleagues without context, and you lose control of the narrative.

This approach also reduces the time deals spend in limbo. I've written more about this in [7 practical methods to shorten the B2B sales cycle](/blog/how-to-shorten-b2b-sales-cycle).

## How should you follow up after sending a proposal?

Even with a live review, deals don't always close on the spot. A disciplined follow-up sequence keeps things moving without becoming pushy:

- **Same day:** recap email with the proposal attached, key points and agreed next step.
- **Day 2–3:** check if any questions came up from other stakeholders. Offer to join a call with legal or finance.
- **Day 7:** share something genuinely useful, such as a relevant example, a draft kickoff agenda or answers to questions raised.
- **Before the validity date:** a direct, polite message: "The proposal is valid until Friday. Should I hold the kickoff slot for you, or has something changed on your side?"

If a buyer goes silent, ask an honest closing question: "Has this become less of a priority?" A clear "no" is more valuable than weeks of ambiguity, and your pipeline reviews become far more accurate as a result.

## Are there special considerations for proposals in Turkey?

Since much of my work is in Turkey, a few practical notes for anyone selling to Turkish companies:

- **Hierarchy matters.** Decisions often sit with a general manager or owner, even in sizeable companies. Make sure your executive summary speaks to that person, and try to get them into the proposal review meeting.
- **Currency and VAT should be explicit.** State whether prices are in TRY, EUR or USD, whether KDV (VAT) is included or excluded, and which exchange rate applies if invoicing in TRY against a foreign-currency price. Ambiguity here causes delays at the finance stage.
- **Payment terms are negotiated.** Expect discussion around payment timing. Decide your acceptable range before the meeting rather than conceding under pressure.
- **Relationships carry weight.** A proposal from someone the buyer has met and trusts carries more weight than a perfect document from a stranger. Investing in an in-person or video meeting is worth it.

## A pre-send checklist

Before any proposal leaves your team, run through this list:

- Executive summary uses the buyer's language and fits on half a page
- Cost of inaction is quantified using the buyer's own numbers
- Every solution element links to a measurable outcome
- Scope includes what's in, what's out, and client responsibilities
- Three pricing options with a clear recommendation
- Currency, taxes and payment terms stated explicitly
- Proof is relevant and accurate
- Next steps include specific dates and a signature method
- Proposal review meeting is booked with the economic buyer
- Validity date is set

Build this into your team's process so quality doesn't depend on which rep wrote the proposal. At [Sales Corner](https://salescorner.co), we treat proposal templates and checklists as part of the sales playbook, not as a one-off task, because consistency is what makes results repeatable.

## FAQ

### What is the most important section of a B2B sales proposal?

The executive summary is the most important section because it is often the only part the economic buyer reads. It should state the buyer's problem, the recommended solution, the expected outcome and the investment in about half a page. If the summary is strong, the rest of the document serves as supporting detail.

### Should I send a proposal after the first sales call?

In most cases, no. After a single call you usually lack the information about budget, decision-makers, timelines and cost of inaction needed to write a proposal that gets signed. Use the first call for discovery, confirm the key details in a follow-up conversation, and only then write the proposal.

### How many pricing options should a B2B proposal include?

Three options work best for most B2B deals: an essential version, a recommended version and an expanded version. This shifts the buyer's thinking from "yes or no" to "which option," and the middle tier usually becomes the anchor. Always mark which option you recommend and explain why.

### What should I do if a buyer goes silent after receiving a proposal?

Follow up with a short sequence that adds value, such as offering to answer stakeholder questions or sharing a draft kickoff plan. If there is still no response before the validity date, ask directly whether the project has become less of a priority. A clear answer, even a "no," helps you keep your pipeline and forecast accurate.

## Conclusion

A B2B sales proposal gets signed when it confirms decisions the buyer has already made in conversation. Do the qualification work first, structure the document around the buyer's problem and outcomes, present pricing as clear options, and deliver it live with the decision-maker in the room. Then follow up with discipline and honesty.

Get those fundamentals right and your proposals stop being documents that sit in inboxes. They become the final step in a sales process your buyers actually trust.
