The right time to hire your first Head of Sales in Turkey is after you, the founder, have closed enough deals to prove a repeatable sales motion. Look for a "builder" rather than a corporate manager: someone who will still carry a quota, write the playbook, and hire the first reps. Run a structured process with a real work sample, offer compensation in net terms that is reviewed against inflation, and give the new hire a clear 90-day plan with measurable milestones.

That is the short answer. The longer one is that this hire can make or break your company's next two years. A great first Head of Sales turns founder intuition into a system. A bad one burns six to twelve months, a chunk of runway, and often your best early customer relationships. I have seen both outcomes in Turkey's B2B market, and below is the process I recommend to founders who ask me how to get this hire right.

## What does a Head of Sales actually do in an early-stage company?

Before you write a job description, be honest about what the job is. In a 10-to-40-person company, a "Head of Sales" is not a VP who manages managers. They are usually:

- **A player-coach**: they close deals themselves, often 50% or more of their time in the first six months.
- **A system builder**: they define pipeline stages, set up the CRM properly, write the first version of the playbook, and decide which metrics matter.
- **The first sales recruiter**: they hire and onboard reps two through five.
- **The founder's translator**: they take what lives in your head about customers, objections and pricing and make it teachable.

If you expect someone to walk in and "manage a team" that doesn't exist yet, you will hire the wrong person. Write the role around the next 12 months, not the org chart you hope to have in three years.

## When are you ready to hire one?

Hiring a Head of Sales too early is one of the most expensive mistakes a founder can make. A sales leader can scale a motion that works. They are rarely the right person to *discover* whether a motion works at all.

You are usually ready when most of these are true:

1. **You have closed a meaningful number of customers yourself.** For most B2B companies that means at least 10 to 20 paying customers who were not friends or favors.
2. **You can describe your ideal customer in one sentence**, including industry, company size, and the role that signs the contract.
3. **Your sales cycle and win rate are roughly predictable.** You don't need perfect data, but you should know whether a typical deal takes three weeks or four months.
4. **You are the bottleneck.** Deals stall because you can't attend the meetings, follow-ups slip, and your product or fundraising work is suffering.
5. **You have runway for at least 12 to 18 months** after the hire, because a sales leader needs time to show results.

If you are still unsure, read my notes on [when to stop founder-led sales and hand it off](/blog/when-to-stop-founder-led-sales). If you have only a handful of customers, you probably need a first sales rep before a sales leader.

## Head of Sales, Sales Manager or VP: which title should you use?

Titles matter more in Turkey than many founders expect. Candidates coming from large corporates often treat titles as a signal of seniority and future marketability. That has two consequences:

- **"VP of Sales" attracts candidates who expect a team, a budget, and less hands-on selling.** Offering this title to a first hire who must also prospect creates a mismatch from day one.
- **"Sales Manager" may be read as too junior** by strong candidates, and it can cap your offer in their eyes.
- **"Head of Sales" is usually the right middle ground.** It signals ownership of the function without promising layers of management that don't exist.

Whatever title you choose, keep room above it. If this person succeeds and the team grows to 15 or 20 people, a VP or CRO title can become a promotion rather than a renegotiation.

## What kind of profile works best in Turkey?

Most candidates you meet will fall into one of three groups.

### The corporate sales leader

They come from global software vendors, telecom companies, banks or large distributors. They have strong enterprise relationships, polished presentation skills, and experience with formal forecasting. The risk is that they are used to brand recognition, marketing-generated leads, pre-sales engineers, and legal teams. At a startup, they will have none of that.

**Ask yourself:** has this person ever built pipeline from zero, without a famous logo behind them?

### The startup builder

They have been an early sales hire or first sales leader at a smaller company, sometimes one that failed. They are comfortable with messy CRMs, cold outreach, and closing deals with incomplete product features. The risk is that they may lack experience managing larger teams or selling into very large enterprises.

For a first Head of Sales, **this is usually the profile I recommend**, especially if your deal sizes are small-to-mid market.

### The promoted top rep

Sometimes the best candidate is already in your company. Promoting a strong rep has clear advantages, including product knowledge, customer trust and cultural fit. But the skills that make someone a great closer are not the same skills that make a great leader. I've written about how to judge this in [when to promote a top sales rep to manager](/blog/promote-top-sales-rep-to-manager).

### Turkey-specific traits worth screening for

- **Relationship depth in your target sector.** Turkish B2B buying is heavily relationship-driven, especially in manufacturing, construction, retail, logistics and finance. A candidate who already knows how purchasing works in your sector can shorten sales cycles noticeably.
- **Comfort with long payment terms and collections.** In many Turkish industries, getting paid is part of the sale. A Head of Sales who ignores payment terms will book revenue you never collect.
- **English and international readiness.** If you sell or plan to sell outside Turkey, test their English in a live role-play, not just through a CV line.
- **Outbound discipline.** Many experienced Turkish sellers built careers on inbound demand and existing networks. If your growth depends on outbound, test it explicitly.

## Where do you find candidates in Turkey?

The best Head of Sales candidates are rarely actively applying. Plan for a mix of channels:

- **Your own network and your investors' networks.** Ask specifically: "Who is the best early sales hire you've worked with?" This question works better than "Do you know any sales leaders?"
- **Founders of companies one stage ahead of you.** They often know sales leaders who want to do it again at an earlier company.
- **LinkedIn outreach.** Search for people who were among the first sales hires at Turkish scale-ups, or who have led sales at companies selling to your target buyer. A personal message from the founder gets much better responses than a recruiter template.
- **Specialized recruiters.** They are useful for senior roles, but brief them carefully. Generic recruiters will send you corporate profiles by default.
- **Customers and partners.** Sometimes the best candidate is the person who sold to *you* well.

Expect the search to take two to four months. Senior candidates in Turkey often have notice periods, and many will want to collect year-end bonuses before moving.

## How should you structure the interview process?

An unstructured process with a few friendly coffees is how founders end up hiring the most charming candidate instead of the most effective one. Here is a process I'd use:

1. **Founder screening call (30–45 minutes).** Cover motivation, the size of their past deals, how they built pipeline, and what they expect in compensation. Ask whether they think in net or gross salary now, because it saves confusion later.
2. **Deep-dive on past results (60 minutes).** Go through two or three deals they personally closed. Ask about the first contact, the stakeholders, the objections, and why the deal nearly fell apart. Vague answers are a warning sign.
3. **Work sample (take-home plus presentation).** Give them a realistic case: "Here is our ICP, our current pipeline numbers, and our pricing. Present your first 90 days, including a target account list for one segment and a draft outreach sequence." Give them a week, and keep it under five to six hours of work.
4. **Live role-play.** You or a team member plays a skeptical buyer. Watch how they discover needs, handle price pushback, and secure a next step.
5. **Team and cross-functional interviews.** Product and customer success should meet them. Sales leaders who blame other teams for lost deals show it early.
6. **Reference checks, including backchannel ones.** Speak to at least one former manager and one person who reported to them. Ask: "Would you hire them again for an early-stage role, and why?"

For more on the kinds of answers strong candidates give, my article on [acing a sales manager interview](/blog/how-to-ace-a-sales-manager-interview) is written for candidates but works just as well as a hiring guide.

## How much does a Head of Sales cost in Turkey?

Compensation varies widely by sector, company stage, and whether the candidate comes from a multinational, so I won't quote market figures here. What I can share is how to *structure* the package so it works in the Turkish context.

### Base and variable split

For a player-coach Head of Sales, a common structure is **60–70% base and 30–40% variable** at target. Example: if total target compensation is 100 units, 65 is fixed and 35 is paid when they and the team hit target. Too little variable and they behave like an administrator. Too much and you'll get short-term deal chasing at the expense of building the team.

Tie variable pay to a mix of:

- **Team revenue or new ARR** (the largest component)
- **Personal closed deals** in the first 6–12 months
- **Collected revenue**, not just signed contracts, especially in sectors with long payment terms

My guide on [designing a sales commission plan that works](/blog/how-to-design-a-sales-commission-plan) covers accelerators, caps and clawbacks in more detail.

### Net versus gross

Turkish candidates very often negotiate in **net monthly salary**. Founders who budget in gross employer cost get unpleasant surprises once SGK contributions and income tax brackets are factored in. Always model the full employer cost before you make an offer. I break this down in [what a sales rep really costs in Turkey](/blog/what-does-a-sales-rep-cost-in-turkey), and the same logic applies, at a higher level, to a sales leader.

### Inflation and reviews

With high inflation, a salary that looks generous in January can feel weak by autumn. Senior candidates know this. Options founders use include:

- **Committing to scheduled salary reviews** (for example, every six months) in the offer
- **Partially indexing pay** to a foreign currency for roles that sell internationally
- **Adding equity or phantom shares** so the upside isn't purely in lira

### Benefits that candidates expect

Private health insurance, meal cards, a company phone and laptop are standard expectations at this level. A car or car allowance is common in field-sales-heavy sectors. Clarify these early so they don't derail the final offer.

## What legal and contract points should you get right?

This is not legal advice, so work with a Turkish employment lawyer, but cover these points:

- **Probation period.** Turkish labor law allows a probation period of up to two months in individual contracts. That is short for judging a sales leader, so your 90-day plan needs early milestones you can assess within that window.
- **Notice periods and severance.** Notice periods increase with tenure, and severance (kıdem tazminatı) becomes relevant after one year of service. Factor this into your risk planning.
- **Non-compete and non-solicitation.** If they will hold your key customer relationships, have a properly drafted clause reviewed by a lawyer. Overly broad clauses are hard to enforce.
- **Commission terms in writing.** Define when commission is earned (signed, invoiced, or collected) and what happens to pipeline if they leave.

## What should the first 90 days look like?

Agree on the plan before they start, ideally building on the one they presented in the interview.

**Days 1–30: Learn and sell.** Shadow every founder sales call they can. Interview 10 or more existing customers. Review lost deals. Close at least a few deals personally, ideally from existing pipeline.

**Days 31–60: Build the system.** Define pipeline stages and exit criteria in the CRM. Draft the first sales playbook. Agree on the five or six metrics that will be reviewed weekly. Start outbound in one focused segment.

**Days 61–90: Prepare to scale.** Present a hiring plan for the next two to three reps, a forecast for the next two quarters, and a clear view of which segments to prioritize or drop.

If pipeline is thin when they start, consider bringing in outside support so they aren't spending their first quarter only on prospecting. ## What mistakes do founders make with this hire?

- **Hiring for the logo.** A big-brand CV doesn't mean they can sell without the brand.
- **Not letting go of sales.** If you still approve every discount and jump into every deal, your Head of Sales has responsibility without authority.
- **Unclear targets.** "Grow revenue" is not a target. "Reach X in new monthly recurring revenue by month 9, with two hired and ramped reps" is.
- **Judging too early or too late.** Month two is too early to judge revenue. Month twelve is too late to notice they never built a pipeline.
- **Skipping references.** Most bad sales leadership hires I have seen could have been spotted with two honest reference calls.

## FAQ

### How many customers should I have before hiring a Head of Sales?

There's no fixed number, but most B2B founders should have personally closed at least 10 to 20 paying customers first. That gives you enough evidence of a repeatable motion for a sales leader to scale. With fewer customers, a hands-on sales rep is usually the better first hire.

### Should my first Head of Sales carry a personal quota?

Yes, in most early-stage companies they should. A personal quota for the first six to twelve months keeps them close to customers and proves the playbook works. The personal portion can shrink as the team grows.

### How long does it take to hire a Head of Sales in Turkey?

Plan for roughly two to four months from opening the role to their first day. Strong candidates are usually employed, may have notice periods, and often time their move around bonus payments. Starting your search before you're desperate gives you far better options.

### How do I know if my new Head of Sales is working out?

Look at leading indicators before revenue: pipeline created, meetings held, conversion between stages, and the quality of the playbook and hiring plan. By the end of 90 days you should see a clear system and a healthier pipeline, even if closed revenue is still catching up. If neither is improving by month four or five, address it directly.

## Conclusion

Hiring your first Head of Sales in Turkey isn't about finding the most impressive CV. You need someone who will sell, build, and hire in a market where relationships, payment terms and inflation all shape how deals get done. Get the timing right, choose a builder over a title-seeker, run a structured process with a real work sample, design compensation for the Turkish context, and give them a 90-day plan with clear milestones.
