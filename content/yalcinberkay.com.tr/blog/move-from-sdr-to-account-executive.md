Most people who start their sales careers as SDRs have the same goal: get promoted to Account Executive. The title carries more responsibility, a bigger variable component, and the chance to actually close the deals you've been booking meetings for. But many talented SDRs stay stuck for far longer than they expected. Some hit their numbers every month and still get passed over. Others get promoted too early and struggle through their first two quarters as an AE.

I've seen this from several angles: as someone who [moved into sales from civil engineering](/blog/from-civil-engineering-to-sales-a-journey-of-self-discovery), as a founder building outbound teams, and as a manager deciding who is ready for the next step. This article is a practical roadmap for SDRs who want to make the move, and for managers who want to build a promotion path that works.

## Why the SDR-to-AE Jump Is Harder Than It Looks

From the outside, the AE role looks like "the same job, just later in the funnel." It isn't. The SDR role rewards **volume, consistency, and resilience**. You win by making the next call, sending the next email, and handling the next rejection without losing energy.

The AE role rewards **judgment, control, and depth**. You win by:

- Understanding a customer's business well enough to build a real case for change
- Managing several stakeholders who each want something different
- Forecasting accurately and knowing which deals are real
- Negotiating without giving away margin
- Keeping a deal moving for weeks or months, not minutes

An SDR who books 15 meetings a month has proven they can open doors. That doesn't yet prove they can walk a buying committee through a 60-day evaluation and get a signature. Your job is to close that gap *before* you get the title, so the promotion is a formality rather than a gamble.

## What Actually Changes When You Become an AE

Before building a plan, be honest about what you're signing up for. Here are the most important shifts:

1. **Your metric changes from activity to revenue.** As an SDR, you control your inputs. As an AE, you're measured on outcomes that depend on many factors you don't fully control.
2. **Your sales cycle gets longer.** Instead of instant feedback, you might wait weeks to learn whether your discovery call was good.
3. **Your pipeline becomes your responsibility.** In many teams, AEs are expected to source a meaningful share of their own pipeline, not just work inbound leads and SDR-booked meetings.
4. **Your mistakes become more expensive.** A bad cold email costs nothing. A poorly handled negotiation can cost the company a large deal.
5. **Your relationships matter more.** You'll work closely with marketing, customer success, product, finance, and sometimes legal.

If those shifts excite you, keep going. If they sound draining, that's useful information too. Some excellent salespeople build careers as senior SDRs, SDR team leads, or partnership managers instead.

## Step 1: Become Undeniable in Your Current Role

No one promotes an SDR who is inconsistent. The first requirement is simple: **hit your quota reliably**. Not once, but repeatedly. Many teams informally look for something like three to six consecutive months at or above target before they seriously consider a promotion.

But numbers alone are not enough. Managers also watch *how* you hit them:

- **Quality of meetings:** Do your booked meetings convert to qualified opportunities, or do AEs complain they're wasting time?
- **Process discipline:** Is your CRM clean? Are your notes useful to the AE taking the call?
- **Coachability:** When your manager gives feedback, do you apply it within a week, or do you repeat the same mistakes?
- **Team contribution:** Do you share what's working, or keep your best sequences to yourself?

A practical exercise: every month, calculate your meeting-to-opportunity conversion rate. If you booked 20 meetings and 8 became qualified pipeline, that's 40%. Track it over time and aim to raise it. This one number tells your manager more about your AE potential than raw meeting volume ever will, because it shows you understand what a *good* prospect looks like.

## Step 2: Start Doing AE Work Before You Have the Title

The fastest way to get promoted is to prove you can already do the job. Here's how to build AE skills while still being an SDR:

### Shadow real deals

Ask two or three AEs if you can join their discovery calls, demos, and negotiation calls as a silent observer. Aim for at least **two shadowed calls per week**. After each call, write down three things:

1. What question unlocked the most useful information?
2. Where did the prospect hesitate or push back?
3. What would you have done differently?

Then ask the AE for five minutes to compare notes. Most good AEs are happy to help someone who is genuinely curious.

### Run your own discovery

Ask your manager whether you can run the first 10–15 minutes of discovery on meetings you've booked, with the AE taking over afterward. This builds real reps in a low-risk environment.

### Follow your meetings to the end

Don't book a meeting and forget about it. Track every opportunity you sourced through to closed-won or closed-lost. When a deal is lost, find out why. When it's won, find out what tipped it. Over six months, you'll build a mental library of how deals actually move, which most SDRs never develop.

### Sit in on pipeline reviews

If your team runs a structured weekly session, ask to attend. Listening to how managers question AEs about deal stages, next steps, and risks is one of the best free educations in sales. ## Step 3: Build Business Acumen

The biggest gap I see in SDRs moving up is not sales technique. It's **business understanding**. AEs need to talk to directors, VPs, and founders about their priorities, budgets, and trade-offs. That conversation falls flat if you don't understand how businesses make money.

Start with these basics:

- **Learn to read a P&L at a high level.** Know what revenue, gross margin, operating expenses, and EBITDA mean, and why a CFO cares about each.
- **Understand your product's ROI story.** If your product saves a team 10 hours a week, what's that worth in money? If it increases conversion by a few percentage points, what does that mean for annual revenue?
- **Study your customers' industries.** Pick your top two verticals and read what their leaders are worried about: regulation, hiring, margin pressure, digital transformation.
- **Understand how buying decisions are made.** Who signs? Who influences? Who can block? How does procurement work in a mid-sized company versus an enterprise?

A simple weekly habit: pick one target account and write a half-page summary of their business, likely challenges, and how your product could help. After 12 weeks, you'll have 12 account plans and a much sharper business mind.

## Step 4: Learn to Own Pipeline

Many new AEs are surprised by how much of their success depends on pipeline they generate themselves. Inbound fluctuates. SDR capacity gets reallocated. Marketing campaigns underperform. The AEs who consistently hit their number are usually the ones who never stop prospecting.

Here's the good news: as an SDR, this is your superpower. Keep it. The trick is to adapt it to the AE context:

- **Fewer, deeper accounts.** Instead of 300 contacts in a sequence, an AE might work 30–50 strategic accounts with multi-threaded outreach.
- **Higher-level personas.** You'll increasingly target economic buyers, not just end users.
- **More research per touch.** Personalization moves from "I saw your LinkedIn post" to "I noticed your company opened two new offices this year; here's how teams in that position typically handle onboarding."

## Step 5: Make Your Ambition Visible

A surprising number of SDRs never clearly tell their manager they want to be promoted. They assume good performance will speak for itself. Sometimes it does. Often it doesn't.

Have an explicit conversation. Here's a simple structure:

1. **State the goal:** "I want to move into an AE role within the next 9–12 months."
2. **Ask for the criteria:** "What would you need to see from me to feel confident I'm ready?"
3. **Agree on milestones:** Turn vague criteria into specifics: quota attainment, conversion rate, number of shadowed calls, a mock demo, a certification.
4. **Schedule check-ins:** Review progress monthly, not once a year.

Then write it down and send a short recap email after the meeting. This creates shared accountability and protects you if your manager changes.

Building visibility outside your team helps too. Sharing what you're learning about your market on LinkedIn, such as short posts on buyer objections, industry trends, or lessons from calls, positions you as someone who thinks beyond activity metrics. It also starts building the personal brand that will help you as an AE when prospects look you up before a meeting.

## Step 6: Prepare for the Promotion Process

Many companies run a formal process for SDR-to-AE promotions: a mock discovery call, a mock demo, or a deal case study presented to leadership. Treat it like a job interview for a new company, because in practice, it is one.

A preparation plan for the four weeks before:

- **Week 1:** Pick a real or realistic prospect. Research their business deeply and build a discovery question list.
- **Week 2:** Run two practice discovery calls with AEs or your manager playing the buyer. Record them and review them honestly.
- **Week 3:** Build and rehearse a tailored demo. Focus on the prospect's problems, not your product's features.
- **Week 4:** Practice handling objections around pricing, timing, and competitors. Prepare a clear mutual action plan showing next steps to close.

When you present, show that you understand **why** a customer buys, not just **how** your product works. That's the signal leaders are looking for.

## Your First 90 Days as an AE

Getting the title is the start, not the finish. The first quarter as an AE sets your reputation. A simple framework:

### Days 1–30: Learn and organize

- Review every open opportunity you inherit. Classify each as real, at-risk, or dead.
- Meet your key internal partners: marketing, customer success, solutions engineering.
- Build your target account list and outreach plan.

### Days 31–60: Execute and build pipeline

- Aim to have pipeline coverage of roughly three times your quarterly target, a common rule of thumb, though the right multiple depends on your win rates.
- Run discovery on every new opportunity with a consistent qualification framework.
- Ask for feedback on at least one recorded call per week.

### Days 61–90: Close and refine

- Push your most qualified deals toward clear decisions, even if the answer is "no."
- Review your forecast accuracy: how close were your predictions to reality?
- Identify your two biggest skill gaps and build a plan for the next quarter.

Expect your first quarter to be uneven. Longer sales cycles mean many of your early efforts won't show results until month four or five. That's normal.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Across the teams I've worked with, the same mistakes come up again and again:

- **Stopping prospecting after promotion.** This is the single most common reason new AEs struggle in their second quarter.
- **Talking too much in discovery.** SDR habits of pitching quickly don't serve you when the goal is to understand.
- **Chasing every deal equally.** Learn to disqualify early. A fast "no" is worth more than a slow "maybe."
- **Avoiding hard conversations.** Asking about budget, decision process, and competitors feels uncomfortable. Ask anyway.
- **Discounting too early.** Price objections are often value objections. Rebuild the value before touching the price.
- **Comparing yourself to senior AEs.** They have years of pattern recognition. Compare yourself to where you were last quarter.

## A Note for Managers and Founders

If you lead a sales team, the SDR-to-AE path is one of your most powerful retention and hiring tools. Promoting from within means AEs who already know your product, your ICP, and your culture.

But it only works with structure. Define clear, written promotion criteria. Give SDRs real exposure to AE work. Don't promote your best SDR just to keep them happy if they aren't ready, because a failed promotion often costs you both a great SDR and a struggling AE. ## Conclusion

Moving from SDR to Account Executive isn't about waiting your turn. It's about deliberately building the skills an AE needs while still excelling at the job you have today. Hit your numbers consistently. Shadow and run real calls. Develop business acumen. Keep prospecting. Make your ambition clear and turn it into measurable milestones. By the time the promotion comes, you should already be doing much of the work.

The SDRs who make this jump successfully are rarely the loudest or the most naturally gifted. They're the most curious and the most intentional about their growth.
