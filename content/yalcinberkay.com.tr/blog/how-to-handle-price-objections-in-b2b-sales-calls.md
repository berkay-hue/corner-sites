When a B2B buyer says "it's too expensive," they are rarely making a final judgment about your price. Usually they are telling you that the value isn't clear yet, that they don't have the budget authority, or that they are testing how firm your number really is. **The best way to handle a price objection is to pause, find out which of these is actually happening, and then respond to that specific concern instead of reaching for a discount.**

In this article, I'll walk through the framework I use and teach my teams for handling price objections on B2B sales calls. It covers what to say, what to ask, when a discount makes sense, and how to stop most price objections from coming up at all. These lessons come from years of running outbound teams and selling B2B services, including in Turkey, where negotiating on price is close to a cultural expectation.

## Why do buyers raise price objections in the first place?

Most reps hear a price objection as rejection. It's usually something else. In my experience, almost every "too expensive" comes from one of five sources:

- **Value gap:** The buyer doesn't yet see how your solution connects to a problem worth solving. The price looks high because the return looks small.
- **Budget reality:** The buyer sees the value, but the money isn't allocated, or it sits in a different department's budget.
- **Comparison:** They have a cheaper quote from a competitor, a freelancer, or an internal team, and they want you to justify the difference.
- **Negotiation habit:** Some buyers, and some procurement teams, push back on every price by default. It's part of their job.
- **Authority gap:** The person on the call can't approve the spend and is anticipating pushback from their boss or CFO.

Each one needs a different response. If you offer a 15% discount to someone with a value gap, you haven't solved the problem. You've made your product look cheaper and still unconvincing. If you launch into a long ROI pitch for someone who is just following procurement habit, you look defensive. Before you can handle the objection, you have to diagnose it.

## What does "it's too expensive" actually mean?

The fastest way to find out is to ask, and most reps skip this step. They hear the word "expensive" and start explaining, justifying, or conceding. The rep who asks one calm question nearly always learns more than the rep who talks for three minutes.

Here are a few diagnostic questions that work well on live calls:

- "Too expensive compared to what?"
- "When you say it's more than expected, what number did you have in mind?"
- "Is it the total amount, or the way the payment is structured?"
- "If price weren't a factor, is this the solution you'd want to move forward with?"

The last one matters most. If the buyer says "yes, honestly, this is exactly what we need," you're dealing with budget or negotiation, and you can talk about terms. If they hesitate or say "well, I'm still not sure about X," the real objection was never price. It's X, and price was the polite way to say it.

## A 5-step framework for handling price objections

This is the sequence I teach. It isn't a script. It's a structure that keeps reps from panicking when money comes up.

### 1. Pause and acknowledge

Don't rush to fill the silence. Take a breath and acknowledge what they said without agreeing that the price is wrong. Something like:

> "That's fair. It's a real investment, and I'd rather talk about it openly than have it sit in the background."

This shows confidence. Reps who answer instantly and nervously signal that they expected the objection and don't fully believe in their price.

### 2. Clarify with a question

Use one of the diagnostic questions above. Your goal is to figure out which of the five sources you're dealing with. Don't answer an objection you haven't understood yet.

### 3. Isolate the objection

Once you know what the concern is, confirm it's the only one:

> "So if we could find a way to make the numbers work, is there anything else that would stop you from moving forward?"

If they bring up something new, such as implementation time, a stakeholder who isn't convinced, or a contract they're locked into, you've just found the real blocker. If they say "no, it's really just the price," you now have a clear, single problem to solve together.

### 4. Reframe around value and the cost of inaction

This is where preparation pays off. Bring the conversation back to the buyer's own numbers, the ones they gave you earlier in discovery. A worked example:

Say you sell an outbound lead generation service for €4,000 per month. During discovery, the buyer told you:

- Their average deal size is €25,000 per year.
- Their sales team needs about 8 more qualified meetings per month to hit target.
- They usually close roughly 1 in 5 qualified meetings.

The reframe:

> "You mentioned 8 extra qualified meetings a month would get your team to target. At your close rate, that's around one or two new customers a month. At €25,000 per customer, even one closed deal covers about six months of our fee. So the question is less whether €4,000 is expensive and more what it costs you each month the pipeline stays where it is."

Notice that you are using **their** numbers, not yours. Buyers argue with vendor claims. They rarely argue with figures they gave you themselves. This is also why weak discovery leads to more price objections: if you never asked about deal size, close rates, or the cost of the problem, you have nothing to reframe with.

### 5. Trade, don't cave

If the buyer still needs a lower number after you've reframed, don't just drop the price. **Every concession should come with something in return.** That keeps your pricing credible and turns the negotiation into a collaboration.

## Should you ever offer a discount?

Yes, but deliberately, and never as your first move. A discount given too early teaches the buyer that your first price wasn't real, and that habit carries into every renewal.

When a discount is justified, tie it to something that has real value for your business. Some common trades:

- **Longer commitment:** "If you sign a 12-month agreement instead of month-to-month, I can take 10% off the monthly fee."
- **Upfront payment:** "If you pay the first quarter upfront, we can bring the price down by 5–8%." This is especially valuable in high-inflation markets.
- **Reduced scope:** "We can get to your budget by starting with two target segments instead of four, then expanding once you see results."
- **Case study or referral:** "If you agree to a case study after 90 days, we can adjust the price for the first term."
- **Faster decision:** "If we can get this signed by the end of the month, I can hold the current pricing before our rate update."

A practical rule I give my teams: **decide your walk-away number before the call, not during it.** If your floor is €3,400 and your list price is €4,000, you know you have €600 of room, and you know which trades unlock it. Reps who improvise discounts on a call almost always give away more than they needed to.

It also helps to remove scope instead of price where you can. Lowering the price for the same deliverables says your price was inflated. Lowering the price for fewer deliverables says your price is fair and you're flexible. ## What should you say when a buyer compares you to a cheaper competitor?

This is one of the hardest versions of the objection, because the buyer has a concrete number in front of them. Don't trash the competitor. It makes you look insecure and can insult the buyer's judgment if they've worked with that vendor before.

Instead, make the comparison fair and specific:

1. **Ask what's included.** "Can you walk me through what their proposal covers?" Cheaper quotes often leave out onboarding, reporting, data quality, or dedicated people.
2. **Compare outcomes, not line items.** "If their option delivers 4 qualified meetings a month and ours delivers 10, the cost per meeting is actually lower with us."
3. **Name the risk honestly.** "The cheaper option may be the right fit if you only need X. If the goal is Y, here's where I'd expect the gap to show up."
4. **Be willing to lose.** Sometimes the cheaper competitor really is the better fit for that buyer. Saying so builds trust, and I've seen buyers come back months later because the rep was honest.

## How do you prevent price objections before they happen?

The best price objection is the one you never get. Most of them can be headed off earlier in the sales process.

**Qualify budget early.** If you only find out at the proposal stage that the buyer's budget is a third of your price, the problem is your qualification, not your objection handling. Frameworks like BANT and MEDDIC exist for this. I compare them in [how to qualify B2B leads with BANT vs MEDDIC](/blog/how-to-qualify-b2b-leads-bant-vs-meddic).

**Anchor a price range on the first or second call.** Don't save the number for a big reveal. Something like "Most clients in your situation invest between €3,000 and €6,000 a month, depending on scope. Does that fit what you're expecting?" filters out bad-fit buyers early and makes the final proposal feel familiar.

**Quantify the problem during discovery.** Ask about deal sizes, conversion rates, team costs, and the cost of the status quo. Without these numbers, you can't do the value reframe in step 4.

**Prepare for the objection before every important call.** I use AI tools to research the prospect's company, likely budget pressures, competitors they might be comparing us to, and possible objections, then rehearse responses. I describe this process in [how to use AI to prepare for sales calls](/blog/how-to-use-ai-to-prepare-for-sales-calls).

**Fill your pipeline with the right accounts.** Many price objections come from prospects who were never a good fit: companies too small, too early, or without the problem you solve. When the pipeline is built on clear targeting and good data, the price conversations get much easier. ## Handling price objections in Turkey

Doing business in Turkey adds a few layers to price conversations that are worth knowing whether you're local or selling into the market from abroad.

- **Negotiation is expected.** Many Turkish buyers see the first price as an opening offer. That doesn't mean you should inflate your list price, but it does mean you should plan your trades in advance and never treat the first pushback as a crisis.
- **Currency matters.** With exchange rate volatility, whether you quote in TRY, EUR, or USD can matter more than the number itself. Some buyers object to price when they're really objecting to currency risk. Offering a TRY price locked for a fixed period, or a quarterly review clause, can solve the objection without a discount.
- **Payment terms are a lever.** Upfront payment is worth more to you in an inflationary environment, and payment flexibility is worth a lot to the buyer. Trading terms often works better than trading price.
- **Relationships carry weight.** A buyer who trusts you will negotiate less aggressively. Time spent building the relationship before the proposal, including meeting in person where possible, often shows up as fewer price objections later.

## How do you train your team to handle price objections?

Individual skill isn't enough. If every rep handles price differently, your pricing becomes inconsistent, and buyers in the same industry talk to each other.

1. **Document your approach.** Write down the common objections, the diagnostic questions, the approved trades, and the discount floors. This belongs in your sales playbook. If you don't have one yet, here's [how to write a sales playbook for your team](/blog/how-to-write-a-sales-playbook).
2. **Role-play weekly.** Fifteen minutes in a team meeting where one person plays a tough buyer is worth more than any training deck. Rotate the objection types: value, budget, competitor, procurement.
3. **Review recorded calls.** Listen for the moment price comes up. Did the rep pause? Did they ask a question, or jump straight to justifying? Did they concede without getting anything back?
4. **Set clear discount approval rules.** For example, reps can offer up to 5% with a trade, 5–10% needs manager approval, and anything above that needs founder or sales director sign-off. This protects margins and slows down panic discounting.
5. **Track discount rates per rep.** If one rep closes at an average 18% discount while the rest of the team averages 6%, that's a coaching conversation, not a pricing problem.

## FAQ

### What is the best response when a prospect says "your price is too high"?

Start by pausing, then ask a clarifying question such as "Too high compared to what?" or "What were you expecting to invest?" The answer tells you whether you're dealing with a value gap, a budget issue, a competitor comparison, or a negotiation tactic. Each needs a different response, so don't defend or discount until you know which one it is.

### Should I give a discount to close a B2B deal?

Only after you've confirmed price is the real blocker and you've reframed around value. When you do discount, always trade it for something, such as a longer contract, upfront payment, reduced scope, or a case study. Decide your lowest acceptable price before the call so you don't improvise concessions under pressure.

### How can I avoid price objections altogether?

Qualify budget early, share a realistic price range on the first or second call, and quantify the cost of the buyer's problem during discovery. When the buyer has already heard a range and calculated what their problem costs them, the final proposal rarely comes as a shock. Targeting well-fit accounts from the start also removes many objections before they happen.

### How do I handle a buyer who compares me to a cheaper competitor?

Don't criticize the competitor. Ask what their proposal includes, compare expected outcomes rather than line items, and be honest about where the cheaper option might fall short for this buyer's goals. If the competitor is genuinely a better fit, saying so builds trust that often pays off later.

## Conclusion

Price objections are not the end of a B2B deal. Usually they're a sign the buyer is seriously considering it. The reps and teams who handle them well do a few things consistently: they pause instead of panicking, ask before they answer, use the buyer's own numbers to reframe value, and trade concessions instead of giving them away. Most importantly, they prevent objections upstream through good qualification, early price anchoring, and solid discovery.

If you build these habits into your playbook and coach them every week, price stops being the scariest moment in the sales call and becomes another part of the conversation.
