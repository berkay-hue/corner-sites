A sales CV that gets interviews proves, in numbers, that you can generate revenue. Lead with quantified results like quota attainment, revenue closed, pipeline created, and deal sizes. Add the context behind those numbers (what you sold, to whom, how long the cycle was) and keep the whole thing to one or two pages that a hiring manager can scan in under a minute. Everything else on the page is secondary.

I've read a lot of sales CVs while building outbound teams at Corner Group, and the pattern is consistent. Most candidates describe their *job*. The ones who get interviews describe their *results*. This guide walks through exactly how to write the second kind of CV, with before-and-after examples, a simple formula for every bullet point, and the mistakes that quietly get good salespeople rejected.

## What do sales hiring managers actually look for in a CV?

When I open a sales CV, I'm trying to answer four questions, roughly in this order:

1. **Can this person hit a number?** Quota attainment, revenue, meetings booked, pipeline generated.
2. **Have they sold something similar?** Similar deal size, buyer type, sales cycle, and motion (inbound, outbound, field, inside).
3. **Do they have a repeatable process?** Or did they get lucky with one big account?
4. **Are they trending up?** Promotions, growing territories, bigger deals over time.

If your CV answers those four questions in the first half of page one, you're already ahead of most applicants. If it opens with "Results-driven, passionate sales professional with excellent communication skills," you've used valuable space to say nothing. Every candidate claims to be results-driven. Your CV is where you prove it.

Remember who is reading. Sales managers are busy, usually reviewing CVs between pipeline reviews and calls. They skim. Your job is to make the skim work in your favour.

## How long should a sales CV be?

- **0–3 years of experience (SDR, BDR, junior AE):** one page.
- **3–10 years (AE, senior AE, team lead):** one to two pages.
- **10+ years or leadership roles:** two pages, rarely more.

The rule is simple: if a line doesn't help prove you can generate revenue in the role you're applying for, cut it. A two-page CV with ten strong bullets beats a three-page CV with thirty weak ones.

## Step 1: Write a headline and summary that sell

The top third of your CV is prime real estate. Use it like a cold email subject line and opening: specific, relevant, and built around outcomes.

**Weak:**
> Sales Professional
> Motivated and hardworking salesperson with strong interpersonal skills, looking for a challenging role in a dynamic company.

**Strong:**
> Account Executive | B2B SaaS | Mid-Market
> Closed new business for 4 years selling HR software to companies with 100–1,000 employees. Averaged 112% of annual quota across 3 years, with a typical deal size of $25K–$60K ARR and a 60–90 day sales cycle. Strongest in outbound-sourced deals and multi-stakeholder evaluations.

The strong version tells the reader what you sell, who you sell to, how well you do it, and what kind of deals you're used to. That's four filtering questions answered in three lines.

Keep the summary to 2–4 lines. Tailor the headline to the job title you're applying for, as long as it honestly reflects your experience.

## Step 2: Turn responsibilities into quantified achievements

This is the single biggest improvement most sales CVs need. Hiring managers already know what an SDR or AE does. They don't need "Responsible for prospecting new clients." They need to know how *well* you did it.

Use this formula for every bullet:

**Action verb + what you did + measurable result + context**

Here are some before-and-after examples:

**Before:** Responsible for generating leads through cold calling and email.
**After:** Booked 18–22 qualified meetings per month through cold calling and multi-step email sequences, ranking #2 of 9 SDRs over 12 months.

**Before:** Managed key accounts.
**After:** Managed a book of 35 mid-market accounts worth $1.2M ARR; grew net revenue retention to 115% through upsells of the analytics module.

**Before:** Exceeded targets.
**After:** Achieved 108%, 121%, and 97% of annual quota (2021–2023), closing 41 new logos with an average contract value of $18K.

Notice the last example includes a year below 100%. That's fine, and it's often a good idea. A track record that shows one honest miss alongside strong years reads as more credible than a perfect wall of "150%+" numbers. Experienced managers know that every rep has a tough quarter. What they want to see is the overall pattern.

### Which numbers should you include?

Pick the metrics that matter most for the role you're targeting:

- **SDR/BDR:** meetings booked, meetings held, pipeline generated (in currency), conversion from meeting to opportunity, activity volume if it was exceptional.
- **Account Executive:** quota attainment (%), revenue closed, number of deals, average deal size, win rate, sales cycle length.
- **Account Manager / CSM:** retention rate, net revenue retention, upsell/cross-sell revenue, churn reduced.
- **Sales Manager:** team quota attainment, team size, reps hired and ramped, reps promoted, forecast accuracy.

### What if you don't have exact numbers?

Many people left a job without exporting their CRM dashboards. You still have options:

- **Use rankings:** "Top 3 of 15 reps in new revenue for 2023."
- **Use ranges you can defend:** "Consistently booked 15+ meetings per month."
- **Use recognition:** "President's Club 2022," "Rep of the Quarter, Q3 2023."
- **Use relative growth:** "Grew territory revenue roughly 40% year over year."

One firm rule: **never inflate numbers.** Sales leaders will ask you to walk through them in the interview, and reference checks often confirm them. A number you can't explain in detail is worse than no number at all.

## Step 3: Give your numbers context

"Closed $800K in revenue" means very different things depending on the situation. Is that from 3 enterprise deals or 200 SMB transactions? Inbound leads handed to you, or deals you sourced yourself?

Add a short context line under each role, either in italics or as the first bullet:

> *Sold a workflow automation platform to logistics companies in Turkey and the Gulf. Deal size $10K–$40K, 2–4 month cycle, ~70% self-sourced pipeline.*

This one line lets a hiring manager instantly map your experience to their role. If they sell $30K deals to logistics companies on a 3-month cycle, you've just become a very attractive candidate. If they sell $500K enterprise contracts, they'll know you'd need to stretch, and that's also useful for both sides.

Context also protects you. If your company had a weak product year or a territory change, a single line like "Territory reassigned mid-year; achieved 94% on adjusted quota" explains the number before anyone has to ask.

## Step 4: Show your process and tools

Results get attention, but process is what convinces a manager that your results are repeatable. Include one or two bullets per role that show *how* you sell:

- "Built a 7-touch outbound sequence (email, LinkedIn, phone) that became the team template, improving reply rates across the SDR team."
- "Ran structured discovery calls using MEDDIC; introduced a mutual action plan for deals above $30K, reducing slipped deals."
- "Created a competitor battlecard used in onboarding for all new AEs."

Then add a compact **Tools** section near the bottom. Keep it to relevant tools, grouped logically:

- **CRM:** Salesforce, HubSpot, Pipedrive
- **Prospecting:** LinkedIn Sales Navigator, Apollo, Lusha
- **Engagement & calls:** Outreach, Salesloft, Gong
- **Other:** Notion, Excel/Google Sheets, ChatGPT for call prep and research

If you use AI tools in a structured way, like preparing account research, summarising calls, or drafting follow-ups, say so. More and more teams expect it, and it signals you can work efficiently. Be specific, though. "Used AI to research accounts before calls, cutting prep time per call to about 10 minutes" is far better than "AI-savvy."

## Step 5: Tailor the CV for each application

You don't need to rewrite your CV for every job, but you should spend 15–20 minutes adjusting it. Here's a quick process:

1. **Read the job description and highlight the 5–6 most important requirements** (deal size, industry, motion, tools, languages).
2. **Mirror their language** where it honestly matches your experience. If they say "new logo acquisition," don't only write "hunting."
3. **Reorder your bullets** so the most relevant achievements appear first under each role.
4. **Adjust your headline and summary** to match the role level and focus.
5. **Remove anything irrelevant** to free up space.

Many companies use applicant tracking systems that filter on keywords, so mirroring the job description also helps you get past the first automated screen. Just never add keywords for skills you don't have. You'll be asked about them.

## How should a sales manager CV be different?

If you're applying for a leadership role, your CV needs to shift from "what I closed" to "what my team achieved because of me." Strong manager bullets look like this:

- "Led a team of 8 AEs to 104% of a $6M team target in 2023, up from 81% the year before I joined."
- "Hired 11 reps in 18 months; 8 reached full productivity within their first 5 months."
- "Rebuilt the pipeline stages and forecasting process, bringing quarterly forecast accuracy within a 10% range."
- "Promoted 3 reps to senior AE and 1 to team lead."

Hiring, ramping, coaching, forecasting, and developing people are the core of the job, so your CV should show evidence of each. Keep a short section with your own best individual-contributor results too, especially if you're a first-time manager. ## What mistakes get a sales CV rejected?

These are the issues I see most often, roughly in order of how much damage they do:

- **No numbers at all.** For a sales role, this is close to an automatic no.
- **A list of duties instead of results.** "Handled inbound leads, conducted demos, sent proposals" describes the job, not your performance.
- **Unexplained short stints.** Three jobs in two years raises questions. Add a brief reason ("company acquired," "sales team restructured") if it's honest and short.
- **Buzzword soup.** "Dynamic, self-motivated, passionate team player" takes space and proves nothing.
- **Inconsistent numbers.** If your CV says 130% and your LinkedIn says 115% for the same year, expect that to come up.
- **Typos and formatting errors.** You're applying to a job where you'll write emails and proposals to clients. Attention to detail matters.
- **Overdesigned templates.** Columns, graphics, and skill bars often break applicant tracking systems and look odd to sales leaders. A clean, single-column layout works best.

## Does your LinkedIn profile need to match your CV?

Yes. Almost every sales hiring manager will check your LinkedIn profile, often before they even finish your CV. Your job titles, dates, and headline numbers should match. Your LinkedIn can be slightly more conversational and include more detail, recommendations, and posts, but contradictions are a red flag.

For sales roles, LinkedIn is also a live demonstration of your skills. A clear headline, a short "About" section built around results, and some genuine activity (sharing what you've learned from deals, commenting thoughtfully on industry posts) show that you know how to build presence with buyers. ## Writing a sales CV for the Turkish market

If you're applying for sales roles in Turkey, a few practical points apply:

- **Prepare both English and Turkish versions.** International companies and many tech firms will want English. Local companies may prefer Turkish. Make sure the numbers and dates match exactly.
- **Photos are still common** on Turkish CVs. If you include one, use a simple, professional headshot. For international roles, a photo is usually optional.
- **List your languages clearly with real levels.** Selling in English, Arabic, German, or Russian is a genuine advantage for companies exporting from Turkey, so state it plainly ("English – professional working proficiency, ran full sales cycles in English").
- **State the currency for your numbers.** Given exchange rate movements, many candidates quote results in USD or EUR so they stay comparable over time. Whatever you choose, be consistent.

## FAQ

### Should I include my quota attainment if I missed target?

Yes, in most cases, especially if you include several periods so the overall trend is visible. A single year at 90% alongside years at 110% and 120% looks honest and credible. If there was a clear external reason, such as a territory change, add a short note of context instead of hiding the number.

### What if I'm moving into sales from another career?

Focus on transferable, measurable results: customers handled, targets met, revenue influenced, persuasion-heavy tasks like fundraising or recruitment. Add a short summary explaining why you're moving into sales, and show initiative with anything concrete, such as a sales course, a side project, or outreach you've done. Hiring managers for junior roles care most about drive, coachability, and evidence that you can handle rejection.

### Do I need a cover letter for sales jobs?

Often it isn't required, but a short, targeted note can help, especially for smaller companies where the founder or sales leader reads applications directly. Treat it like a cold email: three to five sentences, specific to the company, and ending with a clear reason to speak with you. A generic cover letter adds nothing and is better left out.

### How far back should my sales CV go?

Typically 10–15 years is enough, with the most detail on your last two or three roles. Older positions can be condensed into a single line each, or grouped under "Earlier experience." The reader cares most about what you've achieved recently and how it relates to the role in front of them.

## Conclusion

A sales CV that gets interviews works like a good sales pitch. It's built for the buyer, which here means the hiring manager. It leads with proof, gives enough context to build trust, and makes the next step easy. Replace duties with quantified results, add context to every number, show the process behind your performance, and tailor the document for each role. Keep it clean, honest, and consistent with your LinkedIn profile.

Do that, and you'll stand out in a stack of CVs that all claim to be "results-driven."
