The best way to onboard a new sales rep in their first 30 days is to follow a structured, week-by-week plan. It should move them from **learning** (product, customer, process) to **observing** (shadowing calls and reviewing recordings) to **supervised selling** (role-plays and calls with a manager present) and finally to **owning a small pipeline** by the end of week four. A good 30-day onboarding plan sets clear daily activities, defines what "ready" looks like before the rep starts, and measures progress with leading indicators like certification scores and conversations held rather than closed revenue.

I've watched founders hire a capable rep, hand them a laptop and a CRM login, and expect pipeline within two weeks. It almost never works. The rep isn't the problem. The first month simply wasn't designed. This article walks through the exact plan I recommend for small B2B teams, including what to prepare before day one, what each week should look like, and the specific milestones a rep should hit by day 30.

## Why do the first 30 days matter so much?

The first month shapes everything that follows. A new rep forms habits about how they research accounts, how they use the CRM, how they ask for help, and how they handle rejection. If those habits are built on guesswork, you spend the next six months correcting them.

There's also a confidence problem. New reps who make their first calls without enough preparation tend to get burned early. A few rough conversations with senior buyers, and suddenly they're avoiding the phone and "researching" instead. A structured onboarding plan protects their confidence by sequencing difficulty: easy wins first, harder conversations later.

Finally, the first 30 days are your best diagnostic window. You'll learn more about whether you made the right hire in this month than in any interview. That only works if you know what "good" looks like at day 10, day 20 and day 30.

Keep expectations realistic. Thirty days of onboarding doesn't mean a fully ramped rep. Full productivity usually takes several months, depending on deal size and sales cycle. I've broken that down in detail in [how long a new sales rep takes to ramp up](/blog/how-long-does-a-new-sales-rep-take-to-ramp-up). The goal of the first month is narrower: **a rep who understands the customer, can run your process, and has started generating real conversations.**

## What should you prepare before the rep's first day?

Most onboarding failures start before the rep arrives. Use the two weeks between the signed offer and the start date to get these ready:

1. **Tools and access.** CRM account, email, calendar, call recording tool, LinkedIn Sales Navigator (if you use it), shared drives, and Slack or Teams. Test every login. Losing the first two days to IT issues sends the wrong message.
2. **A written onboarding schedule.** A simple document listing what happens each day of week one and each week after that. The rep should receive it before day one.
3. **Core sales materials.** Your ideal customer profile, buyer personas, pitch deck, pricing, common objections, and pipeline stage definitions. If these live only in the founder's head, write them down now. A [sales playbook for your team](/blog/how-to-write-a-sales-playbook) is the single most valuable onboarding asset you can create.
4. **A call library.** Collect 15–25 recorded calls: great discovery calls, a lost deal, a tough objection handled well, and a demo that converted. Label each one with what the rep should learn from it.
5. **A starter account list.** Prepare 50–100 accounts the rep will work in week four. They should be good-fit but not your most strategic accounts. Nobody should practice on your dream customer.
6. **A buddy.** Pair the new rep with an existing rep (or the founder, if there's no team yet) for daily questions that don't need a manager.

If you're hiring your very first rep, much of this won't exist yet. That's normal. It's also a good reason to read [when a founder should hire their first sales rep](/blog/when-should-founder-hire-first-sales-rep), because the timing affects how much documentation you need in place.

## Week 1: How do you build context without overwhelming them?

Week one is about understanding, not activity. Resist the urge to put the rep on the phone. Instead, give them the context that makes every future conversation better.

### Days 1–2: Company, customer and market

- Founder or sales leader session (60–90 minutes): why the company exists, who buys, why they buy, and why they don't.
- Walk through the ideal customer profile and two or three buyer personas in depth: their job titles, daily pressures, what they're measured on, and the words they use.
- Review three to five customer stories. Cover what the customer's situation was before, what made them buy, and what changed after.

### Days 3–4: Product and competition

- Product demo from the person who knows it best, followed by the rep clicking through it themselves.
- A competitor overview: who you lose to, who you win against, and why. The rep should be able to explain in two sentences how you differ from your top three competitors.
- Pricing and packaging, including the discounting rules and who approves exceptions.

### Day 5: Process and tools

- Pipeline stages and exit criteria for each one. If your stages are vague, fix them using a framework like the one in [B2B sales pipeline stages](/blog/b2b-sales-pipeline-stages).
- CRM walkthrough: how to log activities, create opportunities, and update next steps.
- **End-of-week check:** a short written quiz or verbal walkthrough. Can the rep explain who your customer is, what problem you solve, and how you differ from competitors?

**Daily rhythm for week one:** a 15-minute morning check-in on today's plan and a 15-minute end-of-day debrief on what was confusing.

## Week 2: What should a new rep learn from shadowing?

Week two shifts from reading to watching. The rep should observe how real sales conversations unfold, ideally live and through recordings.

### Targets for week two

- **Listen to 15–20 recorded calls** from your library, with written notes on each: what the buyer cared about, which questions uncovered pain, how objections were handled.
- **Shadow 5–8 live calls** across different stages: cold calls, discovery, demos, negotiation.
- **Write the pitch in their own words.** A rep who memorizes your script sounds robotic. A rep who rewrites it in their own voice actually understands it.
- **Research 20 accounts** from their starter list: company size, likely pain, relevant trigger events, and the right contact to approach first.

### Make shadowing active, not passive

Passive shadowing is just sitting quietly with the camera off. It teaches very little. Ask the rep to prepare before each call: "What do you expect the buyer's main concern to be?" After the call, compare their prediction to what happened. That short loop builds judgment faster than any training deck.

## Week 3: How do you move from practice to supervised selling?

Week three is where the rep starts doing the work, with a safety net.

### Role-plays first

Run at least **8–10 role-plays** across the week, covering:

1. A cold call opener and the first 30 seconds
2. A discovery call with a skeptical buyer
3. The three most common objections (often price, timing, and "we already use something")
4. Booking a next step at the end of every conversation

Record the role-plays and review them together. Focus feedback on one or two things per session. Ten corrections at once fixes nothing.

### Certification

By the end of week three, the rep should pass a **pitch certification**: a full mock discovery call or demo with the founder or sales manager playing the buyer. Define the pass criteria in advance, for example:

- Asks at least five open discovery questions before pitching
- Ties the product to the buyer's stated problem
- Handles one objection without becoming defensive
- Ends with a specific, scheduled next step

If they don't pass, they don't fail onboarding. They repeat the certification two or three days later. The point is a clear bar, not a test to punish.

### First live calls with backup

Once certified, the rep should run **3–5 real calls** with the manager or buddy on the line. The senior person stays silent unless the call is going badly wrong. The debrief afterward matters more than the call itself.

## Week 4: How much pipeline should a new rep own by day 30?

In week four, the rep takes ownership of their starter account list and begins generating activity on their own.

### Example week-four targets for an outbound B2B rep

These are illustrative targets I use as a starting point. Adjust them for your deal size and sales cycle.

- **Prospect list built and enriched:** 100 contacts across 50 accounts. A B2B data tool like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) speeds this up considerably.
- **Outbound sequences launched:** personalized first touches to all 100 contacts across email, phone and LinkedIn
- **Calls made:** 30–50 dials per day once sequences are live
- **Conversations held:** 10–15 real conversations during the week
- **Meetings booked:** 2–4 qualified first meetings on the calendar for weeks five and six

Notice what's missing: **closed revenue.** In most B2B sales cycles, a deal sourced in week four won't close for weeks or months. Judging a new rep on revenue in month one is unfair and gives you no information.

If you don't have the capacity to build lists and run early outbound while a new rep ramps, an outside team can keep the top of the funnel moving. [SAAS Corner's outbound lead generation for B2B SaaS](https://saascorner.co) is one way founders bridge that gap.

### The day-30 review

Close the month with a structured 60-minute review covering:

1. **Knowledge:** Can they explain the customer, product and competition clearly?
2. **Skills:** How did certification and live calls go? Where are the two biggest gaps?
3. **Activity:** Did they hit the week-four targets? If not, why?
4. **Behaviors:** Do they ask for help, take feedback well, and keep the CRM clean?
5. **Next 60 days:** Agree on targets and a development focus for months two and three.

## How should a manager measure onboarding progress?

Use **leading indicators**, because results lag too far behind to be useful in month one. A simple scorecard works well:

| Week | Key milestone | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Understands ICP, product, competition | Passes knowledge check |
| 2 | Understands how good calls sound | 15–20 calls reviewed with notes |
| 3 | Can run a discovery call | Passes pitch certification |
| 4 | Generates real conversations | 10+ conversations, 2+ meetings booked |

Review this scorecard in your weekly 1:1. If you're unsure how to structure those meetings so they're useful rather than status updates, see [how to run sales 1:1 meetings that actually work](/blog/how-to-run-sales-1-1-meetings-that-work).

## What are the most common onboarding mistakes?

- **Throwing reps on the phone on day two.** Speed feels productive, but unprepared reps burn good leads and lose confidence.
- **Information dumps.** Forty slides in one afternoon means nothing sticks. Spread content across the week and test understanding.
- **No written plan.** If the plan lives in the manager's head, it changes daily and the rep never knows what "good" looks like.
- **Giving them the worst leads.** Old, recycled lists teach a new rep that outbound doesn't work. Give them fresh, good-fit accounts.
- **Founder disappears after week one.** In a small company, the founder's presence in the first month signals that sales matters.
- **Measuring revenue too early.** This creates panic, discounting, and pushed-forward deals that aren't real.

## Does onboarding look different for teams selling in Turkey?

The framework is the same, but a few local details matter. B2B sales in Turkey is often more relationship-driven, and buyers expect more personal follow-up, frequently through phone and WhatsApp alongside email. Include examples of these channels in shadowing and role-plays.

If your rep will sell both domestically and internationally, have them certify the pitch in both Turkish and English. Translating a pitch in real time rarely works well. Also, plan around public holidays like Ramazan Bayramı and Kurban Bayramı. If week four falls on a holiday period, decision-makers will be hard to reach, so shift the outbound push accordingly rather than judging the rep on a quiet week.

## FAQ

### How long should sales onboarding last?

Structured onboarding typically runs 30 days, but full ramp-up to productivity usually takes several months depending on deal size and sales cycle length. The first 30 days should focus on knowledge, skills and generating early pipeline, not closed revenue. After that, onboarding transitions into ongoing coaching.

### Should a new sales rep have a quota in the first month?

Most new reps shouldn't carry a revenue quota in month one, because deals they source won't close yet. Instead, give them activity and pipeline targets such as conversations held and meetings booked. Introduce a ramped quota in months two or three that increases gradually toward full target.

### Who should be responsible for onboarding a new rep?

The direct manager owns the plan, but in small companies the founder should be heavily involved in the first week, especially in sharing customer context. A peer "buddy" handles day-to-day questions. If there's no sales team yet, the founder effectively plays both roles.

### What if a new rep is struggling after 30 days?

Diagnose whether the gap is knowledge, skill or effort, because each needs a different fix. Knowledge gaps need more training, skill gaps need focused coaching and role-play, and effort gaps need a direct conversation about expectations. Agree on specific 30-day goals for month two and review progress weekly.

## Conclusion

Onboarding a new sales rep well isn't complicated, but it does have to be deliberate. Prepare tools, materials and a call library before day one. Spend week one on context, week two on observation, week three on supervised practice, and week four on real pipeline. Measure leading indicators, not revenue, and close the month with an honest review that sets up the next 60 days.

Do this well and you'll know by day 30 whether you've hired the right person. More importantly, you'll give them the best possible chance to succeed.
