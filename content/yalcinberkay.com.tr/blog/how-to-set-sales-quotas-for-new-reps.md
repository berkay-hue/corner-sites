The best way to set a sales quota for a new rep is to start from the full quota a fully productive rep can realistically hit, then use a **ramp schedule** that phases in that number over the length of your sales cycle, usually three to six months. In the first weeks, measure leading indicators like activities, meetings and pipeline created instead of revenue. As the rep's own pipeline matures, shift gradually to revenue targets.

That's the short answer. Getting it right in practice takes more care. Set the bar too high and you burn out a good hire before their first deal closes. Set it too low and you build a culture where "ramping" becomes a permanent excuse. I've seen both mistakes while building outbound teams across our companies at Corner Group. This guide walks through the method I use, with worked examples you can adapt to your own team.

## Why New Reps Need a Different Quota

A new rep and a tenured rep can sit at the same desk, use the same CRM and sell the same product. They are still not doing the same job yet.

A tenured rep has:

- A pipeline built up over months, with deals at every stage
- Product knowledge deep enough to handle objections without checking with anyone
- Relationships with internal teams (pre-sales, legal, onboarding) that speed up deals
- Pattern recognition about which prospects are real and which are tire-kickers

A new rep has none of these on day one. Their pipeline is empty, and in B2B nothing they start in week one will close in week one. If your average sales cycle is 90 days, a rep who starts prospecting on their first Monday will, on average, close their first deals around month three or four. Giving them a full quota in month one doesn't motivate them. It sets them up to fail on paper while they may be doing everything right.

This is also why the decision to hire matters so much. If you're still unsure whether your company is ready for that first salesperson, read [when a founder should hire their first sales rep](/blog/when-should-founder-hire-first-sales-rep) before you design any quota.

## Step 1: Define the Fully Ramped Quota First

You can't build a ramp toward a number you haven't defined. Start with what a fully productive rep should deliver once they're up to speed.

There are three common ways to arrive at this number. Ideally, use all three and compare.

### Bottom-up: capacity math

Work out what one rep can realistically produce with their available time.

1. Estimate how many qualified opportunities a rep can work per month (for example, 10).
2. Apply your historical win rate (for example, 25%).
3. Multiply by your average deal size (for example, $10,000).

10 opportunities × 25% × $10,000 = **$25,000 per month**, or $75,000 per quarter.

### Top-down: company targets

Take your revenue target for the year and subtract what will come from existing customers, renewals, partners and founder-led deals. Divide the rest across your planned sales headcount. This number is often more ambitious than the bottom-up figure. The gap between the two is a useful conversation with your leadership team.

### Compensation-based: the OTE multiple

A common rule of thumb in B2B sales is that a rep's annual quota should be several times their on-target earnings (OTE), often somewhere in the range of four to six times, depending on margins and deal complexity. If an account executive's OTE is $60,000, a ramped annual quota between $240,000 and $360,000 is a reasonable starting point to sanity-check.

**If your three methods give wildly different numbers, trust the bottom-up math.** It's the only one grounded in what a human can actually do in a week.

## Step 2: How Long Should the Ramp Period Be?

The ramp period should be roughly equal to **your average sales cycle plus the time it takes to learn the product and market**. A simple formula:

**Ramp length = onboarding time + average sales cycle length**

Some examples:

- **Transactional SaaS:** 2 weeks of onboarding + 30-day sales cycle ≈ 2 to 3 months ramp
- **Mid-market B2B software:** 4 weeks of onboarding + 90-day sales cycle ≈ 4 months ramp
- **Enterprise or complex services:** 6 weeks of onboarding + 6-month sales cycle ≈ 6 to 8 months ramp

Don't copy a ramp period from another company because it sounds standard. If your deals take five months to close, a three-month ramp means you're quietly asking new reps to hit numbers that are mathematically out of reach.

## Step 3: Build the Ramp Schedule

Once you know the ramped quota and the ramp length, build a month-by-month schedule. Most ramps follow a pattern like this:

| Month | % of full quota | Primary focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0% | Onboarding, activity and learning goals |
| 2 | 25% | Pipeline creation |
| 3 | 50% | First closes |
| 4 | 75% | Consistency |
| 5+ | 100% | Fully ramped |

For a slower cycle, stretch it out:

| Month | % of full quota |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | 0% (activity-based) |
| 3 | 20% |
| 4 | 40% |
| 5 | 60% |
| 6 | 80% |
| 7+ | 100% |

The exact percentages matter less than the logic: **revenue expectations should rise only as fast as the rep's pipeline can mature.**

## What Should a New Rep Be Measured on in the First Month?

In month one, a revenue quota measures almost nothing about the rep. It mostly reflects deals they inherited or luck. Use leading indicators that predict future revenue:

- **Product certification:** passing a demo or pitch certification with their manager
- **Activity volume:** for example, 40 to 60 outbound touches per day across email, phone and LinkedIn
- **Conversations:** a target number of real conversations with decision-makers per week
- **Meetings booked:** for example, 8 qualified first meetings by the end of month one
- **Pipeline created:** for example, $40,000 in qualified pipeline by day 45

For outbound roles, this phase depends heavily on the quality of the lists reps work from. A new rep calling a bad list will miss activity targets for reasons that have nothing to do with their ability. Make sure reps start with clean, well-segmented prospect data from a tool like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) or your own enrichment process, and give them a defined ideal customer profile to work against.

## Worked Example 1: SaaS Account Executive

Let's put it together for a hypothetical mid-market SaaS company.

**Inputs:**
- Average contract value: $12,000 per year
- Average sales cycle: 75 days
- Historical win rate on qualified opportunities: 25%
- AE OTE: $70,000 (50/50 base and variable)

**Ramped quota:** Bottom-up, a ramped AE can work about 14 qualified opportunities per month. 14 × 25% × $12,000 = $42,000 per month, or roughly $126,000 per quarter and around $500,000 per year. That's about 7x OTE, which is on the high side, so the team sets it at **$105,000 per quarter ($35,000 per month)**, closer to 6x OTE.

**Ramp schedule:**

- **Month 1:** No revenue quota. Goals: pass demo certification, book 10 first meetings, create $60,000 in qualified pipeline.
- **Month 2:** $8,750 (25%), plus a target of $80,000 in new pipeline.
- **Month 3:** $17,500 (50%).
- **Month 4:** $26,250 (75%).
- **Month 5 onward:** $35,000 (100%).

**Pipeline check:** To close $35,000 at a 25% win rate, the rep needs about $140,000 in qualified pipeline maturing each month. If by month three they're sitting at $50,000, you know there's a problem two months before it shows up in revenue. That's exactly the kind of signal a good [weekly pipeline review](/blog/weekly-pipeline-review-guide) should surface early.

## Worked Example 2: Sales Development Rep (SDR)

SDRs don't carry revenue quotas in most setups. They carry meeting or opportunity quotas.

**Inputs:**
- Fully ramped SDR target: 15 qualified meetings held per month
- "Qualified" means the prospect fits the ICP, has a relevant problem and the meeting actually happens (not just booked)

**Ramp schedule:**

- **Weeks 1–2:** Training, messaging practice, shadowing calls. Goal: 30 daily activities by end of week 2.
- **Month 1:** 5 qualified meetings held
- **Month 2:** 10 qualified meetings held
- **Month 3 onward:** 15 qualified meetings held

**Add a quality gate.** Count only meetings the AE accepts as qualified. Without this, new SDRs learn to book anything with a pulse, and your AEs stop trusting the pipeline.

SDR quotas also feed directly into career development. A rep who consistently hits and exceeds this target is building the track record needed to [move from SDR to account executive](/blog/move-from-sdr-to-account-executive), and you should tell them so from day one.

## Worked Example 3: Complex B2B Services in Turkey

Selling services to Turkish companies has its own rhythm. In my experience, decisions often involve more stakeholders, relationships carry significant weight, and an in-person meeting or a coffee can move a deal further than three follow-up emails. Cycles tend to be longer than in transactional markets.

**Inputs:**
- Average deal: a 12-month service retainer
- Sales cycle: around 4 to 5 months
- Win rate: roughly 1 in 4 qualified proposals

**Ramp approach:**

- **Months 1–2:** Activity and relationship goals only. For example, 20 discovery meetings, 6 proposals submitted.
- **Months 3–4:** Proposal value targets instead of closed revenue (for example, proposals worth 4x the eventual monthly quota).
- **Month 5:** 40% of revenue quota.
- **Month 6:** 70%.
- **Month 7 onward:** 100%.

One more consideration for Turkey: **currency.** If your pricing and quotas are set in Turkish lira while costs and prices move with inflation, a quota set in January may be meaningless by July. Either set quotas in a stable currency (EUR or USD) when your contracts are priced that way, or review lira-based quotas quarterly. I've written more on this in my post on [pricing B2B services for Turkish companies](/blog/how-to-price-b2b-services-turkish-companies).

## How Should You Pay New Reps During the Ramp?

A ramped quota with no ramped compensation still hurts. If a rep's variable pay depends on hitting numbers they can't hit yet, their take-home pay collapses in the months when they're working hardest. Common solutions:

- **Guaranteed commission (non-recoverable draw):** Pay a portion of variable compensation regardless of results for the first 2–3 months. This is the most rep-friendly option.
- **Recoverable draw:** Advance the variable pay, then recover it from future commissions. This is less popular and can feel punitive if the ramp is slow.
- **Activity-based bonuses:** Pay on meetings, pipeline created or certifications during the first month.

Whatever you choose, **put it in writing before the rep starts.** Ambiguity about pay is one of the fastest ways to lose a new hire.

## What Are the Most Common Quota Mistakes With New Reps?

These are the ones I see repeatedly:

1. **No ramp at all.** Full quota from day one, then surprise when the rep "underperforms."
2. **Ramp longer than necessary.** A six-month ramp for a 30-day sales cycle tells reps that slow is acceptable.
3. **Inheriting a territory with no pipeline handover.** If a rep takes over accounts, either credit the existing pipeline properly or don't. Don't do it halfway.
4. **Only measuring revenue.** By the time revenue reveals a problem, you've lost three months.
5. **Changing the quota mid-ramp without explanation.** Reps can accept a hard number. They can't accept a moving one.
6. **Ignoring the team context.** A rep without SDR support, marketing leads or decent tooling can't be held to the same number as one with all three.

That last point is often decisive. If your new AEs are expected to generate their own pipeline from scratch, either adjust their quota or invest in pipeline support. ## How to Know if the Quota Is Working

After two or three cohorts of new reps, look at the data:

- **What percentage of new reps hit their month-3 and month-5 targets?** If almost nobody does, your ramp is too aggressive. If everyone smashes it, it's too soft.
- **How long does it actually take reps to reach full productivity?** Compare the real time against the ramp you designed.
- **Which leading indicators best predicted success?** If reps who created $60,000 in pipeline by day 45 almost always ramped successfully, make that your official early benchmark.

A healthy target is that a majority of reps, though not all of them, hit their ramped numbers. A quota that everyone hits isn't a quota. It's a participation award.

## FAQ

### What is a ramped quota in sales?
A ramped quota is a reduced sales target given to a new rep during their first months, increasing step by step until it reaches the full quota. It reflects the fact that new reps need time to learn the product and build a pipeline before deals can close. Most ramps run three to six months, depending on the sales cycle.

### Should a new sales rep have a quota in their first month?
A new rep should have goals in their first month, but usually not a revenue quota. Activity, meeting and pipeline targets are better first-month measures because they predict future revenue and are within the rep's control. Revenue quotas typically start at 25% or less in month two or three.

### How do you calculate a full sales quota?
Multiply the number of qualified opportunities a rep can realistically handle per period by your win rate and average deal size. Then sanity-check the result against company revenue targets and against a multiple of the rep's on-target earnings, often four to six times. If the numbers disagree, the bottom-up capacity calculation is usually the most reliable.

### What if a new rep misses their ramped quota?
Look at the leading indicators first. If activity and pipeline are on track, the rep likely needs more time or coaching on closing. If activity and pipeline are also behind, address it immediately with a clear, documented improvement plan, and check whether the problem is the rep, the lead quality or the quota itself.

## Conclusion

Setting quotas for new reps comes down to honesty about time. Define a realistic full quota from capacity math, build a ramp that matches your actual sales cycle, measure leading indicators while revenue is still catching up, and protect the rep's earnings while they ramp. Then review the results after every cohort and adjust. The quota is a tool for building a productive team.

Done well, a ramp plan tells new hires exactly what success looks like in week one, month three and month six, and that clarity is often what separates reps who stay and grow from those who quietly leave.
