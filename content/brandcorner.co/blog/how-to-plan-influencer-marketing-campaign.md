Planning an influencer marketing campaign comes down to eight steps: set one measurable objective, define the audience you need to reach, set a budget, find and vet creators who already speak to that audience, write a clear brief, agree terms in a contract, launch with a coordinated content calendar, and measure results against the objective you started with. Campaigns that skip any of these steps tend to produce attractive content that nobody can connect to business results. Campaigns that follow them produce something you can evaluate, repeat, and scale.

This guide walks through each step in the order you should tackle it, with worked examples, sample numbers, and the questions brands and agencies most often get stuck on. Whether you're planning a first test with five micro-creators or a quarterly program with fifty, the structure is the same. Only the scale changes.

## Step 1: What exactly do you want this campaign to achieve?

Every planning decision depends on the objective: which creators you pick, which platforms, what you pay, and what you measure. The most common planning mistake is choosing more than one primary goal. "Awareness, engagement, and sales" is not a goal. It is a wish list.

Pick **one primary objective** and at most one secondary objective. Typical options look like this:

- **Awareness:** reach a new audience segment, measured by reach, impressions, and video views.
- **Consideration:** drive people to learn more, measured by link clicks, profile visits, saves, and site sessions.
- **Conversion:** drive purchases, sign-ups, or downloads, measured by discount code redemptions, tracked link conversions, and revenue.
- **Content production:** generate usable UGC for paid ads and owned channels, measured by number of approved assets and their performance in ads.

Then make the objective specific. "Increase awareness" becomes "Reach 400,000 women aged 25–40 in the UK who are interested in home fitness, within six weeks of launch." "Drive sales" becomes "Generate 600 first-time orders at a cost per acquisition below £25."

A clear target like this lets you work backwards. If you need 600 orders and your site converts influencer traffic at, say, 2%, you need roughly 30,000 clicks. If creators in your niche typically drive clicks at around 1% of reach, you need about 3 million impressions. Your own historical data should replace these example figures, but the logic of working backwards from the target holds either way.

## Step 2: Who is the audience, and where do they actually spend time?

Influencer marketing works by borrowing trust from a creator's audience, so you need to know precisely whose trust you're borrowing. Build a short audience profile covering:

- **Demographics:** age range, location, language, and income bracket if relevant.
- **Interests and problems:** what they care about that relates to your product.
- **Platforms and formats:** short-form video, long-form YouTube reviews, Instagram carousels, podcasts, LinkedIn posts.
- **Purchase triggers:** what makes them buy (social proof, demonstrations, price, expert endorsement).

Platform choice should follow the audience. A skincare brand aimed at under-25s will likely prioritize TikTok and Instagram Reels. A B2B software company aimed at operations managers may get far more from LinkedIn creators and niche newsletters. If your audience is professional and LinkedIn is the main channel, it's worth coordinating creator posts with your own company page activity. ## Step 3: How much should an influencer campaign cost?

There is no universal price list. Creator fees depend on audience size, engagement, niche, platform, deliverables, usage rights, and exclusivity. A practical way to budget is to divide spending into four buckets rather than starting from a single number.

1. **Creator fees** are usually the largest share. They cover content creation and posting.
2. **Usage rights and whitelisting** cover the right to reuse creator content in your ads or to run ads from the creator's handle. These are often priced separately, for example as a percentage uplift on the base fee for each month of usage.
3. **Product and logistics** cover samples, shipping, and any event or travel costs.
4. **Amplification and tools** cover paid boosting of top-performing posts, tracking tools, and management time.

As an example, a brand testing the waters with a £15,000 budget might allocate:

- £10,000 to creator fees (for instance, 10 micro-creators at around £600–£1,000 each)
- £2,000 to usage rights for the best-performing assets
- £1,000 to product and shipping
- £2,000 to paid amplification of the top two or three posts

Keep a **10–15% contingency** for reshoots, a replacement creator if someone drops out, or extra boosting when something performs unusually well.

Also decide early how you'll pay. Common models are flat fees, fee plus performance bonus, commission-only (affiliate), and gifting. Flat fees give you the most control over deliverables. Performance-based models align incentives but are less attractive to established creators. Gifting can work for very small creators, but it gives you little leverage over timing or content.

## Step 4: How do you find and vet the right creators?

Discovery and vetting is where campaigns are won or lost. A creator with the "right" follower count but the wrong audience is money spent reaching people who will never buy.

### Where to find creators

- **Your own followers and customers.** People who already use and mention your product are often the most credible partners.
- **Hashtag and keyword searches** on each platform for your category and your competitors' products.
- **Competitor partnerships.** See who is working with brands adjacent to yours, but avoid creators currently under exclusivity with a direct competitor.
- **Creator platforms and agencies** with searchable databases and audience data.

### A simple vetting checklist

For every shortlisted creator, review at least the last 15–20 posts and request audience insights screenshots. Check:

- **Audience fit:** Does the location, age, and gender split match your target? If 70% of a creator's audience is outside your shipping countries, most of your fee is wasted.
- **Engagement quality:** Look past the engagement rate. Are comments specific and conversational, or generic emoji strings? Sudden follower spikes with no viral post to explain them are a red flag.
- **Content quality and style:** Would this person's natural style suit your brand without being forced?
- **Sponsored content performance:** How do their paid posts perform compared with organic ones? A large drop suggests the audience tunes out ads.
- **Brand safety:** Scan past content and public statements for anything that conflicts with your values.
- **Disclosure habits:** Do they label sponsored posts correctly? Creators who don't are a regulatory risk.
- **Professionalism:** Response times, clarity in negotiation, and willingness to share data.

### Mix creator tiers deliberately

Rather than spending your whole budget on one large creator, many brands build a portfolio. A typical structure might be one or two mid-tier creators for reach and credibility, plus eight to fifteen micro-creators for niche trust, volume of content, and testing. This spreads risk: if one creator underperforms, the campaign doesn't collapse.

## Step 5: Write a creative brief that creators actually want to follow

A good brief gives creators enough direction to deliver on your objective and enough freedom to sound like themselves. Audiences notice immediately when a creator is reading a brand's script, and performance usually suffers.

Keep the brief to two or three pages and include:

- **Campaign objective** in one sentence.
- **Product background:** what it is, who it's for, and why it matters.
- **Key messages:** no more than three. For example: "Sets up in under five minutes," "Works without a subscription," and "Free returns for 30 days."
- **Mandatory elements:** tracking link, discount code, tags, hashtags, and required disclosure wording.
- **Do's and don'ts:** claims they cannot make (especially for health, finance, or regulated products), competitor mentions, and tone boundaries.
- **Deliverables:** the exact number and format of posts, such as "1 TikTok video of 30–60 seconds plus 3 Instagram Stories with link sticker."
- **Timeline:** draft submission date, feedback window, and posting date.
- **Approval process:** how many rounds of revisions are included (one or two is standard).
- **Creative inspiration:** a few example hooks or formats, framed as ideas rather than scripts.

One useful technique is to ask each creator for a short concept pitch of two or three sentences before they film. It takes them five minutes, saves both sides from expensive reshoots, and often surfaces better ideas than the brief itself.

## Step 6: Lock in terms with a proper contract

Even for small campaigns, put the agreement in writing. Verbal deals and loose DMs are the root cause of most campaign disputes, from missed posting dates to arguments over whether a brand can reuse a video in ads.

At minimum, the contract should cover deliverables, deadlines, payment terms, content approval, usage rights and their duration, exclusivity, disclosure requirements, performance data sharing, and what happens if either side cancels. For a detailed walkthrough of the terms that protect you, see our guide to [the influencer contract clauses every brand needs](/blog/influencer-contract-clauses-every-brand-needs).

Two clauses deserve special attention at the planning stage because they affect budget:

- **Usage rights:** If you plan to run creator content as paid ads, negotiate this now. Adding it later almost always costs more.
- **Exclusivity:** Asking a creator not to work with competitors for 60 or 90 days is reasonable, but expect to pay for it, especially in crowded categories.

## Step 7: Build the timeline and launch plan

A realistic timeline prevents the most common launch problem, which is creators posting late, out of sequence, or all on the same afternoon. Here is a sample six-to-eight-week plan for a mid-sized campaign:

1. **Weeks 1–2:** Set objectives, finalize budget, and complete creator discovery and vetting.
2. **Week 3:** Outreach, negotiation, and contracts signed.
3. **Week 4:** Product shipped and briefs sent. Creators submit concept pitches.
4. **Week 5:** Drafts submitted, feedback given, final approvals.
5. **Week 6:** Staggered posting window, live monitoring, and community management.
6. **Weeks 7–8:** Paid amplification of top performers, data collection, and reporting.

Allow at least **7–10 days for shipping** if creators need the product to film, and longer for international shipments or customs.

### Should creators post at the same time?

It depends on the objective. For a product launch where you want a moment of saturation, cluster posts within 48–72 hours so the audience sees the product from several trusted sources at once. For always-on sales campaigns, stagger posts across two to four weeks to keep traffic steady and give yourself time to learn which angles work before the rest of the content goes live.

### Prepare your owned channels

Before the first post goes live, make sure your landing page loads quickly on mobile, discount codes work, stock levels can handle a spike, and your social team is ready to reply to comments on creator posts. Directing creator traffic to a slow page or an out-of-stock product wastes the attention you've paid for.

## Step 8: How do you measure whether the campaign worked?

Measurement starts at planning, not after launch. Before any content is posted, set up the tracking you'll need:

- **Unique UTM-tagged links** for each creator, so you can attribute site traffic and conversions in your analytics.
- **Unique discount codes** per creator, which also capture conversions from people who don't click links, such as viewers who watch a video on one device and buy on another.
- **Baseline metrics** for branded search, direct traffic, and social follower growth, so you can spot lift during the campaign window.
- **A reporting template** that creators fill in with native insights (reach, impressions, saves, shares, link clicks) within 7 and 30 days of posting.

Then evaluate against the objective from Step 1. Using the earlier example: if the goal was 600 first-time orders at under £25 CPA and the campaign generated 520 orders at £22, you narrowly missed volume but hit efficiency. That points toward scaling up spending with the best-performing creators rather than rethinking the whole approach.

Look at results by creator, not just in aggregate. It's common for a minority of creators to drive most of the results, and that insight is the foundation of your next campaign. ## What should you do after the campaign ends?

The end of a campaign is the start of a better one. Within two weeks of the final post:

- **Hold a debrief** covering what worked, what didn't, and which creators were easiest and hardest to work with.
- **Rank creators** by performance against your primary metric and by working relationship.
- **Offer long-term deals** to your top performers. Ongoing partnerships tend to feel more authentic to audiences than one-off posts, and repeat mentions build familiarity.
- **Repurpose the best content** in paid ads, email, product pages, and organic social, within the usage rights you negotiated.
- **Document the playbook:** brief template, rate benchmarks, timelines, and tracking setup, so the next campaign starts from a stronger position.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to plan an influencer marketing campaign?

For a small to mid-sized campaign, allow six to eight weeks from setting objectives to final reporting. Discovery, vetting, and contracting usually take the first three weeks, and shipping products and approving content take another two. Rushing these stages is the most common cause of weak content and missed deadlines.

### How many influencers should a first campaign include?

Many brands start with five to fifteen creators, often weighted toward micro-creators. This gives enough variety to compare messages, formats, and audiences without stretching management capacity. Once you know which creators and angles perform, you can scale up with confidence.

### Should I give creators a script?

Generally, no. Give creators key messages, mandatory elements, and clear boundaries, then let them present the product in their own voice, because their audience trusts their natural style. Scripts tend to make sponsored content feel like an advert, which often reduces engagement and conversions.

### What's the most important metric to track?

The most important metric is the one tied to your primary objective. Track reach for awareness campaigns, clicks and site engagement for consideration, and conversions and cost per acquisition for sales campaigns. Engagement rate is useful context, but it should rarely be the headline result.

## Conclusion

A successful influencer campaign is rarely the result of finding one viral creator. It comes from disciplined planning: one clear objective, a well-defined audience, a realistic budget, carefully vetted creators, a brief that respects their voice, a solid contract, a coordinated launch, and measurement built in from day one. Follow these eight steps and each campaign becomes a source of data that makes the next one cheaper and more effective.
