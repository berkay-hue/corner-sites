Engagement rate benchmarks vary widely by platform. As rough working ranges: **Instagram** accounts often land around 1–3% engagement by followers, **TikTok** creators commonly see 3–9% or more when measured by views, **YouTube** videos often fall between 1% and 5% by views, and **Facebook** and **X** usually sit below 1%. The best benchmark for your brand is one built from your own niche, creator tier, and calculation method, because the same creator can show a "2%" or a "12%" engagement rate depending on the formula used.

That last point is why so many brands misread creator performance. A rate pulled from a media kit, another from an influencer platform, and a third from your own spreadsheet may all describe the same account but use different denominators. This guide explains how engagement rate is calculated on each platform, gives practical benchmark ranges to start from, shows how follower tier and niche shift those numbers, and walks through building an internal benchmark you can trust when vetting creators and reporting results.

## Why do engagement rate benchmarks differ so much?

Before comparing numbers across platforms, it helps to understand the four variables that move engagement rate the most.

1. **The formula.** Engagement divided by followers produces a very different number than engagement divided by reach, views, or impressions. Follower-based rates penalize platforms where content travels far beyond a creator's audience, such as TikTok.
2. **Follower tier.** Smaller accounts almost always post higher engagement rates than large ones. A nano creator with 5,000 followers might have a highly engaged community of friends and loyal fans. A celebrity account with 5 million followers has a much more passive audience.
3. **Niche.** Comedy, gaming, beauty, and parenting content tends to spark comments and shares. Finance, B2B software, and luxury travel may get fewer public interactions but more saves, clicks, and private shares.
4. **Content format.** Carousels, Reels, Stories, Shorts, long-form videos, and livestreams each generate different interaction patterns, even on the same platform.

Any benchmark that ignores these variables is closer to a guess than a standard. Use the ranges below as a starting point, then refine them with your own data.

## How do you calculate engagement rate?

There are four common formulas. Pick one per platform and use it consistently.

- **Engagement rate by followers (ERF):** (likes + comments + shares + saves) ÷ followers × 100. Easy to calculate from public data, but it ignores how many people actually saw the post.
- **Engagement rate by reach (ERR):** total engagements ÷ reach × 100. More accurate, but reach is only available from creator-provided screenshots or platform insights.
- **Engagement rate by views (ERV):** total engagements ÷ views × 100. The standard for TikTok, YouTube, Reels, and Shorts.
- **Engagement rate by impressions (ERI):** total engagements ÷ impressions × 100. Common on LinkedIn and X, where impressions are a native metric.

### A worked example

Imagine an Instagram creator with 40,000 followers. A Reel gets 1,800 likes, 90 comments, 60 shares, and 150 saves, for a total of 2,100 engagements. The Reel reached 35,000 accounts and was played 70,000 times.

- By followers: 2,100 ÷ 40,000 = **5.25%**
- By reach: 2,100 ÷ 35,000 = **6.0%**
- By views: 2,100 ÷ 70,000 = **3.0%**

Same post, three defensible numbers. If one shortlisted creator reports 5% by followers and another reports 3% by views, they may be performing identically. Always confirm the formula before comparing.

### Should you average across posts?

Yes. A single viral post distorts the picture. A reasonable standard is to calculate engagement across the creator's last 10–20 feed posts or videos, excluding the highest and lowest outliers. Some teams use the median rather than the mean so one breakout video doesn't inflate the result.

## Engagement rate benchmarks by platform

The table below summarizes general working ranges often seen across the industry for typical creator accounts. Treat them as directional, not absolute.

| Platform | Typical formula | Rough working range | What "strong" usually looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instagram | By followers | 1–3% | Above 3–4% for mid-size accounts |
| TikTok | By views | 3–9% | Consistently above 8–10% |
| YouTube | By views | 1–5% | Above 4–5%, plus healthy comment depth |
| LinkedIn | By impressions | 1–4% | Above 4–5% with substantive comments |
| Facebook | By followers or reach | Under 1% | Above 1–2% |
| X (Twitter) | By impressions | Under 1% | Above 1–2% |
| Pinterest | Saves and outbound clicks | Below 1% interaction | Strong save and click rates matter more |
| Twitch | Chat activity vs. average viewers | Varies widely | Active chat relative to concurrent viewers |

### Instagram

Instagram is the platform where follower-based engagement rate is most commonly quoted, and it is also where averages have trended down as the feed has become more algorithmic and video-focused. For most mid-tier creators, 1–3% by followers is normal. Nano and micro creators frequently land higher.

Pay attention to **saves and shares**, which Instagram now surfaces more prominently in creator insights. A creator with a modest like count but a high save rate on tutorials or recipes may be driving more purchase intent than a creator with a big like count on lifestyle selfies. For Stories, use reply rate, sticker taps, and link clicks relative to Story views rather than followers.

### TikTok

TikTok distributes content based on interest rather than follow graphs, so follower counts are a weak denominator. Measure engagement by views. A typical range is roughly 3–9%, with standout creators sustaining higher rates across many videos.

Because individual videos can spike unpredictably, look at the **consistency of view counts** as well as engagement. A creator whose videos regularly earn 30,000–60,000 views with 6% engagement is usually a safer bet than one with a single 2-million-view video and a string of 2,000-view posts.

### YouTube

YouTube engagement is usually lower on a percentage basis because long-form viewers watch more than they interact. A range of 1–5% by views is common for likes and comments combined. For Shorts, expect numbers closer to TikTok behavior.

On YouTube, engagement rate alone undersells performance. **Average view duration, comment quality, and the long tail of views** matter enormously. A sponsored integration in a video that keeps earning views for 18 months can outperform a viral short-form post that disappears in 72 hours.

### LinkedIn

LinkedIn creators, from founders to industry analysts, are increasingly part of B2B influencer programs. Engagement is typically measured by impressions, and rates in the low single digits are normal. The real signal is in the comments: are they from decision-makers in your target industry, or from engagement pods trading generic "Great post!" replies?

### Facebook and X

Both platforms generally show engagement rates below 1%. That doesn't make them worthless. Facebook groups and pages still drive meaningful traffic in certain demographics and niches, and X remains influential in tech, finance, sports, and news. Just don't compare their numbers directly to TikTok or Instagram. A 1.5% rate on X can be genuinely strong.

### Pinterest and Twitch

Pinterest behaves more like a visual search engine than a social network. Saves and outbound clicks are the metrics that matter, and traditional engagement rates look low. Twitch is a live medium: judge creators by average concurrent viewers, chat activity, and how many viewers stay through a sponsored segment.

## How does follower tier change the benchmark?

Across nearly every platform, engagement rate declines as audience size grows. A practical way to set expectations is to adjust your benchmark by tier:

- **Nano (1K–10K followers):** Highest engagement rates, often well above platform averages. Audiences are close-knit, but reach is limited.
- **Micro (10K–100K):** Still above average engagement with meaningfully more reach. Often the sweet spot for cost efficiency.
- **Mid-tier (100K–500K):** Engagement close to platform norms. Good balance of reach and credibility.
- **Macro (500K–1M):** Below-average rates are normal. You're buying scale and awareness.
- **Mega (1M+):** Lowest rates. A 1% rate on a 3-million-follower Instagram account still means roughly 30,000 interactions per post.

So a 2% Instagram rate is mediocre for a 7,000-follower nano creator but respectable for a 900,000-follower macro creator. Compare creators against peers in the same tier and niche, not against the whole platform.

## What engagement rate is "too good to be true"?

High engagement isn't automatically good. Some patterns suggest the numbers aren't organic:

- **Rates far above tier norms with flat follower growth**, such as a 200,000-follower account consistently posting 15% engagement by followers on static images.
- **Comments that are generic, repetitive, or emoji-only**, especially from accounts with no profile photos or posts.
- **Sudden spikes in followers or likes** that don't line up with a viral post or press moment.
- **Audience geography that doesn't match the creator's language or content**, for example, a German-language creator whose audience is mostly in countries where German isn't widely spoken.
- **Engagement that arrives within minutes and then stops**, a common sign of engagement pods.

## How to build your own internal engagement benchmark

Public ranges get you started, but your best benchmark comes from your own campaigns. Here's a step-by-step approach.

1. **Choose one formula per platform.** For example: Instagram feed by followers, Reels and TikTok by views, YouTube by views, LinkedIn by impressions. Document it so everyone on the team calculates the same way.
2. **Segment by tier and niche.** Create separate benchmark rows for, say, "micro beauty creators on TikTok" and "mid-tier fitness creators on Instagram."
3. **Collect organic baselines.** For each shortlisted creator, record the median engagement rate across their last 15 organic posts.
4. **Record sponsored performance separately.** Sponsored posts usually perform somewhat below a creator's organic average. Tracking the gap tells you how well the brief and product fit landed.
5. **Calculate a sponsored-to-organic ratio.** If a creator's organic median is 6% and your sponsored post hits 5.1%, that's an 85% ratio. Over time, you'll learn what ratio is typical for your brand and spot standout partners.
6. **Update quarterly.** Platform algorithms change frequently. Refreshing benchmarks every quarter keeps your targets realistic.

After two or three campaigns, you'll have a benchmark grounded in your audience, product category, and creative approach, which is far more useful than any industry average.

## Is engagement rate the right KPI for your campaign?

Engagement rate is a strong indicator of content resonance and audience attention, but it isn't a business outcome. A post with 8% engagement that drives no site traffic may be less valuable than one with 3% engagement that sends hundreds of qualified visitors to your product page.

Match the metric to the goal:

- **Awareness campaigns:** reach, views, engagement rate, and brand lift.
- **Consideration campaigns:** saves, shares, link clicks, profile visits, and comment sentiment.
- **Conversion campaigns:** tracked clicks, promo code redemptions, revenue, and cost per acquisition.

## FAQ

### What is a good engagement rate on Instagram?

For most mid-tier Instagram creators, around 1–3% by followers is typical, and anything consistently above 3–4% is generally strong. Nano and micro creators often exceed these figures, while macro and mega accounts usually fall below them. Always compare creators within the same follower tier and niche.

### Why are TikTok engagement rates higher than other platforms?

TikTok engagement is usually measured by views rather than followers, and its interest-based algorithm shows content to people already likely to enjoy it. That combination tends to produce higher percentages than follower-based rates on Instagram or Facebook. The two numbers aren't directly comparable unless you use the same formula.

### Should I use followers or reach to calculate engagement rate?

Reach-based engagement is more accurate because it only counts people who actually saw the content. Follower-based rates are easier to calculate from public data, which makes them useful for initial screening. A practical approach is to screen with follower-based rates, then request reach and view data from shortlisted creators before signing.

### How many posts should I analyze to benchmark a creator?

Analyzing the last 10–20 posts gives a reliable picture without going back so far that the data no longer reflects current performance. Use the median, or remove the top and bottom outliers, so one viral post doesn't skew the result. Separate sponsored and organic posts to see how branded content performs.

## Conclusion

Engagement rate benchmarks are useful only when you know what's behind the number. Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, LinkedIn, Facebook, and X each have different norms, and those norms shift further with creator tier, niche, and format. The brands that get the most from engagement data pick one formula per platform, compare creators against true peers, watch for numbers that look too good to be true, and build internal benchmarks from their own campaign results. Most importantly, they treat engagement as one signal among several, connected to reach, attribution, and ROI.
