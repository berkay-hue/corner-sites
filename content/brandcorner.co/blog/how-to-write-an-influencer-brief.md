An influencer brief creators will follow is **short, specific about the goal, and generous with creative freedom**. It gives one clear message, a small number of non-negotiable requirements, exact deliverables and deadlines, and then trusts the creator to translate all of that into content their audience will actually watch. The best briefs fit on one or two pages, separate "must do" from "nice to have," and explain *why* each requirement exists.

Most briefs fail for the opposite reasons. They run 12 pages, bury the key message under brand history, and script every second of a 30-second video. Creators either ignore the document or follow it so literally that the content reads like an ad and underperforms. This guide covers what to include, what to leave out, a worked example, and how to get creators to buy in before they hit record.

## Why do creators ignore most influencer briefs?

Creators rarely ignore a brief because they are careless. Usually the document makes it hard to know what matters. A few patterns cause most of the trouble:

- **Too many messages.** When a brief lists seven product benefits, the creator has to guess which one to lead with. Usually they guess differently than you would.
- **No hierarchy of requirements.** If the brand logo placement and the legal disclosure are formatted the same way, the creator can't tell which one is negotiable.
- **Scripts instead of direction.** Word-for-word scripts strip out the creator's voice, and their voice is the reason you hired them.
- **Missing logistics.** Vague deadlines, unclear approval steps, and no mention of usage rights lead to back-and-forth that drains goodwill.
- **No reasoning.** "Don't film in the bathroom" sounds arbitrary. "Don't film in the bathroom because our retail partner flagged it as off-brand for this line" gets compliance.

A good brief fixes each of these. The brief should be the output of that plan, not a substitute for it.

## What should an influencer brief include?

Here are the ten sections that belong in almost every brief, in the order a creator wants to read them.

### 1. The campaign in two sentences

Open with a short summary the creator can repeat back to you. For example: *"We're launching a fragrance-free moisturizer for people with sensitive skin. We want your audience to understand it's gentle enough for daily use, and we want them to try it with a 15% first-order discount."*

If you can't write this summary, the campaign isn't ready to brief.

### 2. The objective and the one metric that matters

Tell creators what success looks like. Awareness, consideration and conversion campaigns call for different content, and creators make better choices when they know which one you want:

- **Awareness:** reach, views, completion rate. This favors hooks and entertainment.
- **Consideration:** saves, shares, comments with questions. This favors demonstrations and education.
- **Conversion:** link clicks, code redemptions, sales. This favors clear calls to action and urgency.

Pick one primary metric. If you need to track sales, explain the mechanics early. Creators should know whether they're using a unique link, a promo code, or both. Our guide on [tracking influencer sales with UTMs and promo codes](/blog/track-influencer-sales-utm-promo-codes) covers the setup.

### 3. The audience and the insight

Describe who you're trying to reach and what they care about. Skip the demographic slide. Something like this works better: *"Your followers who've tried 'clean' products and been disappointed. They're skeptical of big claims and trust real-life testing over before/after photos."*

The insight is the most useful line in the brief because it tells the creator *how* to talk, not just *what* to say.

### 4. One key message and up to three proof points

Choose a single message. Then give a maximum of three proof points the creator can use to support it:

- **Key message:** Gentle enough for daily use on sensitive skin.
- **Proof point 1:** Fragrance-free and dermatologist-tested.
- **Proof point 2:** Absorbs in under a minute, no greasy finish.
- **Proof point 3:** 30-day return policy if it doesn't work for you.

Make clear that the creator should say the message in their own words. Only claims with legal or regulatory weight should be phrased exactly as written.

### 5. Mandatories vs. creative freedom

This is the section that decides whether creators follow your brief. Split requirements into two clearly labeled lists.

**Must include (non-negotiable):**

- Platform-appropriate paid partnership disclosure in the first frame or caption line
- Product visible on screen for at least 3 seconds
- Spoken or on-screen mention of the discount code
- Approved claims only (no "cures," "heals," or medical language)

**Suggestions (use if they fit your style):**

- Showing your actual morning routine
- Comparing texture to a product you've used before
- A reaction shot after the first application

Keep the must-include list to five items or fewer. Every extra mandatory makes the content feel more like an ad. If something isn't important enough to reject a draft over, it belongs in suggestions.

### 6. Deliverables with exact specs

Ambiguity here causes most revision rounds. Spell out every deliverable:

- **1 × TikTok video**, 30–60 seconds, 9:16, native audio or trending sound
- **3 × Instagram Stories**, including one with a link sticker
- **1 × Instagram Reel**, cross-posted from TikTok without the watermark
- **Raw footage** delivered via shared folder, if you plan to repurpose content

Note any caption requirements, hashtags, or handles to tag. If you plan to run the content as ads, say so here. Creators sometimes shoot differently when they know a piece will be used in paid media, for example by leaving space for text overlays or filming an alternative hook. Our guide to [influencer whitelisting and paid amplification](/blog/how-to-run-influencer-whitelisting) explains how to set that up.

### 7. Timeline with real dates

Use calendar dates, not "week two." A typical single-post timeline looks like this:

1. **Product ships:** March 3
2. **Concept or outline due:** March 10
3. **Draft content due:** March 17
4. **Brand feedback returned:** within 48 hours of receiving the draft
5. **Final content due:** March 21
6. **Go-live window:** March 24–26
7. **Performance screenshots due:** 7 days after posting

Committing to a brand-side turnaround time (48 hours is reasonable) signals respect and keeps the schedule from slipping on your end.

### 8. Disclosure and compliance

State the disclosure rules plainly. Ask creators to use the platform's built-in paid partnership tools and a clear label such as "#ad" or "paid partnership" where viewers will see it immediately, not hidden among a dozen hashtags. Include any category-specific restrictions. Health, finance, alcohol and children's products often have extra rules depending on the market.

### 9. Usage rights, exclusivity and payment

Creators want to know how their content will be used and when they'll be paid. Summarize:

- How long you can reuse the content (for example, 90 days organic, 30 days paid)
- Which channels you can use it on
- Any exclusivity window that bars them from working with competitors
- Payment amount, schedule and method

The brief should summarize these terms, and the contract should define them. For the clauses that protect both sides, see [influencer contract clauses every brand needs](/blog/influencer-contract-clauses-every-brand-needs).

### 10. Approval process and point of contact

Name one person who gives feedback, and say how many revision rounds are included. Two rounds is common. Tell creators what you'll review for: claim accuracy, mandatories and brand safety. Taste is not on that list. This reassures them you won't rewrite their content.

## How long should an influencer brief be?

Aim for **one to two pages**, or a short slide deck of five to seven slides. If you have extra material such as brand guidelines, product fact sheets or mood boards, attach it as an appendix and label it "reference only."

A practical test: a creator should be able to read the core brief in under five minutes and explain the campaign back to you in one sentence. If they can't, cut until they can.

Format matters too. Use headings, bullet points and bold labels. Plenty of creators read briefs on their phones between shoots, so dense paragraphs and complex tables work against you.

## What does a good influencer brief look like? A worked example

Here's a condensed brief for a hypothetical sensitive-skin moisturizer launch, written the way a creator would want to receive it.

**Campaign summary:** We're launching a fragrance-free daily moisturizer for sensitive skin. We want your audience to see it as a safe everyday option and try it with your code.

**Goal:** Conversions. Primary metric is code redemptions.

**Who we're talking to:** People who've been burned (sometimes literally) by "gentle" products. They trust honest testing over polished claims.

**Key message:** Gentle enough for every day, even on reactive skin.

**Proof points:** Fragrance-free and dermatologist-tested · absorbs fast, no greasy feel · 30-day returns.

**Must include:**
- Paid partnership label at the start
- Product clearly on screen for 3+ seconds
- Code SKIN15 spoken or shown on screen
- No medical claims

**Ideas, totally optional:** Your real morning routine · honest first impressions · how it sits under makeup or sunscreen.

**Deliverables:** 1 TikTok (30–60s) · 3 IG Stories with link sticker · raw footage.

**Dates:** Concept March 10 · Draft March 17 · Final March 21 · Live March 24–26.

**Usage:** 90 days organic reuse on brand channels; 30 days paid usage via whitelisting.

**Contact:** One campaign manager, feedback within 48 hours, up to 2 revision rounds.

That's the whole brief. A creator can absorb it quickly, knows exactly what will get a draft rejected, and still has room to make something that sounds like them.

## How do you get creators to actually follow the brief?

A well-written document is only half the job. How you deliver it decides whether creators engage with it.

### Hold a 15-minute kickoff call

For larger partnerships, a short call beats any document. Walk through the key message and mandatories, then ask the creator how *they* would approach it. You'll often hear a better idea than anything in your brief, and creators commit more to concepts they helped shape.

### Ask for a concept before a draft

Asking for a short outline first (a hook, the beats of the video, the call to action) catches misunderstandings before anyone films. Fixing a three-line outline is far cheaper than reshooting a finished video.

### Brief the right creators

A brief can't fix a poor fit. If a creator's audience, tone or values don't match the campaign, no amount of direction will make the content feel authentic. Careful selection upfront makes briefing much easier later.

### Give feedback that's specific and limited

When reviewing drafts, tie every note to the brief: "The code isn't mentioned. That's a must-include," or "This line reads as a medical claim. Could you rephrase as 'calms the look of redness'?" Avoid subjective notes like "make it more fun." Bundle all feedback into one message per round.

### Share results afterward

Send creators their performance data and what you learned. This builds trust, improves the next brief, and lays the groundwork for repeat partnerships.

## What mistakes should you avoid in an influencer brief?

- **Writing a script.** Offer talking points, not lines.
- **Sending the same brief to every creator unchanged.** Keep the core consistent, but adjust suggestions to each creator's format. A comedy creator and a tutorial creator need different prompts.
- **Hiding the call to action.** If you need a code mentioned, say so in the must-include list, not on page six.
- **Forgetting the "why."** One line of reasoning per mandatory greatly increases compliance.
- **Changing the brief mid-campaign.** If something must change, tell creators immediately, explain why, and adjust timelines or fees if the change creates real extra work.
- **Leaving out usage rights.** Surprising a creator later with a request to run their video as an ad damages the relationship and can create legal problems.

## FAQ

### How detailed should an influencer brief be?

Detailed enough on logistics, light on creative direction. Deliverables, dates, disclosure rules and usage rights should be exact. Tone, format and storytelling should mostly be left to the creator. A one- to two-page brief with a reference appendix is usually enough.

### Should I give influencers a script?

Generally, no. Scripts make content sound like advertising and remove the authenticity audiences follow creators for. The exception is legally required wording, such as specific product claims or regulated disclaimers, which should be provided word for word and clearly marked.

### How many revisions should a brief allow?

Two revision rounds is a common, fair standard for most sponsored posts. State this in the brief and the contract, and define what counts as a revision (fixing mandatories or claim accuracy) versus a new request, which may require extra payment.

### What's the difference between a creative brief and an influencer brief?

A creative brief guides an internal or agency team producing content in the brand's voice. An influencer brief guides an independent creator producing content in *their* voice for *their* audience. That means fewer rules, more flexibility, and clearer explanations of why each requirement exists.

## Conclusion

Creators follow briefs that respect their time and their craft. Lead with a two-sentence summary, commit to one message, keep mandatories to five or fewer, and spell out deliverables, dates and rights precisely. Then hold a short kickoff call, ask for a concept before filming, and give feedback tied to the brief rather than personal taste. The result is content that delivers your message and still sounds like the creator, which is why you hired them in the first place.
