The best way to pitch creators and get replies is to send a short, personalized message that proves you know their content, states a clear offer with real compensation, and makes saying yes easy. Creators ignore pitches that look mass-produced, hide the budget, or ask for a call before explaining anything. If your first message answers "why me, what's the deal, and what happens next" in under 150 words, your reply rate will rise noticeably.

Most brands get the strategy right and the outreach wrong. They spend weeks building a creator shortlist, then send a generic template from a no-reply-looking address and wonder why no one answers. This guide covers the full outreach process: preparation, the anatomy of a good pitch, ready-to-adapt templates, follow-up timing, DM etiquette, and how to measure and improve your results over time.

## Why do most creator pitches get ignored?

Creators with an engaged audience, even mid-sized ones in the 20,000 to 200,000 follower range, often receive several brand pitches a week. Many of these pitches are low-effort. Creators learn to filter fast, and they usually decide within a few seconds whether a message deserves a reply.

The most common reasons pitches get skipped:

- **Obvious copy-paste.** "Hey love your content!" with no specific reference signals a mass blast.
- **No mention of payment.** Vague phrases like "exciting collaboration opportunity" usually mean gifted-only, and creators know it.
- **Wrong fit.** A skincare pitch to a creator who never posts skincare shows that you didn't look.
- **Too long or too demanding.** A 500-word essay or a request to "jump on a quick call" before any details puts the work on the creator.
- **Suspicious sender.** Free email domains, missing brand names, or pushy urgency can look like scams, and creators are targeted by plenty of them.

Fix these five issues and you are already ahead of most of the brands in a creator's inbox.

## Do your homework before you write a single word

A good pitch starts long before the email. Ten minutes of research per creator is the difference between a reply and silence.

### Confirm the creator actually fits

Check that the creator's audience, tone, and values match your brand. Look at their last 15 to 20 posts, not just their top-performing ones. Do they already talk about your category? Do they do sponsored content, and does it perform close to their organic posts? If you need a framework for this step, our guide on [matching creators to your brand positioning](/blog/how-to-match-creators-to-brand-positioning) walks through it in detail.

Also verify the audience is real before you invest time in outreach. A quick check of engagement patterns, comment quality, and follower growth spikes will save you from pitching inflated accounts.

### Find the right contact channel

Use the channel the creator has signaled they prefer, in this order:

1. **Business email in their bio or link page.** This is the clearest invitation.
2. **Talent manager or agency contact.** Many established creators route all brand deals through representation. Pitching them directly can slow things down or annoy their team.
3. **Platform creator marketplaces**, if the creator is listed there.
4. **Direct messages**, as a last resort or as a nudge after email.

When a creator is represented by a talent agency or management firm, finding the right manager can be tricky. A B2B contact database like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) can help you locate the correct person at the agency instead of guessing a generic inbox.

### Collect three personalization hooks

For each creator, note three specific things you can reference:

- A recent post or video that genuinely relates to your product
- A recurring series, format, or inside joke their audience knows
- A value, cause, or opinion they've expressed that aligns with your brand

You will only use one or two in the pitch, but having three keeps it from sounding forced.

## What should a creator pitch include?

A pitch that gets replies usually has six parts. Keep the whole thing between 80 and 150 words.

1. **A specific subject line.** Include your brand name and the type of offer. Aim for under 50 characters so it doesn't get cut off on mobile. Example: "Paid TikTok partnership – Northwind Coffee"
2. **A personal opening line.** One sentence that proves you've watched their content. "Your 'cheap breakfast in 5 minutes' series is exactly how our customers use our cold brew" beats "We love your content."
3. **Who you are, in one line.** Brand name, what you sell, and why it fits their audience.
4. **The offer.** Deliverables, timing, and compensation. Even a range is better than nothing.
5. **Why them specifically.** One sentence connecting their audience to your goal.
6. **A low-effort next step.** A yes/no question or a request for their rate card, not "let me know your thoughts."

Sign off with your full name, role, and a real company email address. A professional signature with your website builds trust immediately.

### Should you include the budget in the first message?

Yes, in most cases. Stating a budget or a range ("We're budgeting $800–$1,200 for one TikTok and usage rights for 30 days") filters out mismatches early and signals that you are serious. If you prefer to let the creator quote first, say clearly that the campaign is paid and ask for their rates. What you should never do is let a creator spend time on a back-and-forth only to learn it's gifted-only. ## Creator pitch templates you can adapt

Use these as structures, not scripts. Swap in your own hooks every time.

### Template 1: Paid sponsored post

**Subject:** Paid Reel partnership – [Brand]

> Hi [Name],
>
> Your recent Reel on [specific topic] stopped me mid-scroll, especially [specific detail]. I lead creator partnerships at [Brand], where we make [product] for [audience].
>
> We're launching [product/campaign] in [month] and would love to pay you for one Instagram Reel plus three Stories. Our budget is [$X–$Y], and we're flexible on creative direction because your audience trusts your style.
>
> Would you be open to it? If so, I'll send a one-page brief and you can share your rate card.
>
> [Your name]
> [Role], [Brand] | [website]

### Template 2: UGC content (no posting required)

**Subject:** UGC project for [Brand] – paid, no posting

> Hi [Name],
>
> I came across your UGC portfolio and loved the [specific video] you made for a [category] brand, especially the hook in the first two seconds.
>
> We're looking for three 20–30 second videos showing [product] in a real morning routine. We pay [$X] per video, plus 60 days of paid usage rights. You won't need to post on your own channels.
>
> Interested? Happy to send the brief today.
>
> [Your name]

### Template 3: Long-term ambassador invitation

**Subject:** 6-month ambassador role at [Brand]?

> Hi [Name],
>
> You've mentioned [product category] in at least four videos this year, and your take on [specific opinion] matches how we think about it at [Brand].
>
> We're building a small ambassador group: monthly retainer of [$X], two pieces of content per month, early access to new products, and a personal affiliate code with [Y]% commission.
>
> Could we send over the details?
>
> [Your name]

Notice what all three share: a real reference, a clear deliverable, actual numbers, and a one-step reply.

## How long should you wait before following up?

Most replies come after a follow-up, not the first email. Creators are busy, inboxes are messy, and a polite nudge is expected. A simple cadence works well:

- **Day 0:** Initial pitch
- **Day 3 or 4:** Short follow-up in the same thread, two or three sentences, adding one new detail (a product link, a launch date, or an example of past creator content)
- **Day 8 to 10:** Second follow-up, optionally paired with a short DM: "Sent you an email about a paid collab with [Brand], wanted to make sure it didn't get buried."
- **Day 14 to 18:** Final, friendly close-out: "No worries if the timing isn't right. I'll keep you in mind for future campaigns."

Stop there. More than three follow-ups starts to feel like pressure, and creators talk to each other about brands that are hard to deal with. The close-out message also has a surprising effect: it often prompts a reply from creators who meant to answer and forgot.

## How do you pitch creators through DMs?

DMs are useful for smaller creators without a business email, or as a supporting touch after email. A few rules:

- **Keep it under 60 words.** DMs are read on phones, often in between other messages.
- **Lead with the brand and that it's paid.** "Hi! I'm [Name] from [Brand]. We'd love to pay you for a TikTok featuring [product]." This immediately separates you from scam accounts.
- **Use the official brand account** with a verified-looking profile, a real posting history, and a clear bio. Pitching from a personal account with no posts raises red flags.
- **Ask for the best email** to send details, rather than negotiating a full deal in DMs.
- **Never send links in the first DM.** Unknown links are a classic phishing pattern and many creators won't click them.

## What should you do when a creator replies?

Speed matters. Reply within 24 hours on business days, because momentum fades fast and creators often juggle several offers at once.

When they say yes or ask for details:

1. **Send a concise brief**, not a 15-page deck. Cover the goal, key messages, dos and don'ts, deliverables, timeline, and approval process. Our guide on [writing an influencer brief creators will follow](/blog/how-to-write-an-influencer-brief) shows what to include and what to leave out.
2. **Confirm compensation, usage rights, and exclusivity in writing** before any content is produced.
3. **Agree on one point of contact** on each side to avoid crossed wires.
4. **Ship product early**, ideally at least 10 to 14 days before the content deadline, so the creator has time to genuinely use it.

When they say no, thank them and ask if you can reach out for future campaigns. A respectful "no" today is often a "yes" in three months when budgets, timing, or product fit change.

When they counter with a higher rate, don't take it personally. Ask what flexibility exists, for example fewer deliverables, shorter usage rights, or a mix of flat fee and affiliate commission. Negotiation done respectfully is often the start of a strong relationship, and that relationship is where the real value lives. ## How do you measure and improve your outreach?

Treat creator outreach like any other outbound channel: track it, test it, and refine it. Brands that run disciplined outbound sales, like the data-driven approach practiced at [Sales Corner](https://salescorner.co), know that small improvements in each step compound over time. The same logic applies here.

Track these numbers in a simple spreadsheet or your influencer platform:

- **Open rate**, if your email tool tracks it, to test subject lines
- **Reply rate**, the most important early indicator
- **Positive reply rate**, meaning replies that move toward a deal
- **Time to reply** and which follow-up touch triggered it
- **Conversion to signed deal**

Then run one test at a time. Send 20 to 30 pitches with version A of a subject line and 20 to 30 with version B, keeping everything else the same. Other useful tests include stating the budget versus asking for rates, leading with a content reference versus a product benefit, and sending on Tuesday morning versus Thursday afternoon.

Keep your batches manageable. Twenty genuinely personalized pitches per week will usually outperform 200 generic ones, both in replies and in the quality of creators you end up working with.

## FAQ

### What is a good reply rate for creator outreach?

Reply rates vary widely by niche, creator size, and offer, so there's no universal benchmark. As a general pattern, personalized paid offers to well-matched creators get far more replies than generic gifted requests. Track your own baseline over your first 50 to 100 pitches and focus on improving it.

### Should I pitch creators by email or DM?

Email is usually better for paid partnerships because it's more professional, easier to track, and preferred by creators with managers. DMs work well for smaller creators without a listed email, or as a short nudge after an email. Whichever you use, lead with your brand name and make clear the collaboration is paid.

### Is it okay to offer only free product?

Gifting can work with nano creators or genuine fans of your brand, but you should say so upfront and not expect guaranteed content. Make the offer no-strings-attached or pair it with a commission so the creator benefits. For any required deliverables, plan to pay a fee.

### How many times should I follow up with a creator?

Two or three follow-ups over roughly two weeks is a respectful limit. Each follow-up should be shorter than the original and add something new, such as a launch date or product detail. After the final message, step back and note the creator for future campaigns.

## Conclusion

Pitching creators successfully comes down to respect for their time and their craft. Do the research, reference something real, put the offer and compensation upfront, make the next step effortless, and follow up politely without pushing. Then measure what works and keep refining. Brands that treat outreach as relationship-building rather than a numbers game get more replies, better content, and partners who want to work with them again.
