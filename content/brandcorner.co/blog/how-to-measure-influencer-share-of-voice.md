Influencer share of voice is the percentage of all creator-generated conversation in your category that is about your brand rather than your competitors. To measure it, you define a competitor set, collect every influencer mention of each brand over the same period, and divide your brand's mentions, reach or engagement by the category total. If creators mentioned your brand 180 times out of 800 category mentions in a quarter, your influencer share of voice is 22.5%.

The definition is simple. The hard part is making the number reliable and useful. Many teams count hashtags in one tool for one month and compare that figure to an unrelated number later. This guide covers the full process: scoping the category, deciding what counts as a mention, choosing the right metric, collecting and cleaning data, calculating the share, and turning it into decisions.

## What is influencer share of voice, exactly?

Share of voice (SOV) comes from advertising, where it measured a brand's portion of total ad spend or impressions in a category. Influencer share of voice applies the same idea to creator content. It answers one question: **when creators talk about our category, how often are they talking about us?**

This differs from the metrics most influencer reports focus on:

- **Engagement rate** tells you how well individual posts performed.
- **Earned media value** puts a dollar estimate on exposure. It has well-known weaknesses, which we cover in our piece on [whether earned media value is a useful influencer metric](/blog/earned-media-value-influencer-metric).
- **Brand lift** measures changes in awareness, consideration or perception among the audience.

Share of voice is **relative**. A campaign that produced 5 million impressions sounds strong. If your three main competitors produced 40 million in the same window, you are losing the conversation. SOV puts your results in context.

## Why should brands track it?

There are three practical reasons.

1. **It catches competitive shifts early.** If a competitor starts seeding product to dozens of mid-tier creators, their SOV usually rises before you see any effect on sales.
2. **It separates bought attention from earned attention.** When you split paid and organic mentions, you can see whether creators talk about you without being paid. That is a strong signal of brand health.
3. **It supports budget conversations.** Marketers have long observed that brands whose share of voice exceeds their share of market tend to grow. Whatever the exact relationship in your category, SOV gives finance and leadership a benchmark that makes sense to them.

SOV works best as one of several measures. Pair it with the approach in our guide on [how to measure influencer brand lift](/blog/how-to-measure-influencer-brand-lift) to see whether a bigger share of the conversation actually changes how people think about you.

## Step 1: Define your category and competitive set

Every SOV number depends on its denominator, so you need to settle what "the category" means first.

- **Pick 3 to 6 competitors.** Too few, and you miss the real competition. Too many, and the data gets noisy and expensive to collect. Choose brands your customers actually consider, not just brands of a similar size.
- **Define the category boundaries.** A protein snack brand needs to decide whether it competes with protein bars only, or also with protein shakes and "healthy snacks" broadly. Write the boundary down.
- **List brand terms for every competitor.** Include the brand name, common misspellings, product line names, official handles and branded hashtags. One competitor might need 8 to 12 search terms to be captured properly.

**Example:** A mid-size skincare brand might define its category as "vitamin C and brightening serums" and pick three competitors: two established players and one fast-growing newcomer. It then lists brand names, hero product names, @handles and campaign hashtags for all four brands.

## Step 2: Decide what counts as an influencer mention

This step causes more inconsistency than any other. Agree on rules before you collect anything.

### Who counts as an influencer?

Set a minimum follower threshold, for example 5,000 followers. Without one, every customer review and personal post enters the data. Some brands track a separate "everyday consumer" SOV, but keep it apart from creator SOV.

### What counts as a mention?

Decide whether each of these counts:

- Tagging the brand handle
- Naming the brand in the caption or audio
- Showing the product visibly without naming it
- Using a branded hashtag
- Mentioning the brand in a comparison or "dupe" video

A good default is to count a post if the brand is **named, tagged or clearly shown**, and to count each post only once per brand. If a creator compares three serums in one video, that video is one mention for each of the three brands.

### Which platforms?

List them explicitly: for example Instagram, TikTok and YouTube. A B2B brand might focus on LinkedIn instead. ## Step 3: Choose the SOV metric that fits your goal

You can calculate share of voice in several ways. Each answers a slightly different question.

| Metric | Formula | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Mention SOV** | Your mentions ÷ total category mentions | Tracking how many creators talk about you |
| **Reach SOV** | Your potential reach or impressions ÷ category total | Estimating how much audience attention you capture |
| **Engagement SOV** | Your engagements ÷ category engagements | Measuring how much resonance you capture |
| **Sentiment-adjusted SOV** | (Positive − negative mentions) ÷ category total | Understanding quality, not just volume |

We recommend tracking **at least two**, usually mention SOV and engagement SOV. A brand that leads on mentions but trails on engagement is getting lots of low-impact posts. The reverse suggests a few high-performing creators are carrying the brand.

Your choice should follow from your campaign goals. If you haven't defined those clearly yet, start with our guide on [how to set influencer campaign KPIs that work](/blog/how-to-set-influencer-campaign-kpis).

## Step 4: Set a fixed time window and a baseline

SOV only means something when you compare like with like.

- **Choose a consistent window.** Monthly works for brands with steady activity. Quarterly suits brands with lower volumes, where weekly numbers would swing too much.
- **Establish a baseline before your campaign.** Measure at least one full period before launch. If you plan a campaign for March, measure January and February first.
- **Note seasonal events.** Launches, holidays, and competitor announcements all distort SOV. Log them so you can explain spikes later.

**Example:** A brand measuring a six-week spring campaign might measure a six-week baseline beforehand, the six campaign weeks, and a six-week "tail" afterward. This shows whether SOV gains last beyond the paid activity.

## Step 5: Collect the data

You have three main options, often used in combination.

1. **Social listening or influencer platforms.** Most tools let you build keyword and handle queries for your brand and competitors, filter by follower count and export results. They are the most scalable option, but coverage varies by platform. Stories, ephemeral content and spoken mentions in video are often missed.
2. **Platform-native search and manual tracking.** For smaller categories, a spreadsheet and disciplined manual searching of hashtags and tags can work. It takes more time but is often more accurate for niche creators.
3. **Your own campaign data.** For your paid creators, you already have posts and performance data from contracts and reports. Use it to fill gaps in listening tools, but apply the same rules you use for competitors.

For each mention, record at minimum: date, platform, creator handle, follower count, brand(s) mentioned, post URL, views or impressions (if available), engagements, and whether the post is disclosed as paid.

## Step 6: Clean and classify the data

Raw exports usually need significant cleanup. Plan for it.

- **Remove duplicates and irrelevant matches.** A brand called "Glow" will pull in thousands of unrelated posts. Exclude false positives manually or with negative keywords.
- **Remove the brands' own accounts.** Posts from official brand handles are not influencer mentions.
- **Tag paid vs. organic.** Use disclosure labels (#ad, "paid partnership" tags) as the main signal. Proper disclosure, as outlined in our [guide to FTC disclosure rules for influencer campaigns](/blog/ftc-disclosure-rules-influencer-campaigns), makes this classification far more reliable.
- **Tag sentiment.** Positive, neutral or negative. Automated sentiment tools struggle with sarcasm and "dupe" content, so spot-check a sample of 50 to 100 posts by hand.
- **Tag creator tier.** For example nano (5K–10K), micro (10K–100K), mid-tier (100K–500K) and macro (500K+).

## Step 7: Calculate your share of voice

Here is a worked example with hypothetical numbers. Our skincare brand ("Brand X") and three competitors are measured over one quarter.

**Mentions:**
- Brand X: 180
- Competitor A: 320
- Competitor B: 240
- Competitor C: 60
- **Total: 800**

Brand X mention SOV = 180 ÷ 800 = **22.5%**

**Reach (combined impressions):**
- Brand X: 9.5M
- Competitor A: 14M
- Competitor B: 12M
- Competitor C: 2.5M
- **Total: 38M**

Brand X reach SOV = 9.5 ÷ 38 = **25%**

**Engagements:**
- Brand X: 410K
- Competitor A: 520K
- Competitor B: 610K
- Competitor C: 60K
- **Total: 1.6M**

Brand X engagement SOV = 410 ÷ 1,600 = **25.6%**

What this tells you: Brand X is third by number of mentions, but its reach and engagement shares are higher than its mention share. Its creators are posting fewer times but getting relatively strong attention. Competitor B gets far more engagement per mention, so it is worth studying which creators and formats it uses.

## Step 8: Segment the results to find the real story

The headline number is only the start. Segmentation is where you find what to do next.

### Paid vs. organic

Suppose 110 of Brand X's 180 mentions were paid and 70 were organic. Organic mentions across the category were:

- Brand X: 70
- Competitor A: 150
- Competitor B: 200
- Competitor C: 50
- **Total: 470**

Brand X organic SOV = 70 ÷ 470 = **14.9%**

This changes the picture. Brand X's overall 22.5% share depends heavily on paid partnerships. Competitor B has far more creators talking about it voluntarily. That points to stronger word of mouth, better product seeding, or a more shareable product. The right response could be a gifting program, an affiliate scheme, or a product-led content angle, not simply more paid posts.

### By platform

Brand X might hold 35% SOV on Instagram but only 12% on TikTok. That gap often matters more than the blended figure, especially if your target audience spends more time on the weaker platform.

### By creator tier

If competitors dominate among micro-creators while you rely on a handful of macro creators, your SOV is fragile. Losing one partnership could drop it sharply.

### By sentiment

A competitor with high SOV but a large share of negative mentions, such as complaints about a reformulation, is not necessarily winning. Sentiment-adjusted SOV keeps you from chasing volume for its own sake.

## Step 9: Connect SOV to business outcomes

Share of voice is a leading indicator, not an end goal. To make it useful for decisions:

- **Plot SOV alongside sales, site traffic and branded search** over several periods. Look for whether SOV changes come before changes in those outcomes.
- **Compare SOV to your share of market.** If you hold roughly 15% of category sales but 25% of influencer SOV, your creator program is punching above its weight. If the numbers are reversed, you are under-invested relative to your market position.
- **Tie it into your ROI model.** SOV explains *why* awareness-driven returns may be rising or falling. Our guide on [how to measure influencer marketing ROI](/blog/how-to-measure-influencer-marketing-roi) shows how to combine attribution data with upper-funnel metrics like this.

## How often should you measure influencer share of voice?

For most brands, **monthly tracking with a quarterly deep dive** works well. Monthly numbers show trends and flag competitor activity quickly. Quarterly reviews have enough volume for reliable segmentation by platform, tier and sentiment. During major campaigns or launches, weekly tracking can help you adjust creator mix in flight. Just don't read too much into single-week swings when volumes are small.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Changing the competitor set between periods.** Adding a fourth competitor mid-year makes your SOV drop artificially.
- **Mixing data sources.** Comparing a listening tool's numbers this quarter with manual counts last quarter produces meaningless trends.
- **Ignoring video audio and on-screen text.** On TikTok and YouTube, many mentions are spoken rather than written. Text-only tracking undercounts them.
- **Counting only your own campaign posts.** Unless you measure competitors with the same method, you don't have share of voice. You only have volume.
- **Reporting the headline number alone.** Without the paid/organic and sentiment splits, SOV can hide serious weaknesses.

## FAQ

### What is a good influencer share of voice?

There is no universal benchmark, because SOV depends on how many competitors you track and how crowded your category is. A more useful test is whether your SOV is growing over time and whether it matches or exceeds your share of market. Leading your category on organic SOV is generally a stronger position than leading on paid SOV alone.

### Do I need paid tools to measure influencer share of voice?

Not necessarily. Smaller brands in niche categories can track SOV manually with platform search, branded hashtag monitoring and a structured spreadsheet. As volumes grow past a few hundred mentions per month, a social listening or influencer platform saves significant time and improves consistency.

### Should paid partnership posts count toward share of voice?

Yes, but report them separately as well as in the total. Total SOV shows your overall presence in the conversation, while organic SOV shows how much creators talk about you without payment. The gap between the two is one of the most revealing numbers in the analysis.

### How is share of voice different from earned media value?

Share of voice measures your portion of the category conversation relative to competitors. Earned media value assigns an estimated dollar value to your exposure in isolation. SOV is generally more useful for competitive strategy because it shows whether you are gaining or losing ground.

## Conclusion

Measuring influencer share of voice comes down to discipline. Define your category and competitors, agree on what counts as a mention, pick two or three complementary metrics, and collect data the same way every period. The headline percentage is useful, but most of the value comes from segmentation. Splitting by paid vs. organic, platform, creator tier and sentiment shows where you are strong, where competitors are gaining, and where your next budget should go.
