The FTC requires that whenever a creator has a **material connection** to a brand, they must **clearly and conspicuously disclose it**. A material connection includes payment, free products, a commission, a family relationship, or employment. The disclosure has to be hard to miss, use plain language like "ad" or "sponsored," and appear with the endorsement itself, not buried in a bio or a wall of hashtags. Both the creator and the brand can be held responsible when it doesn't happen.

That's the short version. The practical version is harder, because most compliance failures don't come from bad intent. They come from vague briefs, rushed approvals, platform quirks, and nobody checking posts after they go live. This guide covers what the rules actually require, how disclosure works on each major platform, and how to build a repeatable process. It is written for brands and agencies running creator programs in the US or targeting US audiences. It is a practical overview, not legal advice, so have counsel review your specific program.

## What are the FTC disclosure rules, exactly?

The core framework is the FTC's **Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising**, usually called the Endorsement Guides. The FTC updated them in 2023 to reflect how social media advertising works today. The agency also publishes a plain-language resource for creators called *Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers*, which is worth sharing with every creator you work with.

The principle underneath all of it is simple: **consumers should know when someone recommending a product has a reason to be biased.** If a viewer would weigh a recommendation differently after learning the creator was paid, that connection must be disclosed.

Several points from the 2023 update matter for influencer campaigns:

- **"Clear and conspicuous" is now explicitly defined.** On social media, the disclosure must be *unavoidable*, meaning a viewer shouldn't have to click, expand, or scroll to find it.
- **Tags, likes, and virtual influencers can count as endorsements.** An AI-generated or animated "creator" promoting your product is still subject to the rules.
- **Brands can be liable** for misleading claims or missing disclosures by creators they work with, especially if they lack a reasonable monitoring program.
- **Fake and manipulated reviews are treated seriously.** This includes buying reviews, suppressing negative ones, and having employees post reviews without disclosure. The FTC has also finalized a separate rule on fake reviews and testimonials that allows civil penalties.

## What counts as a "material connection"?

Many brands think of disclosure as something that only applies to paid posts. In practice, the definition is much broader. A material connection exists if the creator received any of the following:

1. **Cash payment** of any amount, whether flat fee, retainer, or performance bonus.
2. **Free product**, even if no post was required. Gifting campaigns are the most common blind spot.
3. **Affiliate commissions or promo-code revenue share.**
4. **Discounts, early access, or perks** such as trips, event tickets, or exclusive experiences.
5. **A personal or business relationship**, including employees, family members, investors, and brand ambassadors.
6. **Contest entries or sweepstakes chances** tied to posting.

Here's a concrete example. A skincare brand sends a $60 serum to 200 creators with a note saying "no obligation to post." Thirty of them post about it. All thirty need to disclose that they received the product for free. "No obligation" doesn't remove the material connection. It only means the creator chose to post.

Employee advocacy works the same way. If a B2B company encourages staff to share product content on LinkedIn, those employees should note that they work for the company. This applies to [B2B influencer marketing programs](/blog/influencer-marketing-for-b2b) as much as consumer campaigns.

## What does a compliant disclosure look like?

The FTC doesn't mandate specific wording, but it is clear about what works and what doesn't.

### Wording that generally works

- **#ad** or **Ad:**
- **#sponsored** or **Sponsored**
- **"Paid partnership with [Brand]"**
- **"[Brand] sent me this for free"**
- **"I earn a commission if you buy through my link"**
- **"I'm an ambassador for [Brand]"**, when the relationship is clear

### Wording that is too vague or easy to miss

- **#sp, #spon, #collab**: abbreviations many viewers don't understand
- **#partner** or **#ambassador** on their own, without naming the brand
- **"Thanks, [Brand]!"**, which could mean anything
- **#ad buried among 15 other hashtags** at the end of a caption
- **Disclosures only in the bio, the "About" tab, or a linked page**
- **"Link in bio" with no disclosure about the link's affiliate nature**

### Placement rules of thumb

- **Captions:** Put the disclosure at the start, ahead of the "more" truncation. On Instagram, captions typically collapse after the first line or two, so the first roughly 100 characters are the safe zone.
- **Video:** Disclose *in the video itself*, both said aloud and shown on screen. A caption-only disclosure on a TikTok or Reel can be missed by viewers who never read captions.
- **Livestreams:** Repeat the disclosure periodically, because viewers join at different times. A good practice is every 15 to 20 minutes, plus whenever the product is featured.
- **Stories:** Place a text overlay on every frame that features the product, not just the first one. Make sure it's legible against the background.
- **Language:** The disclosure should match the language of the content. A Spanish-language post needs a Spanish-language disclosure.

## Are platform "paid partnership" tools enough?

Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook, and others offer built-in branded content labels. Use them, because they improve transparency and often unlock features like [whitelisting and paid amplification](/blog/how-to-run-influencer-whitelisting). But don't treat them as a complete solution.

The FTC has said that platform tools may not be sufficient on their own. Some labels are small, sit in low-contrast areas of the screen, or are easy to scroll past. The safest approach is to **layer** the disclosures:

1. Turn on the platform's branded content tool.
2. Add a plain-language disclosure at the start of the caption.
3. For video, include a verbal and on-screen mention within the first few seconds.

Here is a practical breakdown by platform:

| Platform | Built-in tool | Recommended addition |
|---|---|---|
| Instagram (feed/Reels) | Paid partnership label | "#ad" in the first line of caption, verbal mention in Reels |
| Instagram Stories | Paid partnership label | Text overlay on every product frame |
| TikTok | Content disclosure setting / branded content toggle | Verbal and on-screen disclosure in the first 3 to 5 seconds |
| YouTube | "Includes paid promotion" checkbox | Verbal disclosure before the sponsored segment, plus disclosure in the description above the fold |
| LinkedIn | Brand partnership label (where available) | "Sponsored by [Brand]" or "I work at [Brand]" in post text |
| Podcasts | None | Host reads the disclosure before the ad or integrated mention |

## Who is responsible: the brand or the creator?

Both. The creator is responsible for making the disclosure and for not making false claims about products they haven't used. The brand is responsible for not encouraging misleading content and for having **reasonable procedures** to train, monitor, and correct its creators.

In practice, regulators have focused heavily on brands, since brands set the terms, write the briefs, and control the money. The FTC has also sent warning notices to hundreds of companies, putting them on notice about deceptive endorsement practices. Once a company receives that kind of notice, later violations can bring civil penalties of tens of thousands of dollars *per violation*, with the amount adjusted annually for inflation. In a campaign with dozens of creators and hundreds of posts, that exposure adds up quickly.

Agencies are not exempt either. If you plan and manage campaigns for clients, you are part of the chain of responsibility.

## How to build an FTC-compliant influencer workflow

Compliance should be part of your campaign process from the start, not a last-minute check. Here's a seven-step workflow that works for programs with five creators or five hundred.

### 1. Put disclosure requirements in the contract

Your creator agreement should require FTC-compliant disclosure on every piece of content. It should specify acceptable wording and placement, and it should give you the right to request edits or removal. Tie a portion of payment, for example the final 20 to 30%, to compliant posting. Our guide to [influencer contract clauses every brand needs](/blog/influencer-contract-clauses-every-brand-needs) covers how to structure these terms alongside usage rights and exclusivity.

### 2. Spell it out in the brief

Don't write "please follow FTC guidelines" and assume creators know what that means. Give them the exact requirements for each format:

- "Start your caption with: *#ad I partnered with [Brand] to try…*"
- "Say 'This video is sponsored by [Brand]' within the first 5 seconds."
- "Add 'Ad' as text overlay on every Story frame showing the product."

A clear [influencer brief creators will actually follow](/blog/how-to-write-an-influencer-brief) prevents most disclosure problems before they happen.

### 3. Control the claims, not just the disclosure

Disclosure doesn't fix a misleading claim. If a creator says your supplement "cured my anxiety" or your app "doubled my income," you can be liable for that claim even with #ad in place. In your brief, include:

- **Approved claims** with substantiation you can back up
- **Prohibited claims**, especially health, financial, and environmental claims
- A rule that creators only describe experiences they actually had

### 4. Review content before it goes live

Build a pre-approval step into your timeline, typically 48 to 72 hours before the scheduled post date. Use a simple checklist:

- [ ] Platform branded content tool enabled
- [ ] Disclosure visible in first line of caption
- [ ] Verbal and visual disclosure in video
- [ ] No prohibited or unsubstantiated claims
- [ ] Affiliate links disclosed as such
- [ ] Disclosure in the same language as the content

### 5. Monitor posts after they go live

Approved drafts don't always match what gets published. Creators edit captions, re-record videos, or post at the last minute. Check every post **within 24 hours of going live**, and spot-check evergreen content such as YouTube videos and pinned posts quarterly. Many [influencer marketing platforms](/blog/how-to-choose-influencer-marketing-platform) include automated content tracking that can flag missing disclosures at scale.

### 6. Correct problems quickly and document everything

When you find a non-compliant post:

1. Contact the creator the same day with the specific fix needed.
2. Request the edit within 24 to 48 hours.
3. If the creator doesn't comply, request removal and enforce the contract terms.
4. Log the issue, the date you found it, the action taken, and the resolution.

Documentation shows that you run a reasonable monitoring program, and that matters if a regulator ever asks.

### 7. Train your team and your repeat creators

Hold a short annual refresher for internal marketers, community managers, and account managers. For long-term ambassadors, send a one-page disclosure reminder at the start of each contract cycle. Rules evolve, and so do platform features.

## Special situations to watch

**Gifting and seeding.** Include a printed card in every package that asks recipients to disclose the gift if they post. Track who received product so you can monitor what they post afterward.

**UGC and reposts.** If you repost a creator's content on your own channels, the material connection still exists. When you run creator content as paid ads, the ad is already clearly from your brand. Even so, testimonials in those ads must reflect genuine experiences and typical results.

**Affiliate programs.** Every affiliate link needs a disclosure near the link itself. "Links in this post may earn me a commission" works. A generic disclosure on a separate page does not.

**Employee advocacy.** Employees sharing company content should identify their affiliation. This is especially relevant on LinkedIn. If you manage executive or company pages there, agencies like [Social Media Corner, which handles B2B LinkedIn and Instagram management](https://socialmediacorner.co), can help build disclosure into your posting guidelines.

**Kids' content.** The FTC has flagged that children may not understand disclosures the way adults do. Campaigns aimed at children require extra caution and usually a separate legal review.

**Reviews.** Never pay for positive reviews, offer incentives conditioned on a positive rating, or hide negative reviews. If you incentivize reviews at all, the incentive must not depend on sentiment, and reviewers should disclose it.

Missing disclosures show up again and again on lists of [common influencer campaign mistakes brands make](/blog/common-influencer-campaign-mistakes). They're also among the easiest to prevent.

## Does disclosure hurt campaign performance?

This is a common worry, and it's mostly unfounded in practice. Audiences on social platforms are used to sponsored content. What damages trust is *discovering* an undisclosed partnership later. Creators with loyal audiences routinely run clearly labeled sponsorships without losing engagement, because the content itself is good and the fit is genuine.

When performance drops after you add a disclosure, the cause is usually the content, not the label. It may feel like an ad read rather than an authentic recommendation. The fix is better creator matching and more creative freedom, not hiding the partnership.

## FAQ

### Does #ad at the end of a caption count as proper disclosure?

Usually not, if it's buried among other hashtags or hidden behind a "more" truncation. The FTC expects disclosures to be unavoidable, so place "#ad" or "Sponsored" at the beginning of the caption where viewers see it without expanding the text. For video content, also disclose verbally and on screen.

### Do creators need to disclose free products if they weren't paid?

Yes. Receiving a free product is a material connection under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, even when posting was optional. The creator should say something like "[Brand] sent me this for free" or use "#gifted" alongside clear context.

### Can a brand be fined for an influencer's missing disclosure?

Yes, brands can face FTC action if they fail to train and monitor the creators they work with. Penalties can reach tens of thousands of dollars per violation in certain circumstances, such as after a company has received a formal notice. A documented program of contracts, briefs, approval, and post-live monitoring is your best protection.

### Do FTC rules apply to non-US creators?

They apply when the content is aimed at or reasonably likely to reach US consumers, regardless of where the creator lives. Other countries have their own disclosure requirements, such as those in the UK and the EU. International campaigns should follow the strictest applicable standard.

## Conclusion

FTC compliance in influencer marketing comes down to one principle: tell people when there's a connection, and make that hard to miss. The brands that get it right don't depend on creators remembering the rules. They build disclosure into contracts, briefs, approvals, and post-live monitoring, so every campaign is compliant by default. Doing this protects you from regulatory risk, and it also protects the trust that makes creator marketing work.
