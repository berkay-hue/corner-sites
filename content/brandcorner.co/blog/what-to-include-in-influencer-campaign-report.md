An influencer campaign report should include **the campaign's original objectives, a summary of results against those objectives, reach and engagement data, traffic and sales attribution, cost-efficiency metrics, a creator-by-creator breakdown, qualitative insights, and clear recommendations for the next campaign.** A good report covers more than what happened. It explains whether the campaign worked, why it worked or didn't, and what to change. If someone reading it can't decide whether to renew budget, rehire creators, or change strategy, the report isn't finished.

Many influencer reports fail in one of two ways. Some are screenshot dumps full of likes and follower counts. Others are spreadsheets so dense that nobody past the marketing coordinator reads them. This guide covers what belongs in a useful report, how to structure it for different readers, which metrics to skip, and how to collect the data without losing a week to it.

## Who actually reads an influencer campaign report?

Before choosing what to include, think about who will open the document. Most reports have three audiences:

- **Executives and budget holders** want to know whether the money was well spent and whether to do it again. They will read one page, maybe two.
- **Marketing managers** want to compare channels, refine targeting, and plan the next quarter. They read the metrics sections closely.
- **Campaign operators** (in-house team or agency) need creator-level detail: who delivered, who was late, whose content converted, and whose audience didn't fit.

A strong report serves all three. It puts the decision-relevant summary first and the granular data later, so each reader can stop where they need to.

## The core sections of an influencer campaign report

### 1. Executive summary

Keep this to five to eight sentences or a short bulleted block at the top. It should state:

- The campaign goal in one line (for example, "Drive first purchases of the new protein bar among 25–34-year-old fitness enthusiasts")
- The headline result against the primary KPI
- Total spend and the single most important efficiency metric
- One or two key learnings
- A clear recommendation: scale, adjust, or stop

**Example:** "We partnered with 8 creators across Instagram and TikTok to drive first purchases of the new bar. The campaign generated 300 attributed orders against a target of 250, at a cost per acquisition of $66.67. TikTok creators drove 70% of orders with 40% of spend. We recommend shifting budget toward TikTok and rehiring the three top-performing creators for a 3-month ambassador program."

If your executive summary can't fill in that template, the campaign probably lacked a clear objective. That is a planning issue, and our guide on [how to plan an influencer marketing campaign step by step](/blog/how-to-plan-influencer-marketing-campaign) addresses it.

### 2. Objectives and KPIs set before launch

Restate what the campaign was supposed to achieve, using the targets agreed *before* launch. This keeps the report honest. Teams often pick whichever metric looks best after the fact, and this section prevents it.

List:

- **Primary objective** (awareness, consideration, conversion, or content production)
- **Primary KPI and target** (for example, 250 orders, 1 million impressions, or 40 usable UGC assets)
- **Secondary KPIs** (engagement rate, link clicks, follower growth on brand account, email sign-ups)
- **Benchmarks used** (past campaigns, paid social averages, or industry ranges)

A simple table with three columns (KPI, target, actual) followed by a status (met, exceeded, missed) gives readers instant clarity.

### 3. Campaign overview

This section gives context so the numbers make sense. Include:

- **Dates:** brief sent, content live dates, tracking window end date
- **Creators:** number, platforms, follower tiers (nano, micro, mid-tier, macro)
- **Deliverables:** for example, 8 Reels, 12 TikToks, 24 Story frames, 4 YouTube integrations
- **Budget breakdown:** creator fees, product seeding costs, paid amplification, platform or agency fees
- **Usage rights acquired:** duration and channels, since these add value beyond the campaign itself

Be specific about the tracking window. A campaign that ran for two weeks but was measured for 30 days will show different results than one measured for 7 days. If you're unsure how long to run and measure, see [how long an influencer campaign should run](/blog/how-long-should-influencer-campaign-run).

### 4. Reach and awareness metrics

For awareness-focused campaigns, this is the core of the report. For conversion campaigns, it's supporting context. Include:

- **Impressions** (total times content was displayed)
- **Reach** (unique accounts that saw content, when available)
- **Video views** and, ideally, **average watch time or completion rate**
- **Story metrics:** reach, exits, link taps, replies
- **Branded search lift or direct traffic changes** during the campaign window

Use creator-provided screenshots or platform API data, not estimates based on follower counts. Follower count is not reach. A creator with 200,000 followers may reach 15,000 people on an average post.

If brand awareness or perception was a goal, surface-level reach won't prove impact. Consider a proper study, as outlined in our guide on [how to measure influencer brand lift](/blog/how-to-measure-influencer-brand-lift).

### 5. Engagement and content performance

Engagement shows whether the audience actually paid attention. Report:

- **Total engagements** (likes, comments, shares, saves)
- **Engagement rate**, with the formula stated clearly (engagements ÷ reach is more meaningful than engagements ÷ followers)
- **Saves and shares** separately, since they signal stronger intent than likes
- **Top-performing posts** with a thumbnail and a sentence on why they likely worked
- **Underperforming posts** with the same treatment

Always compare engagement rates against relevant platform norms. A 2% rate means something very different on Instagram than on TikTok. Our breakdown of [influencer engagement rate benchmarks by platform](/blog/influencer-engagement-rate-benchmarks) gives useful reference points.

### 6. Traffic, conversions, and sales

For most brands this is the section leadership cares about most. Include:

- **Link clicks / sessions** from UTM-tagged links
- **Landing page behavior:** bounce rate, time on page, add-to-cart rate
- **Conversions:** orders, sign-ups, app installs, demo requests
- **Promo code redemptions** by creator
- **Revenue attributed** and **average order value**
- **New vs. returning customers**, if your analytics allow it

Be transparent about attribution. State whether you used last-click, promo codes, a blended model, or post-purchase surveys ("How did you hear about us?"). Promo codes often capture purchases that UTMs miss, such as someone who watched a TikTok and later typed your URL directly. Codes also leak onto coupon sites, so flag any unusual redemption spikes. For the setup details, see [how to track influencer sales with UTMs and promo codes](/blog/track-influencer-sales-utm-promo-codes).

### 7. Cost-efficiency metrics

This is where raw numbers become business decisions. Calculate and report:

- **CPM** (cost per 1,000 impressions)
- **CPE** (cost per engagement)
- **CPC** (cost per click)
- **CPA** (cost per acquisition)
- **ROAS** (revenue ÷ spend)

**Worked example (illustrative numbers):**

- Total spend: $20,000
- Impressions: 1,200,000 → **CPM = $16.67**
- Engagements: 45,000 → **CPE = $0.44**
- Link clicks: 6,000 → **CPC = $3.33**
- Orders: 300 → **CPA = $66.67**
- Revenue: 300 × $75 AOV = $22,500 → **ROAS = 1.13**

A ROAS of 1.13 might look weak at first glance. But the brand also received, for instance, 20 pieces of licensed content it can use in paid ads for 6 months. Producing similar content in-house could easily cost several thousand dollars, and if the product has strong repeat-purchase rates, customer lifetime value may make a $66.67 CPA very profitable. The report should spell this context out. For a fuller framework, read our [practical guide to measuring influencer marketing ROI](/blog/how-to-measure-influencer-marketing-roi).

Compare these numbers to your other channels where possible. If your paid social CPA is $90, an influencer CPA of $66.67 is a strong argument for more budget.

### 8. Creator-by-creator breakdown

Aggregated numbers hide the most actionable insight: which creators delivered and which didn't. Build a table with one row per creator, including:

- Platform and follower count
- Fee paid
- Deliverables completed on time (yes/no)
- Impressions, engagement rate, clicks, conversions
- CPM and CPA per creator
- Content quality rating (1–5, scored by the team)
- Rehire recommendation (yes / maybe / no)

In most campaigns, a small number of creators drive a large share of results. In the example above, if two of eight creators produced 60% of orders, that changes how you allocate the next budget. Their content style, audience fit, and messaging angle also become your template for future briefs.

### 9. Qualitative insights

Numbers don't capture everything. Include:

- **Comment sentiment:** a summary of positive, neutral, and negative themes, with a few representative quotes
- **Recurring questions** from audiences (about price, sizing, availability, ingredients). These are free product and messaging research
- **Creative observations:** which hooks, formats, or angles worked (for example, "unboxing-style Reels outperformed talking-head videos")
- **Creator feedback:** what creators said about the brief, product, or process

This section often produces the most useful learnings for future creative direction.

### 10. Compliance and operational notes

Document any issues that affected results or created risk:

- Were all posts properly disclosed per FTC guidelines?
- Did any creators miss deadlines or deliver off-brief content?
- Were there approval bottlenecks on the brand side?
- Did any content need revisions or removal?

This makes the report a useful operational record and protects the brand if questions come up later. It also feeds directly into contract and brief improvements.

### 11. Learnings and recommendations

End the body of the report with three to six concrete recommendations. Avoid vague statements like "keep optimizing." Write specific actions instead:

1. Shift 60% of the next campaign budget to TikTok based on CPA performance.
2. Offer 3-month ambassador agreements to the top three creators.
3. Lead future briefs with the "day-in-the-life" angle that drove the highest click-through.
4. Extend the promo code window from 14 to 30 days, as 25% of redemptions came after day 14.
5. Whitelist the top two posts for paid amplification.

## Which metrics should you leave out?

A focused report is more persuasive than an exhaustive one. Consider dropping or demoting:

- **Follower counts as a success metric.** They describe the creator, not the campaign's results.
- **Total likes without context.** Likes are the weakest engagement signal.
- **Earned media value (EMV) as a headline number.** EMV formulas vary wildly between tools and can inflate perceived value. If you report it, explain the methodology and keep it secondary.
- **Metrics with no target.** If nobody agreed a metric mattered before launch, think carefully before featuring it afterward.

## How should you structure the report for different readers?

A practical format that works for most brands and agencies:

- **Page 1:** Executive summary and KPI scorecard (target vs. actual)
- **Pages 2–3:** Key metrics, efficiency, and top-performing content
- **Pages 4–5:** Creator breakdown and qualitative insights
- **Final page:** Recommendations and next steps
- **Appendix:** Raw data, screenshots, full comment analysis, attribution methodology

Use visuals sparingly and purposefully: one chart comparing CPA by creator says more than ten pie charts. Include thumbnails of top content so readers can see what worked.

## How do you collect the data efficiently?

Reporting gets painful when data collection starts after the campaign ends. Build it into the process from day one:

1. **Define KPIs and tracking in the brief.** Tell creators upfront which metrics they'll need to share and by when.
2. **Set up unique UTMs and promo codes per creator** before content goes live.
3. **Require insights screenshots** (or platform API access) at 24 hours, 7 days, and 30 days post-publish. Make this a contractual deliverable.
4. **Log content as it goes live** in a shared tracker with links, dates, and deliverable status.
5. **Pull analytics and e-commerce data** at the end of the tracking window, not before.
6. **Score content quality** as a team within a week of the campaign ending, while impressions are fresh.
7. **Draft the report within two weeks** of the tracking window closing, so learnings inform the next campaign.

An influencer platform can automate much of steps 2–5. Spreadsheets also work fine for smaller campaigns with fewer than ten creators.

## FAQ

### How long should an influencer campaign report be?

Most effective reports are 5–10 pages plus an appendix. The executive summary should fit on a single page so decision-makers can act without reading further. Detailed data belongs in an appendix or linked spreadsheet.

### When should an influencer campaign report be delivered?

Deliver the report within one to two weeks after the tracking window closes, which is typically 14–30 days after the last post goes live. For longer campaigns or ambassador programs, add monthly interim reports so you can adjust spend mid-flight.

### What is the most important metric in an influencer report?

The most important metric is the one tied to the campaign's primary objective, agreed before launch. For sales campaigns, that's usually CPA or ROAS. For awareness campaigns, it's reach, CPM, or brand lift. No single metric is universally most important.

### Should creators receive a copy of the campaign report?

Sharing a short, creator-specific summary is a good practice, especially with creators you plan to rehire. It shows how their content performed and what resonated, which helps them create better work for you next time and strengthens the long-term relationship.

## Conclusion

A useful influencer campaign report connects the work back to business goals. It restates the objectives, shows results against them, translates activity into cost-efficiency metrics, and identifies which creators and creative approaches drove the outcome. Most importantly, it ends with specific recommendations, so every campaign makes the next one smarter and more profitable.
