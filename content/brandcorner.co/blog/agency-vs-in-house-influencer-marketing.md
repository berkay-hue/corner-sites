Choose an **agency** if you need to launch quickly, lack internal influencer expertise, or run campaigns in short bursts. Choose **in-house** if influencer marketing is a core, always-on channel and you want to own creator relationships, data, and institutional knowledge over the long term. Many growing brands land on a **hybrid model**, with an internal owner setting strategy and holding key relationships while an agency or freelancers handle execution at scale.

The right answer depends less on which option is "better" and more on four variables: your budget, your campaign cadence, how much expertise you already have, and how strategic creators are to your brand. This guide covers the real trade-offs, a worked cost comparison, and a step-by-step framework so you can decide with confidence.

## What's the quick decision rule?

If you want a fast gut check before reading further, use this:

- **Fewer than 3–4 campaigns per year, no one internally with influencer experience:** start with an agency.
- **Monthly or always-on creator activity, budget to hire at least one dedicated person:** build in-house.
- **Always-on activity plus large seasonal spikes (launches, holidays, multi-market pushes):** go hybrid.
- **Testing the channel for the first time with a small budget:** consider freelancers or a self-serve platform before committing to either.

The rest of this article explains why these rules work and when to break them.

## What does an agency actually do versus an in-house team?

Before comparing costs, be clear about the work itself. Influencer marketing breaks down into roughly seven jobs, whoever does them:

1. **Strategy:** goals, audiences, positioning, channel mix, budget allocation.
2. **Creator discovery and vetting:** finding the right people and checking for fake followers, brand-safety issues, and audience fit.
3. **Outreach and negotiation:** pitching, agreeing on rates, deliverables, and usage rights.
4. **Contracts and compliance:** agreements, disclosure requirements, exclusivity, payment terms.
5. **Campaign management:** briefs, product seeding, content review, deadlines, posting schedules.
6. **Amplification:** whitelisting, paid boosting, repurposing content into ads.
7. **Measurement and reporting:** tracking links, promo codes, brand lift, ROI analysis.

**An agency** typically bundles all seven and brings an existing creator network, negotiation experience, tools already paid for, and a team that has seen many campaigns succeed and fail.

**An in-house team** does the same work but builds the capability inside your company. Day one is slower, but every relationship, insight, and data point stays with you.

The key question isn't "who can do these jobs?" Both can. It's "which of these jobs do we want to own permanently?"

## How much does each option cost?

Costs vary widely by market, team seniority, and campaign scope. Rather than quoting industry averages, here's a worked example with clearly stated assumptions you can replace with your own numbers.

### Example: agency model

Assume a mid-sized consumer brand runs influencer campaigns year-round.

- Agency retainer or management fee: **$8,000/month** → $96,000/year
- Creator fees (paid to creators, often passed through by the agency): **$150,000/year**
- Agency markup or percentage on creator spend (some charge 10–20%, some include it in the retainer): assume **15%** → $22,500

**Total: roughly $268,500/year**, with about $118,500 going to management rather than creators.

### Example: in-house model

Same brand, same creator budget, but managed internally.

- One influencer marketing manager (salary plus benefits and overhead, using an illustrative figure): **$90,000/year**
- Influencer platform or discovery tool: **$15,000/year**
- Freelance support during peak periods (contract coordinators, editors): **$10,000/year**
- Creator fees: **$150,000/year**

**Total: roughly $265,000/year**, with about $115,000 going to management.

### What the example shows

In this scenario, the headline costs are nearly identical. The difference is **what you get for the management spend**:

- With the agency, you get a full team (strategist, account manager, coordinators, analysts) from day one, but the knowledge leaves when the contract ends.
- With in-house, you get one person who will ramp up over several months, but who builds lasting assets: creator relationships, performance benchmarks, and a repeatable process.

At smaller budgets, the math often favors agencies or freelancers, because a full-time hire is a fixed cost that doesn't shrink when you pause campaigns. At larger budgets, in-house usually wins on efficiency, because agency fees tied to spend grow as you scale while salaries don't.

## When does an agency make more sense?

An agency is usually the stronger choice in these situations:

**1. You need results in weeks, not months.** A product launch in six weeks doesn't leave time to hire, onboard, and train someone. An established agency can often have creators shortlisted within days.

**2. Your campaigns are seasonal or sporadic.** If you run a holiday push and one summer campaign, paying a full-time salary for 12 months makes little sense.

**3. You're entering a new market or platform.** Agencies with experience in a specific country, language, or platform (such as TikTok Shop or YouTube long-form) shorten your learning curve considerably.

**4. You lack the expertise to evaluate what good looks like.** If no one internally can tell an inflated engagement rate from a healthy one, an agency protects you from expensive beginner mistakes.

**5. You need scale quickly.** Coordinating 50+ creators across a single launch is operationally heavy. Agencies have processes and staff for this.

### The downsides to watch for

- **Knowledge walks out the door.** When you switch agencies, creator relationships and performance history often go with them.
- **Less control over creator selection.** Some agencies lean on their existing roster rather than finding the best fit for your brand.
- **Misaligned incentives.** If fees are a percentage of creator spend, there's a built-in incentive to spend more, not to spend smarter.
- **Slower feedback loops.** Approvals pass through an account manager, which can add days to every revision cycle.

## When does in-house make more sense?

Building internally tends to win when:

**1. Influencer marketing is a core, always-on channel.** If creators drive a meaningful share of awareness or sales every month, this is a capability you should own.

**2. Long-term creator relationships matter.** Ambassador programs, recurring partnerships, and co-created products depend on trust built over time. That's hard to outsource. Our guide on [building long-term creator partnerships](/blog/how-to-build-long-term-creator-partnerships) covers why continuity matters so much.

**3. Your brand voice is nuanced or technical.** B2B, regulated industries, and premium brands often need someone who lives inside the company to brief creators accurately.

**4. You want to integrate creators with other teams.** In-house managers can connect influencer work directly to product, paid social, email, and ecommerce, repurposing creator content across channels without cross-agency friction.

**5. You want to own the data.** Every campaign teaches you which creators, formats, and offers convert. In-house, those lessons compound.

### The downsides to watch for

- **Slow ramp-up.** Expect a new hire to need roughly three to six months to build a creator pipeline and refine processes.
- **Single point of failure.** If your only influencer manager leaves, the program can stall.
- **Tool and process costs.** You'll need to pay for discovery, contracting, payments, and reporting infrastructure yourself.
- **Limited bandwidth.** One person can comfortably manage a certain number of active creators. Beyond that, quality slips.

## What about a hybrid model?

For many brands, the most practical answer is a mix. Common hybrid setups include:

- **In-house strategy, outsourced execution:** your team sets goals, chooses creators, and owns relationships, while an agency or freelancers handle outreach, logistics, and reporting.
- **In-house always-on, agency for spikes:** your team runs the monthly ambassador program, and an agency is brought in for big launches or new markets.
- **In-house core creators, agency for discovery:** your team manages your top 10–20 recurring partners, while an agency tests new creators at scale.

The hybrid model works best when **ownership is crystal clear**. Decide in writing who owns creator relationships, contracts, content usage rights, and campaign data. Without this, you get duplicate outreach, confused creators, and reports nobody trusts.

## How do you decide? A 6-step framework

Use this framework to make a structured decision rather than a gut call.

1. **Map your cadence for the next 12 months.** List every planned campaign, launch, and seasonal moment. If activity is continuous, lean in-house. If it's clustered, lean agency.

2. **Calculate your true budget.** Separate creator fees from management costs. If your total annual influencer budget can't comfortably absorb a full-time salary plus tools, an agency or freelancer model is likely more efficient for now.

3. **Audit your internal skills.** Score your team from 1–5 on each of the seven jobs listed earlier. Any job scoring 1–2 is a candidate for outsourcing.

4. **Decide what must stay in-house.** Most brands should own at least strategy, budget decisions, and final creator approval, regardless of model.

5. **Set a review checkpoint.** Commit to your chosen model for 6–12 months, then reassess with real performance data.

6. **Plan the transition path.** If you start with an agency, require that creator contacts, contracts, content files, and performance data are shared with you, so you can bring the work in-house later without starting from zero.

## How do you evaluate an influencer agency?

If you go the agency route, ask these questions before signing:

- **How do you find and vet creators?** Look for a process that goes beyond their existing roster, including fraud checks and audience analysis.
- **How are you paid?** Flat retainer, percentage of spend, or performance-based? Each creates different incentives.
- **Who owns the creator relationships and content rights?** Get this in writing.
- **What does reporting look like?** Ask for a sample report. Vanity metrics alone (impressions, reach, "earned media value") are a red flag if not paired with business outcomes.
- **Who will actually work on our account?** Meet the team, not just the sales lead.
- **What happens when we leave?** Clarify handover of data, contacts, and assets.

Also review the agency's standard creator agreements. Our checklist of [influencer contract clauses every brand needs](/blog/influencer-contract-clauses-every-brand-needs) is a useful reference for spotting gaps around usage rights, exclusivity, and disclosure.

## How do you build an in-house influencer team?

If you're building internally, start lean and expand as results justify it.

### Your first hire

Look for someone who combines **relationship skills** (creators should enjoy working with them), **operational discipline** (deadlines, contracts, payments), and **analytical comfort** (tracking, attribution, reporting). Previous agency experience is a real advantage, because they've seen many campaigns and know the common pitfalls.

### Your first 90 days

- **Days 1–30:** define goals and KPIs, select tools, build a creator shortlist, draft brief and contract templates.
- **Days 31–60:** run a pilot with 5–10 creators, set up tracking with UTMs and promo codes, document every step.
- **Days 61–90:** analyze results, identify top performers for repeat partnerships, and build a 6-month roadmap.

### Tools and support

The right software can make a single manager far more productive. As the program grows, typical next hires are a coordinator for logistics and a content or paid specialist for amplification.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Choosing based on cost alone.** The cheapest option often costs more in wasted creator fees and poor-fit partnerships.
- **Hiring junior and expecting senior results.** An entry-level hire without guidance will struggle to build strategy from scratch.
- **Outsourcing strategy entirely.** Even with an agency, someone internal must own goals and judge whether the work is moving the business.
- **Ignoring data ownership.** Without access to historical performance, every new partner starts from zero.
- **Never revisiting the decision.** What made sense at a $50,000 budget rarely makes sense at $500,000.

## FAQ

### Is it cheaper to run influencer marketing in-house or through an agency?

It depends on your volume. At small or sporadic budgets, agencies and freelancers are usually cheaper because you avoid fixed salaries and tool costs. At larger, always-on budgets, in-house tends to be more cost-efficient because management costs don't rise with creator spend.

### How long does it take to build an in-house influencer program?

Most brands should expect three to six months before an in-house program runs smoothly. That time covers hiring, tool setup, building a creator pipeline, and refining processes through a pilot campaign. Agencies can typically launch faster because those foundations already exist.

### Can I switch from an agency to in-house later?

Yes, and many brands do exactly that once the channel proves its value. To make the transition smooth, ensure your agency contract gives you access to creator contacts, signed agreements, content files, and performance data. Without these, your in-house team will effectively start over.

### Is a hybrid model hard to manage?

A hybrid model works well when roles are clearly defined and documented. The main risk is confusion over who owns creator relationships, approvals, and reporting. Agree on these responsibilities upfront and hold a regular sync between your internal lead and external partners.

## Conclusion

There's no universal winner between agency and in-house influencer marketing. Agencies give you speed, expertise, and scale without long-term commitments, and they suit brands with sporadic campaigns or limited internal experience. In-house teams give you control, lasting relationships, and compounding data, and they suit brands where creators are a core, always-on channel. For many growing companies, a hybrid that keeps strategy and key relationships internal while outsourcing execution offers the best of both.

Start by mapping your campaign cadence, separating creator fees from management costs, and deciding which capabilities you want to own for good. Then commit to a model, measure it honestly, and adjust as you grow.
