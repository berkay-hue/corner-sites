In B2B SaaS, the choice between a free trial and a demo depends on **your product's complexity, your average contract value (ACV), and whether buyers can evaluate the product on their own**. A free trial works better for low- to mid-priced products where users can see value within a few days without help. A demo is the right choice for high-priced products that require integration during setup and need sign-off from multiple decision-makers. For many companies, a hybrid model that combines the two by segment delivers the best results.

This decision changes far more than the text on a website button. It directly affects how your sales team is structured, where your marketing budget goes, what data you track in your CRM, and your customer acquisition cost. Companies that pick the wrong model run into one of two problems. Either they end up dealing with hundreds of users who open a trial account and never come back, or they burn out their sales team running 45-minute demos for small customers who could have bought on their own. In this article, we walk through the differences between the two models, when each one works, how to calculate the costs, and how to implement them, with concrete examples.

## What is the key difference between a free trial and a demo?

Both models aim to move a prospect from "I'm interested" to "I'm buying." The difference lies in who leads that journey.

**A free trial** puts the user in control. The user signs up and explores the product on their own for a set period, usually 7, 14 or 30 days. They also make the purchase decision largely based on their product experience. In this model, the product is the sales engine. Even if there is a sales team, it usually plays a supporting role.

**A demo** puts the sales team in control. The prospect fills out a form, books a call with a sales rep, and sees the product under their guidance. The rep analyzes the prospect's needs, presents the product through the prospect's own scenario, handles objections and moves the process toward a proposal.

Here is a summary of the key differences:

- **Time to value:** In a trial, users have to find the value themselves. In a demo, the value is shown to them.
- **Scalability:** A trial can serve many users at once. A demo is limited by the sales rep's calendar.
- **Quality:** Demo requests are usually higher quality, because setting aside time for a call is a commitment. Trial sign-ups are more numerous but more mixed.
- **Information gathering:** In a demo, you learn the prospect's budget, decision process and timeline directly. In a trial, you have to infer this information from usage behavior.
- **Cost structure:** A trial requires investment in the product and onboarding. A demo requires sales team salaries and time.

## When does a free trial work better?

A free trial is powerful when the product can sell itself. If most of the conditions below apply to you, you should consider a trial-led model.

**1. Setup is fast and can be done without help.** A trial works if users can see their first meaningful result within 10-15 minutes of signing up. An email signature management tool, a simple project management app or a form builder, for example, all fall into this category.

**2. Average contract value is low or mid-range.** For a subscription worth a few thousand dollars a year, it isn't economical for a sales rep to hold 3-4 meetings. In this price range, the most effective way to keep customer acquisition cost low is to let the product itself drive the purchase decision.

**3. The user and the buyer are the same person, or closely connected.** With a tool a marketing specialist buys for their own team, the user, the evaluator and the decision-maker are often the same person. In that case, a trial can convert directly into a purchase.

**4. The market is crowded and comparison-heavy.** If buyers want to try several alternatives side by side, not offering a trial can knock you off the shortlist. A company that offers only demos while all its competitors offer trials makes buyers ask, "What are they hiding?"

**5. You can track product usage data.** The strength of the trial model comes from seeing which features users engage with and stepping in at the right moment. Without this infrastructure, a trial turns into an uncontrolled leak. ## When is a demo the better choice?

A demo comes to the fore when buyers can't, or would rather not, evaluate the product on their own.

**1. The product requires integration or data migration.** For software that integrates with ERP, accounting, CRM or production systems, a trial account is little more than an empty screen. Users can't see the value without their own data. In a demo, by contrast, you can show a scenario populated with sample data and tailored to the prospect's industry.

**2. The purchase decision involves multiple people.** In enterprise sales, IT, finance, legal and the user department all take part in the decision. Each of them asks different questions. A trial account doesn't answer those questions. A sales rep, however, can address each stakeholder's concerns individually.

**3. Average contract value is high.** On a deal worth tens of thousands of dollars a year, the sales team's time pays for itself. At this level, buyers already expect a consultative, customized experience.

**4. The product's value is complex or emerges over the long term.** The value of a forecasting or analytics platform may only become clear after several weeks. A 14-day trial isn't enough to demonstrate it. In a demo, you can explain the expected outcome through a business scenario.

**5. The market needs education.** If you are creating a new category and buyers haven't yet clearly defined the problem, leaving users alone with the product is risky. A demo gives you the chance to explain the problem and the solution together.

## What questions should you ask to choose the right model?

This decision should be made with a systematic framework, not gut feeling. Answer the six questions below with your team and note which model each answer points to.

1. **How long does it take a new user to see their first meaningful result without help?** Under 30 minutes is a signal in favor of a trial. If it takes several days or requires integration, that's a signal in favor of a demo.
2. **What is your average annual contract value?** A low range points toward a trial, a high range toward a demo. The middle ground usually calls for a hybrid model.
3. **How many people are involved in the purchase decision?** One or two people point to a trial; three or more point to a demo or a hybrid model.
4. **What are your competitors doing, and what do buyers expect?** Deviating from the industry norm is possible, but it needs to be a deliberate choice.
5. **What is your sales team's capacity?** You can't handle 300 demo requests a month with two sales reps. If capacity is insufficient, you need either a trial or a strong pre-demo qualification mechanism.
6. **Can you track product usage data and pass it on to your sales team?** Without this infrastructure, the trial model's efficiency drops significantly.

For example, consider appointment scheduling software for SMBs. Setup takes 15 minutes, the monthly price is low and the business owner makes the decision alone. Almost all six questions point to a trial. On the other hand, a maintenance management platform for factories requires integration with machine data, has a high annual contract value, and the decision is made jointly by the production manager, IT and procurement. In this case, a demo is practically mandatory.

## Can you use both together?

Yes. In practice, many B2B SaaS companies use hybrid approaches rather than a pure model. The four most common hybrid models are:

### Segment-based routing

The website offers both "Try for free" and "Request a demo." However, the sign-up form asks for company size, routing small companies into the trial flow and companies above a certain headcount to the sales team. This way, the sales team spends time only on high-potential accounts.

### Sales-assisted trial

Users start the trial on their own, but accounts that show certain behaviors in the product (e.g., inviting three people to the team or using a critical feature) are passed to the sales team. At that point, a sales rep reaches out and turns the trial into a sales opportunity.

### Guided trial after the demo

A short discovery call and demo come first, followed by a trial environment set up with the customer's own data. This model is ideal for highly interested buyers who want to see the product in their own environment. This setup is often called a "pilot" or a "proof of concept (POC)."

### Interactive demo

You offer an interactive tour that requires no sign-up, letting users click through the product's core flows. This approach bridges the gap between a trial and a demo and lets website visitors get a feel for the product without talking to sales.

## How much does it cost?

Both models have costs; they just show up in different places. Before deciding, we recommend running a simple unit economics calculation with your own numbers. The example below is entirely hypothetical and is meant only to illustrate the calculation logic.

**Trial model example:**

- You get 500 trial sign-ups per month.
- Your marketing spend for these sign-ups is $10,000 per month. Cost per sign-up is $20.
- Your trial-to-paid conversion rate is 8%. That means 40 new customers per month.
- Additional monthly cost for the onboarding team, support and infrastructure is $4,000.
- Customer acquisition cost: (10,000 + 4,000) / 40 = **$350**.

**Demo model example:**

- You get 80 demo requests per month.
- Your marketing spend for these requests is $8,000 per month.
- Your demo-to-close conversion rate is 20%. That means 16 new customers per month.
- The total monthly cost of two sales reps is $12,000.
- Customer acquisition cost: (8,000 + 12,000) / 16 = **$1,250**.

At first glance, the trial model looks much cheaper. But the second half of the calculation is the revenue each customer brings in. If a trial-model customer pays $600 a year, you recover the acquisition cost in about 7 months. If a demo-model customer pays $9,000 a year, you recover it in under 2 months. So the right question isn't "Which is cheaper?" but **"Which is more efficient relative to customer lifetime value?"**

Costs that are commonly overlooked in this calculation include:

- Support tickets generated by trial users
- Server and infrastructure costs for trial accounts
- Sales time wasted on demo no-shows
- Hiring and training time for the sales team

## Which metrics should you track?

Whichever model you choose, you can't improve a process you don't measure. The core metrics to track differ between the two models.

### For free trials

- **Activation rate:** How many users who start a trial complete the first action that delivers the product's core value?
- **Time to value:** The average time from sign-up to the first meaningful result.
- **Trial-to-paid conversion rate:** How many of all trial sign-ups become paying customers?
- **Weekly active usage:** How often users come back during the trial period.
- **PQL rate:** The share of accounts that meet the usage criteria you've defined.

### For demos

- **Demo request rate:** What share of website visitors request a demo?
- **Show rate:** What share of scheduled demos actually take place?
- **Demo-to-opportunity conversion:** How many post-demo conversations become qualified sales opportunities?
- **Win rate:** What share of opened opportunities close?
- **Sales cycle length:** The average time from first contact to signature.

## How do you design a free trial process?

If you've chosen the trial model, simply adding a sign-up button isn't enough. A successful trial process is built with these steps:

1. **Define the "aha moment."** Identify the concrete action through which users first feel the product's value. For a CRM, this might be creating the first opportunity. For a reporting tool, it might be sharing the first dashboard.
2. **Shorten the sign-up form.** Ask only for an email and password to start the trial. Collect data such as company details, role and team size step by step during onboarding.
3. **Solve the empty-screen problem.** Provide a starter environment populated with sample data, or templates. An empty screen is the most common reason users leave in the first few minutes.
4. **Set up a behavior-based email flow.** Instead of sending everyone the same email, create messages triggered by the steps users take. For example, send a short guide video to a user who still hasn't created their first project by day three.
5. **Match the trial length to the product's value cycle.** A 7-day trial isn't enough for a product used for weekly reporting. Choose a length that lets users experience at least one full cycle.
6. **Identify sales intervention points.** Define which usage signals call for a sales rep to step in, and push those signals to your CRM automatically.
7. **Plan the end-of-trial experience.** Rather than abruptly locking users out when the trial ends, show them a summary of the data they've created and offer an extension option.

## How do you design a demo process?

In a demo model, success depends more on process design than on the sales rep's charisma.

1. **Qualify before the demo.** Ask about company size, role and top priority in the form. Automatically route unsuitable requests to resource content or the trial flow.
2. **Place calendar booking right after the form.** Waiting for a sales rep to get back after the form is submitted cools interest. Letting users pick a convenient time on the same screen increases the show rate.
3. **Secure attendance.** Send a reminder email and the meeting agenda beforehand. Personalize the agenda based on the need the prospect stated in the form.
4. **Separate discovery from the demo.** Spend the first 10-15 minutes understanding the prospect's current processes and pain points. Only start showing the product once you have that information.
5. **Show the flow that solves the prospect's problem, not every feature.** Instead of rushing through 40 features, explain in depth how you solve the 2-3 problems the prospect raised.
6. **Use social proof.** Share a short story about the results a company similar to the prospect's industry achieved. To keep these stories ready, you can use [our guide to writing an effective B2B SaaS case study](/blog/how-to-write-b2b-saas-case-study).
7. **End with a clear next step.** Every demo should end with a next meeting on the calendar or a concrete action: a technical evaluation, a pilot setup or a proposal presentation.

## What should you watch out for in the Turkish market?

B2B buyer behavior in Turkey differs from general global trends in a few respects.

- **Relationship-driven buying:** Especially at mid-sized and large companies, buyers want to see that there is a reachable team behind the software vendor. A pure trial model may not meet this need. Even if you offer a trial, having a visible sales or customer success rep builds trust.
- **Turkish-language onboarding:** Even if the product interface is in Turkish, user drop-off during the trial increases if help content, email flows and videos remain in English.
- **Pricing transparency:** Because of currency fluctuations, buyers are cautious about pricing. Clearly stating whether post-trial pricing will be in Turkish lira or foreign currency, and how it will be updated, has a positive effect on conversion.
- **KVKK and data residency:** Enterprise buyers ask where their data is stored before uploading real data to a trial account. Have the answers to these questions ready during the demo or in trial onboarding.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **Copying the competition:** Just because a competitor offers a trial doesn't mean it's right for your product. Your product complexity and target segment may be different.
- **Starting the trial and leaving users on their own:** A trial offered without onboarding and follow-up is largely a wasted channel.
- **Handling every demo request the same way:** Running the same demo flow for a one-person startup and a company with hundreds of employees is a waste of resources.
- **Turning the demo into a product tour:** A demo given without listening to the prospect remains a monologue and rarely turns into a sale.
- **Looking only at sign-up numbers:** Growth in trial sign-ups is misleading if activation and conversion rates are falling.
- **Making the decision once and forgetting about it:** As your product evolves, your target segment shifts or your pricing goes up, you need to reassess your model.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days should a free trial last in B2B SaaS?

The ideal length is the time it takes users to fully experience the product's core value at least once. For simple tools, 7-14 days is usually enough. For products that run on weekly or monthly cycles, 30 days may be more appropriate. Very long trials, on the other hand, increase users' tendency to procrastinate.

### What is the difference between freemium and a free trial?

A free trial gives access to all or most of the product for a limited time and requires payment once it ends. Freemium offers a limited version of the product for free indefinitely and encourages upgrading to a paid plan for advanced features. Freemium builds a broader user base, but its conversion rate is usually lower than a trial's.

### Does offering both a trial and a demo cause confusion?

Not if positioned correctly. You need to clearly state which audience each option is for. For example, you can use clear messages like "Try it yourself now" and "Request a personalized walkthrough for your team." Automatically routing users by company size in the sign-up form also reduces confusion.

### Which brings in more qualified leads: a trial or a demo?

Demo requests are usually more qualified, because setting aside time for a call requires a higher commitment. However, trial users reveal their intent more reliably through their in-product behavior. That's why lead quality can also be high in a trial model backed by usage data.

## Conclusion

Choosing between a free trial and a demo is less a matter of preference and more a matter of fit. Your product's complexity, your contract value, the number of people in the decision process and your sales team's capacity largely tell you which model will be more efficient. Trials stand out for low-priced, quick-to-set-up products, while demos stand out for high-value products that require integration. Companies in the middle ground can combine the advantages of both worlds with segment-based hybrid models.

Whichever model you choose, the key to success is measuring the process, tracking the right data in your CRM and improving regularly based on the results.
