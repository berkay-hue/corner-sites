**Buying a B2B customer list is not illegal in itself, but if your approach is "buy it and start using it right away," it isn't safe either.** Whether it is safe comes down to three things: the legal basis on which the data was collected, how current and accurate it is, and how you use it. In Turkey, KVKK (the Personal Data Protection Law) and Law No. 6563, and in Europe, GDPR, place much of the responsibility for purchased data on the company that uses it. In other words, saying "the vendor gave us the list" won't protect you.

At B2B SaaS companies, the idea of buying a list usually comes up in a quarter when the pipeline is running thin. A new SDR team has been hired and the targets are set, but there's no one to call or email. In this article, we use concrete examples to examine the legal, operational and financial risks of buying lists. If you do choose this route, we also explain step by step how to manage the risk and what works better in the long run.

## What exactly does buying a B2B customer list mean?

In practice, "buying a list" can refer to three very different things:

1. **Buying a ready-made, static list:** Getting an Excel file from a broker, such as "procurement managers at manufacturing companies in Istanbul with 500+ employees." The source is often unclear, the last update date is unknown, and the same list has been sold to other companies too.
2. **Subscribing to a data platform:** SaaS data platforms where you build your own lists by filtering company and contact information, and where the data is updated regularly. Here you're not buying a specific file but access to a database.
3. **Custom list building:** A list prepared specifically for you, in which an agency or an in-house team researches and verifies each record one by one based on your ideal customer profile (ICP).

The level of risk varies significantly across these three models. The riskiest are static lists of unknown origin. Data platforms and custom research, when used properly, offer much more defensible ground. ## Is buying a B2B list legal in Turkey?

Short answer: **It depends.** Being "B2B" doesn't mean the data isn't personal data. A corporate email address like ayse.yilmaz@firma.com.tr is personal data because it identifies a specific individual. That's why the assessment needs to be made under several different pieces of legislation.

*Note: This section is for general information purposes only. Always seek advice from a legal counsel for your specific situation.*

### What you need to know about KVKK

Under the Personal Data Protection Law No. 6698, the moment you process the data of the people on a list you've purchased, you become a **data controller**. This requires you to answer the following questions:

- **What is the legal basis?** In B2B communication, the "legitimate interest" basis is usually the one under discussion. This basis is not unlimited. Processing must not harm the person's fundamental rights and freedoms and must align with their reasonable expectations. A software company presenting an offer directly related to their work to someone with a CTO title is not the same as passing that person's mobile number around on a sales list.
- **How will you fulfill your obligation to inform?** Even when you don't obtain the data directly from the person, you must inform them of the purpose for which their data is being processed. In practice, this means clearly stating who you are, why you're processing the data and how they can object at the first point of contact.
- **Is the data coming from abroad or going abroad?** If you use platforms where data is stored on servers outside Turkey, you need to assess KVKK's provisions on cross-border transfers (mechanisms such as standard contracts).
- **Is your VERBİS registration up to date?** If you're subject to the registration obligation, you need to update your inventory whenever you start a new data processing activity.

Administrative fines for KVKK violations are adjusted every year by the revaluation rate and can reach substantial amounts. But for most SaaS companies, the real risk isn't the fine; it's **reputational damage and the erosion of customer trust.** Especially if you sell to enterprise customers, your data practices are now being scrutinized during their procurement processes. ### What do Law No. 6563 and İYS say?

The Law on the Regulation of Electronic Commerce and its related regulation generally require prior consent for commercial electronic messages (email, SMS, calls). However, **prior consent is not required for messages sent to tradespeople and merchants**. This exemption matters for B2B sales, but it has a few subtleties:

- The exemption depends on the recipient being a merchant or tradesperson. A company employee's personal Gmail address or personal mobile number may not fall within its scope.
- The recipient always has the **right to refuse**. You must offer an easy, free way to opt out in every message.
- You need to clarify how the registration and query obligations of the Message Management System (İYS) apply to your sending model.

In short, the generalization "it's B2B, so no permission is needed" is dangerous. The accurate statement is this: B2B outbound is a defensible activity when you reach out **to the right person, through the right channel, with the right content, and always with an option to opt out**.

### GDPR if you sell internationally

If you're reaching people in Europe, GDPR comes into play. Under GDPR, legitimate interest can also be used for B2B communication. However, when you don't obtain data directly from the person, the obligation to inform (Article 14) is clear: you must state the source of the data, the purpose of processing and the person's rights. The upper limit GDPR sets for serious violations is 20 million euros or 4% of global annual turnover. Some EU countries also apply their own local rules to B2B email. Germany is known as a particularly strict example.

## Why do purchased lists often fail to deliver the expected results?

Legal risk is only half of the equation. The other half is performance. Most purchased lists don't address the real bottlenecks in sales:

- **Data decays quickly.** People change jobs, get promoted, companies merge, domains change. A considerable share of a list compiled a year ago now points to the wrong person or to an address that no longer exists.
- **The same list has been sold to others.** Dozens of companies may have bought the "IT managers in Turkey" list along with you. If these people receive similar emails every day, your message gets lost in the noise.
- **ICP fit is low.** Ready-made lists are usually built with rough filters like industry and headcount. In SaaS sales, however, what really matters are triggers such as the technology in use, growth signals, new funding or hiring.
- **It damages your email infrastructure.** High bounce rates, spam traps and complaints lower your domain's sender reputation. Google and Yahoo now require bulk senders to keep their spam complaint rate below 0.3% and to offer one-click unsubscribe. A bad list can send not only that campaign but also the invoices and onboarding emails you send to your customers into the spam folder.
- **It increases churn risk.** Selling to the wrong profile may look like a closed deal in the short term, but a poorly fitting customer leaves quickly. ## How much does it cost? Sticker price vs. real cost

The most misleading part of the decision to buy a list is the price tag. The calculation below is based entirely on **example assumptions**. Think of it as a template to rerun with your own numbers.

Let's say you paid 25,000 TL for a list of 5,000 records. At 5 TL per record, it looks reasonable at first glance.

1. **Verification:** When you run it through an email verification tool, 25% of the records turn out to be invalid or risky. You're left with 3,750 records.
2. **ICP filter:** When your SDR team reviews the remaining list, they find that only half of the records actually match your target profile. You're left with 1,875 records.
3. **Reply rate:** If you get a 2% reply rate with a non-personalized sequence, you'll receive about 37 replies. Half of them will be "not interested" or "take me off your list."
4. **Meetings:** In the end, you get roughly 15-18 meetings.

At this point, the cost per meeting rises to the 1,400-1,700 TL range based on the list alone. On top of that, you need to add:

- The cost of the verification tool
- The hours your SDRs spend cleaning and researching the list (usually the biggest item)
- Additional sending domains and the warm-up process
- Weeks lost if your domain reputation is damaged
- The cost of legal review

For comparison, consider this: if the same SDR, in the same amount of time, works through a personalized sequence on 500 up-to-date, signal-based records filtered by ICP, it wouldn't be surprising for the reply rate to multiply several times over. Fewer records often mean more meetings. The metric you should measure is **not cost per record, but cost per qualified meeting.**

## When can buying a list make sense?

Rejecting list buying outright isn't realistic either. In some scenarios, a well-structured data source can save a lot of time:

- **Market sizing:** Using company-level data before entering a new vertical or country to answer questions like "How many companies are in this segment, and how many have a defined decision-maker role?" Here you may not even need personal data.
- **Account lists for an account-based approach:** First building a list of companies (accounts), then adding contact data later in a targeted, verified way.
- **Enriching existing CRM data:** Filling in missing titles, industries or headcounts for companies you're already in contact with.
- **Regularly updated data platforms:** Platforms where you can filter and verify, instead of a static file. For example, tools like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) let you build lists with your own ICP filters and see email verification status. This is a far more controllable model than an Excel file of unknown origin. Still, responsibility under KVKK and Law No. 6563 remains with you.

The common thread: in scenarios that make sense, data is used **as a starting point**, not as a campaign on its own.

## How should you evaluate a data provider?

If you've decided to buy data, ask the provider the following questions in writing. Every question you can't get a clear answer to is a red flag.

1. **From which sources and on what legal basis was the data collected?** "Publicly available sources" is not a sufficient answer on its own. Being publicly available doesn't mean data can be processed for any purpose.
2. **When was it last updated, and how often is it updated?**
3. **Is email verification performed? Can the verification date be seen per record?**
4. **How many customers has the same list been sold to?** Is it exclusive or shared?
5. **How are opt-out requests and objections handled?** When someone asks for their data to be deleted, is that request reflected in the database?
6. **Does the contract define data processing and the allocation of responsibility?**
7. **Can they provide sample data?** Verify a 100-record sample yourself and measure its ICP fit. If even the sample is bad, the full list won't be any better.
8. **Where is the data stored?** Is there any cross-border transfer involved?

## Steps for using purchased data safely

Once you have the data, we recommend the following process to minimize risk:

1. **Put the legal framework in writing.** Clarify with your legal counsel which legal basis you're relying on, your legitimate interest assessment and your privacy notice. Do this before the campaign, not after a complaint arrives.
2. **Verify the entire list.** Separate invalid, risky and "catch-all" addresses. Keeping the bounce rate below 2-3% is a widely accepted threshold for protecting sender reputation.
3. **Filter out personal channels.** Treat personal email addresses (Gmail, Hotmail, etc.) and personal mobile numbers separately. Remember that the B2B exemption may not apply to these channels.
4. **Import into your CRM with a source tag.** Keep a field in your CRM recording where, when and under which contract each record came from. When an objection comes in, you should be able to show this information within seconds.
5. **Cross-check against existing customers and your opt-out list.** Writing again to someone who previously said "don't contact me" is the worst-case scenario, both legally and reputationally.
6. **Don't send from your main domain.** Use separate, warmed-up domains for outbound. Increase daily sending volume gradually.
7. **Be transparent in your first message.** Clearly state who you are, why you're writing to this person and how they can opt out. A one-line note such as "If you're not interested, just reply and I'll remove you from the list" provides both legal and practical protection.
8. **Start with a small test group.** Try it with 200-300 records first. Don't send to the entire list before measuring bounce, reply and complaint rates.
9. **Process opt-out requests immediately.** Remove "take me off" replies from your system automatically or, at the latest, on the same day.
10. **Set a retention period.** Delete or anonymize records with no engagement within a set period. Data kept forever is a risk that lasts forever.

## What can you do instead of buying a list?

Instead of a definition like "Industry: Finance, Employees: 200+," define the triggers that increase the likelihood of a purchase: hiring a new sales director, using a specific competitor's software, funding news, opening a new office and so on. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is buying a B2B email list banned in Turkey?

No, it isn't outright banned. However, because the corporate email addresses on the list are considered personal data, you must meet the legal basis and notification obligations under KVKK. In addition, under Law No. 6563, you must offer an easy way to refuse in every message.

### Is it allowed to send emails to tradespeople and merchants without permission?

The relevant regulation does not require prior consent for commercial electronic messages sent to tradespeople and merchants. However, this exemption does not remove the recipient's right to refuse, and it may be debatable for channels such as personal email or personal phone numbers. KVKK obligations also continue to apply separately.

### How do I test the quality of a purchased list?

Before buying, ask for a sample of 100-200 records. Run this sample through an email verification tool to measure the rate of invalid addresses, then review the records one by one to calculate how well they fit your ideal customer profile. If the results from the sample are poor, the full list is most likely of the same quality.

### Which is safer: buying a list or subscribing to a data platform?

In general, data platforms that are regularly updated and show verification status are more controllable and safer than static lists of unknown origin. Still, whichever model you choose, legal responsibility passes to your company the moment you use the data. That's why verification, transparent communication and opt-out management are mandatory in both models.

## Conclusion

Buying a B2B customer list is, in itself, neither a miracle nor a crime. What's risky is using data of unknown origin in mass sends without verifying it, without establishing a legal framework and without clarifying your ICP. For a team that asks the right questions, verifies its data, communicates transparently and measures results by "cost per qualified meeting," data can be a powerful accelerator. In the long run, however, your most valuable asset will be your own signal-based, continuously fed data system.
