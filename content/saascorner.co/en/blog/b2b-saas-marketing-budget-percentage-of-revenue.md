One of the questions B2B SaaS leaders ask most often during budget season is: "What percentage of our revenue should we spend on marketing?" The appeal of the question is easy to understand. A single ratio can be presented to the board, discussed easily with the finance team, and benchmarked against competitors. In practice, though, this approach often pushes companies either to overspend or to underinvest so much that they miss growth opportunities. That's because a marketing budget is less a percentage of past revenue than a function of the target you want to reach in the future.

In this article, we set aside the search for the "right ratio" and use concrete examples to show how to calculate your budget backward from your target, which assumptions to start with depending on your growth stage, how the budget should be split across line items, and which metrics to use to track whether the spend is working. The goal is to help you walk into your next budget meeting with a defensible calculation, not with "this is how everyone in the industry does it."

## Why Is There No Single Answer to "What Percentage?"

Allocating a fixed percentage of revenue to marketing can be a reasonable shortcut in mature, slow-growing industries. In B2B SaaS, however, three structural factors undermine that shortcut.

**First, SaaS revenue is cumulative.** A customer you win this year generates recurring revenue for years. As a result, the return on today's marketing spend shows up not in this year's income statement but in the ARR of future years. Looking at a percentage of current revenue ignores the timing of the payback.

**Second, the company's stage changes the ratio entirely.** For an early-stage startup with annual revenue of a few million TL, "10 percent of revenue" barely funds a single meaningful campaign. On the other hand, for a company that has built a large customer base and generates a significant share of its growth from expansion revenue from existing customers, the same ratio may be more than necessary.

**Third, the sales model determines the cost structure.** In a self-serve, product-led (PLG) model, marketing takes on part of the work a sales team would otherwise do, and the budget shifts toward marketing. In an outbound-heavy model selling to enterprise customers with long cycles, most of the cost sits in the sales team, SDRs, and data infrastructure.

That's why it's healthier to treat the ratio debate as an **outcome**: first you model the target and the funnel, the budget emerges, and only then do you look at its ratio to revenue and ask whether it's reasonable.

## The Key Variables That Determine Your Budget

Before running any numbers, you need to pin down your company against the variables below. Whether you're at 5 million or 500 million TL in ARR, these form the backbone of your budget.

- **Growth target:** How much new ARR do you want to win from new customers over the next 12 months? Are you planning expansion revenue (upsell, cross-sell) separately?
- **Average contract value (ACV):** An SMB subscription at 30 thousand TL per year and an enterprise contract at 1 million TL per year require completely different marketing economics.
- **Sales cycle length:** The longer the cycle, the bigger the time gap between marketing spend and closed deals, which affects cash planning.
- **Conversion rates:** Your conversion rates from lead to MQL, MQL to SQL, and SQL to customer. If you don't know these rates, the first step in budgeting is to measure them.
- **Pipeline source mix:** How much of your pipeline comes from inbound marketing, how much from outbound, and how much from partners?
- **Gross margin:** Required to calculate CAC payback period.
- **Market maturity:** Is the category well known, or do you first need to explain the problem? If you're creating a new category, awareness spend is unavoidable.

Each of these variables pushes the answer to "what percentage" up or down. When defining your conversion stages, it's critical to draw a clear line between MQL and SQL; [our article on the difference between MQL and SQL in B2B SaaS](/blog/what-is-mql-mql-vs-sql-b2b-saas) is a good place to start.

## Working Backward from the Target: A Step-by-Step Budget Model

The most defensible method is the "reverse funnel" calculation. You start from your revenue target, work backward through each conversion stage, and end up with how much demand you need to generate from each channel and what it will cost. The example below is built entirely on hypothetical figures; you'll need to replace them with your own data.

### 1. Convert your new ARR target into a number of customers

Suppose your current ARR is 30 million TL and you're targeting **12 million TL** in new ARR from new customers next year. If your average ACV is 240 thousand TL:

- 12,000,000 / 240,000 = **50 new customers**

### 2. Use your win rate to find the number of SQLs needed

If your SQL-to-customer win rate is 25 percent:

- 50 / 0.25 = **200 SQLs**

### 3. Split SQLs by source

Based on your historical data, let's say 50 percent of pipeline comes from inbound marketing, 35 percent from outbound, and 15 percent from partners:

- Inbound: 100 SQLs
- Outbound: 70 SQLs
- Partner: 30 SQLs

### 4. Calculate the number of MQLs needed for inbound

If your MQL-to-SQL conversion rate is 30 percent:

- 100 / 0.30 ≈ **333 MQLs**

### 5. Use cost per MQL to find the program budget

If the average cost per MQL across your channels is 3,000 TL:

- 333 × 3,000 ≈ **1 million TL** inbound program budget (ads, content distribution, webinars, etc.)

### 6. Add outbound, partner, and fixed costs

Add the SDR capacity, data, and sequencing tools needed for the 70 SQLs coming from outbound; co-marketing funds for the partner channel; team salaries; and content production. A sample allocation:

- Inbound program budget: 1,000,000 TL
- Outbound tools, data, and infrastructure: 500,000 TL
- Content, events, and brand initiatives: 1,000,000 TL
- Partner marketing funds: 400,000 TL
- Marketing team personnel costs: 2,500,000 TL
- **Total: 5,400,000 TL**

### 7. Now look at the ratios

This is where the "what percentage" question becomes meaningful:

- Ratio to current ARR: 5.4 / 30 = **18 percent**
- Ratio to targeted new ARR: 5.4 / 12 = **45 percent**

The second ratio is usually more informative, because it looks at the revenue the spend is expected to generate.

### 8. Sanity-check it with CAC payback period

Marketing doesn't win customers on its own. If the sales team's annual cost is 6 million TL, total sales and marketing cost comes to 11.4 million TL. If your gross margin is 80 percent:

- Gross profit equivalent of new ARR: 12 × 0.80 = 9.6 million TL
- Payback period: 11.4 / 9.6 ≈ 1.19 years, or **roughly 14 months**

If this period fits your company's cash position and investor expectations, the budget is reasonable. If it's too long, you need to revisit the target, the investments that would improve conversion rates, or the channel mix.

The strength of this model is that every assumption is visible. When the board asks, "Why is the budget this high?", your answer isn't a ratio but a testable chain of logic.

## Starting Assumptions by Stage

If you don't have enough historical data for the reverse funnel model, you'll need to start your first draft with some rough assumptions. The framework below isn't an industry average; it's a logical approach you can use to kick off the planning discussion.

### Early stage (searching for product-market fit)

At this stage, a percentage of revenue is almost meaningless, because revenue is small and the budget is mostly funded by investment. The right question isn't "How much should we spend?" but **"What is the minimum we need to spend to learn which channel works?"**

- A significant portion of the budget goes to experiments: small-scale ad tests, founder-led outbound, a few in-depth pieces of content.
- Avoid expensive brand campaigns and large trade shows.
- Set a clear learning goal for each channel: "Does this channel produce 10 qualified meetings in three months?"

### Growth stage (repeatable sales model established)

Here the company usually aims for aggressive growth, and sales and marketing spend can reach a significant share of revenue; it's not even surprising for it to exceed revenue in some periods. What matters is that spending stays within healthy CAC payback and LTV:CAC limits.

- Scale investment goes into channels that have been proven to work.
- Investment in long-term assets such as content and SEO begins.
- Budget is allocated to marketing operations (RevOps, CRM, attribution).

### Mature stage (scale and efficiency)

Because the existing customer base is large, part of new revenue comes from expansion, and marketing spend as a ratio of revenue naturally declines. The focus shifts to efficiency and brand strength.

- Customer marketing, community, and advocacy programs gain weight.
- The partner ecosystem becomes a major pipeline source.
- Returns are continuously optimized by channel; underperforming spend is cut.

## Where Does the Budget Go? Core Line Items

Finding the total figure is half the job. The other half is allocating it to the right line items. A B2B SaaS marketing budget generally consists of four main groups.

### 1. People

Marketing team salaries, plus agency and freelancer fees. In most B2B SaaS companies, this is one of the largest line items. It's important to track it separately from the program budget; otherwise, the perception that "we spend too much on marketing" may actually be about team costs rather than campaigns.

### 2. Program and channel spend

- **Paid search:** Brings in high-intent leads in categories where demand already exists. For setup and cost details, see our [Google Ads guide for B2B SaaS](/blog/google-ads-b2b-saas-guide).
- **LinkedIn ads:** Strong targeting, but high cost per click. We covered when they create value in [our article examining the value of LinkedIn ads for B2B SaaS](https://saascorner.co/blog/linkedin-reklamlari-b2b-saas-deger-mi).
- **Content and SEO:** Low returns in the short term, compounding returns in the long term. In your budget plan, account for the fact that the impact of this line item will show up six to twelve months later.
- **Events and webinars:** Effective ways to build relationships in enterprise sales; however, they're costly and their impact on pipeline must be measured rigorously.
- **Partner marketing:** Joint events, co-created content, and marketing development funds.

### 3. Technology and data

CRM, marketing automation, analytics, website infrastructure, and data and sequencing tools for outbound. For companies in Turkey, this is the line item most exposed to currency risk; most tools are priced in foreign currency.

### 4. Content and creative production

Case studies, product videos, guides, and sales enablement materials. This line item is often underestimated; yet every other channel becomes inefficient without good content.

One rule of thumb can help with budget allocation: **Put most of the budget into proven channels and set aside a small but protected portion for experiments.** A marketing team without an experimentation budget can't find new sources of growth once existing channels become saturated.

## Reflecting the Outbound–Inbound Balance in Your Budget

At many B2B SaaS companies in Turkey, a significant share of pipeline comes from outbound. In that case, thinking of the marketing budget as only ads and content paints an incomplete picture. Outbound has a different cost structure:

- **SDR salaries and commissions:** Usually sit in the sales budget, but they are a demand generation function.
- **Contact data and prospecting tools:** Up-to-date data is essential to reach the right decision-maker. For example, tools like [Apollo.io](https://get.apollo.io/679irkzm35ya) are commonly used to build target account lists and manage email sequences.
- **Email infrastructure:** Separate domains, a warm-up process, and verification tools for deliverability.
- **Content support:** The case studies and industry-specific content that outbound messaging relies on.

Building outbound in-house can take several months, from hiring the team to selecting tools. You also need to factor the cost of that ramp-up period into your budget. Some companies choose to run demand generation with an external team to speed up this phase; for example, working with a partner like [Sales Corner, which specializes in data-driven B2B sales and lead generation](https://salescorner.co), can offer a more predictable variable cost model instead of fixed team costs.

What matters is being able to compare outbound and inbound costs within the same framework. To do this, calculate each channel's **cost per SQL** and **cost per customer won**. Even if a channel shows a low cost per MQL, a channel with a weak close rate can actually be the most expensive one.

## Considerations Specific to the Turkish Market

Applying budget recommendations from global sources directly often produces misleading results in Turkey. Keep these differences in mind when planning:

- **Currency and inflation effects:** When your revenue is in TL but part of your costs are in foreign currency, the real value of your budget shifts over the year. Track foreign-currency line items on a separate row and set up a quarterly revision mechanism.
- **Ad cost volatility:** Costs on digital ad platforms can change during the year. Instead of a fixed annual ad budget, set quarterly target costs (for example, a maximum cost per SQL).
- **Relationship-driven sales culture:** In Turkey, references, face-to-face meetings, and trust carry a lot of weight in enterprise decision-making. For this reason, budget allocated to events, customer reference programs, and the partner channel can deliver higher returns.
- **The Turkish-language content gap:** In many B2B SaaS categories, high-quality Turkish-language content is limited. This means SEO investment can pay off relatively faster. ## How Do You Know Whether the Budget Is Working?

The real work begins once the budget is approved. The metrics below show whether your spend is going to the right places.

- **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** Total sales and marketing cost divided by the number of new customers. Calculate it separately by channel and by segment.
- **CAC payback period:** As in the example above, how many months it takes for the spend to be recovered through gross profit. One of the most practical metrics for cash planning.
- **LTV:CAC ratio:** The ratio of customer lifetime value to acquisition cost. If your churn rate isn't measured reliably, your LTV estimate can be misleading; in that case, give more weight to the payback period.
- **Pipeline per TL spent:** The ratio of pipeline value generated by marketing to spend. In companies with long sales cycles, it provides an early warning signal before closed revenue arrives.
- **Cost per SQL by channel:** Cost per MQL alone is misleading; track it all the way down to SQL and customer won.
- **Marketing-sourced and marketing-influenced revenue:** First-touch attribution and multi-touch attribution tell different stories. Report both together.

All of these metrics depend on a sound CRM setup. If lead source fields aren't filled in, or opportunities aren't linked to the right campaigns, even the best budget model turns into guesswork. ## Common Budgeting Mistakes

Some mistakes come up again and again when planning a marketing budget:

1. **Planning by adding a percentage to last year's budget.** Channel performance may have changed; the "we spent this much last year, let's increase it a bit this year" approach also scales up inefficient channels.
2. **Allocating the entire budget to short-term demand generation.** Companies that lean solely on performance ads eventually face rising costs and saturated audiences. Content and brand investments are compounding assets that lower CAC down the road.
3. **Planning marketing and sales budgets in separate silos.** When SDRs are planned under sales, content under marketing, and CRM under operations, nobody sees the total cost of the funnel.
4. **Not setting aside an experimentation budget.** Without a protected budget for testing new channels, every experiment is seen as "taking money away from an existing channel," and none get run.
5. **Treating foreign-currency tool costs as fixed in TL.** This leads to the tools budget ballooning unexpectedly mid-year.
6. **Thinking about measurement as an afterthought.** If attribution isn't set up before campaigns launch, the end-of-budget review turns into a matter of opinion.
7. **Forgetting existing customers.** Expansion revenue is usually less costly than acquiring new customers. Not budgeting for customer marketing is a major missed opportunity.

## A 90-Day Budget Planning Process

The process below offers a practical framework for putting your marketing budget on solid ground:

1. **Weeks 1–2: Data collection.** Pull the last 12 months of spend by channel, along with lead, MQL, SQL, and customer-won counts, from your CRM. Identify missing fields.
2. **Week 3: Calculate conversion rates.** Determine actual conversion rates for each stage and each channel, as well as the average sales cycle.
3. **Week 4: Clarify the target.** Finalize new ARR, expansion revenue, and segment targets together with finance and sales leadership.
4. **Weeks 5–6: Build the reverse funnel model.** Using the steps above, calculate the SQLs and MQLs needed by channel, then the costs.
5. **Week 7: Build scenarios.** Prepare three scenarios: conservative, base, and aggressive. Show the CAC payback period for each.
6. **Week 8: Allocate to line items.** Split the budget into people, programs, technology, and content; protect the experimentation budget as a separate row.
7. **Weeks 9–10: Set up measurement infrastructure.** Put in place CRM fields, UTM structure, campaign-to-opportunity attribution, and reporting dashboards.
8. **Weeks 11–12: Approval and communication.** Present the budget to leadership along with its assumptions; align on SLAs with the sales team (lead follow-up time, MQL acceptance criteria).
9. **Week 13 and beyond: Quarterly revision.** At the end of each quarter, feed actual conversion rates back into the model and update the channel mix.

This process may seem laborious in the first year. But from the second year on, you'll have a model fed by real data, and budget discussions will become much shorter and more objective.

## Conclusion

The honest answer to "What percentage of revenue should a B2B SaaS marketing budget be?" is this: the right ratio is an outcome derived from your company's goals, ACV, sales model, and conversion rates. Instead of starting with a fixed percentage of revenue, build a model that works backward from the target, makes every assumption visible, and is sanity-checked against CAC payback period. Your budget will be both more defensible and more efficient.

In summary:

- Start from your new ARR target and model the funnel backward.
- Use the ratio as a control metric, not a starting point.
- Evaluate marketing and sales costs within the same framework.
- Protect your experimentation budget while making scale investments in proven channels.
- Factor local dynamics such as currency, the relationship-driven sales culture, and the Turkish-language content opportunity into your plan.
- Update the model with real data every quarter.
