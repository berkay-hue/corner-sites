In B2B SaaS, comparison pages are content that places your product side by side with well-known competitors or alternative solutions and targets the search queries closest to a purchase decision. Someone searching for "X vs Y," "X alternatives" or "best X software" already knows they need a solution and is narrowing down their options. That's why a well-built comparison page can generate far more demo requests than blog posts, with far less traffic.

In this guide, we walk step by step through the types of comparison pages, which competitors to start with, how to build the page section by section, the ethical and legal boundaries, technical SEO details and how to measure performance. Our examples reflect the real search behavior of B2B SaaS companies in the Turkish market.

## What is a comparison page and why is it critical for B2B SaaS?

B2B purchasing is never a one-person decision. When a company selects a CRM, HR software or accounting solution, there is usually an end user, a manager and a finance or IT approver involved. At some point in the process, each of them types "Which one is a better fit for us?" into Google.

Comparison pages are critical for three reasons:

- **Purchase intent is high.** Someone searching "What is a CRM?" is still in the learning stage. Someone searching "HubSpot vs Pipedrive" has already built a shortlist.
- **You control the narrative.** If you don't write a comparison page, a competitor or a review site will write one for you, and it most likely won't present you in terms of your greatest strengths.
- **It equips your sales team.** A good comparison page also becomes the source of your standard answer to the question "How are you different from Competitor X?" in sales calls.

Comparison pages rank much faster when they're built on a solid foundation.

## What types of comparison pages are there?

Not every comparison page serves the same search intent. Before you start planning, you need to distinguish between five core types.

### 1. "Us vs Competitor" pages

Example query: "[Your product] vs [Competitor]". These pages work once your brand has some level of awareness. The user knows both options and is deciding between them. Search volume is usually low, but the conversion rate is among the highest of any page type.

### 2. "Competitor alternatives" pages

Example query: "[Competitor] alternatives" or "what to use instead of [Competitor]". This is the most valuable format for brands that aren't well known yet. Your competitor's existing users look for alternatives because of price increases, missing features or poor support. Even if these people have never heard of you, your comparison page can get you onto their shortlist.

### 3. "Competitor A vs Competitor B" pages

Example query: "Salesforce vs HubSpot". You compare two of your competitors and then present your own product as a third option. Search volume can be large, but these pages need careful framing. Since the reader isn't looking for your product, the page will cost you credibility unless you deliver an impartial and genuinely useful comparison.

### 4. Category lists

Example query: "Best CRM software 2026" or "Bookkeeping software for SMBs". These pages list multiple options. When you include your own product in the list, clearly stating your selection criteria increases credibility.

### 5. "Us vs the current method" pages

Example query: "Excel or CRM for customer tracking?" In many B2B categories in Türkiye, the real competitor isn't another piece of software but Excel, WhatsApp groups or in-house tools. These pages target the part of the market that hasn't gone digital yet, and this is often where you face the least competition.

## Which competitors should you start with?

Writing ten pages for ten competitors at once wastes resources and increases the risk of producing low-quality content. Follow these steps to prioritize:

1. **Ask your sales team.** Which competitors came up in the deals you lost and won last quarter? If your CRM doesn't have a "competitor" field, add one right away. Within two months, you'll have a clear picture.
2. **Look at search data.** For each competitor, examine the volume and difficulty of queries such as "[competitor] alternatives," "[competitor] vs," "[competitor] pricing" and "[competitor] reviews." Check Turkish and English variations separately. Turkish users usually write brand names in English but attach Turkish suffixes ("HubSpot alternatifi" for "HubSpot alternative," "Pipedrive fiyatları" for "Pipedrive pricing").
3. **Assess your chances of winning.** Is there a clear area where the competitor is weak and you are strong? Differences such as Turkish-language support, local invoicing integrations, TRY-based pricing or KVKK (Turkish data protection law) compliance can be a significant advantage in the Turkish market.
4. **Build a scoring table.** Score each competitor from 1 to 5 based on how often you encounter them in sales, search volume and the strength of your differentiation. Make the three competitors with the highest total score your priority for the first quarter.

**Example scenario:** Picture a SaaS company selling CRM to SMBs in Türkiye. The sales team reports that in most conversations, two global CRMs and Excel come up as alternatives. In that case, the first three pages should be: "Global CRM A alternatives," "Our product vs Global CRM B" and "Excel or CRM for customer tracking?" Pages about the big players in the enterprise segment can wait until the next quarter, even if they have higher search volume.

## What sections should a comparison page include?

Successful comparison pages share a similar skeleton. The structure below is a good starting point for "Us vs Competitor" and "Competitor alternatives" pages.

### Top section: the 10-second summary

On the first screen of the page, the reader should get the answer to this question: "What is the core difference between these two products, and who is each one right for?" A two- or three-sentence summary and a CTA are enough. For example: "Competitor X offers a comprehensive platform for large sales teams. Our product delivers faster setup for teams of 5-50 people, with a Turkish interface, e-invoice (e-Fatura) integration and TRY-based pricing."

### Comparison table

The table is the most scanned section of the page. Eight to 15 rows is ideal. More won't get read; fewer will feel superficial. You can choose your table rows from the following:

- Starting price and pricing model
- Free trial or free plan
- Turkish interface and Turkish-language support
- Local integrations (e-Fatura, local banks, accounting software)
- Data hosting location and KVKK
- Setup time
- Mobile app
- Reporting and API

**Important rule:** You shouldn't win every row of the table. Honestly show the two or three rows where the competitor is genuinely better. A table with a checkmark on every row looks like an ad to the reader and destroys trust.

### "Who is it right for?" section

This section may be the most valuable part of the page. Write two short lists with the headings "Competitor X may be the better choice if..." and "Our product may be the better choice if..." When readers see themselves in the right segment, they make a decision. When they see themselves in the wrong segment, they were never going to be a good-fit customer anyway, and this protects your sales team's time.

### Detailed comparison sections

Write 100-200 word explanations for each important criterion in the table. Add screenshots, setup steps and real use cases. Instead of abstract claims like "more user-friendly," choose verifiable statements such as "It takes a sales rep 3 clicks to create their first opportunity."

### Social proof

The experiences of customers who switched from a competitor to you are the most persuasive content on a comparison page. If you have a customer story about the switching process, add a summary to the page and link to the full version. We explain in detail how to prepare this kind of content in our [guide to writing B2B SaaS case studies](/blog/how-to-write-b2b-saas-case-study).

### Migration section

If you're targeting a competitor's users, the biggest objection is "How do I move my data?" Clearly explain the data migration process, the estimated timeline and the support your team provides. If possible, make a concrete offer such as free migration support.

### FAQ and closing CTA

Answer the 4-6 most frequently asked questions about the competitor and close the page with a clear call to action.

## Where are the boundaries when writing about a competitor?

Comparison pages are one of the most legally and ethically sensitive areas of marketing. Following these principles protects both your reputation and your company:

- **Use only verifiable information.** Take the competitor's pricing, features and limitations from their own website or publicly available documentation. State on the page the date you collected the information ("This comparison was prepared based on publicly available information as of ...").
- **Avoid disparaging language.** Phrases like "outdated technology" or "terrible support" both erode trust and create legal risk.
- **Check local regulations.** Türkiye has regulations on comparative advertising, and comparisons are expected to rely on objective, measurable and non-misleading criteria. Getting an opinion from a legal advisor before publishing is good practice.
- **Be careful with trademark use.** Don't use a competitor's logo or visual identity in a way that could be mistaken for their actual product.
- **Open a correction channel.** Add a note at the bottom of the page: "If you notice any inaccurate information in this comparison, please let us know." This small detail builds a strong sense of trust with readers.

An ethical approach isn't just risk management; it's also a sales strategy. ## What should you watch for on the technical SEO side?

The technical setup of the page determines rankings just as much as content quality.

### URL structure

Choose a consistent structure and apply it across all pages:

- `/karsilastirma/urununuz-vs-rakip-x`
- `/alternatifler/rakip-x-alternatifleri`

Grouping all comparison pages in a single folder is beneficial for both user experience and internal linking.

### Title and meta description

Use both the competitor's name and a differentiating element in the title. For example: "Competitor X Alternatives: 5 CRMs with Turkish-Language Support (With Pricing Comparison)". In the meta description, tell readers what they'll find on the page: a pricing table, who each option is right for, and the migration process.

### Structured data

You can use FAQ schema for the FAQ section and Article schema for the page as a whole. Avoid adding review ratings or stars for the competitor's product. This can be misleading and may also conflict with search engine policies.

### Internal linking

Comparison pages often end up isolated within a site. To prevent this:

- Add a "Comparisons" section to your main menu or footer.
- Add contextual links from relevant blog posts to your comparison pages.
- Add a "Compare us with competitors" link from your pricing page.
- Link your comparison pages to one another ("See also our comparison with Competitor Y").

### Freshness

Competitors change their pricing and features frequently. A comparison page with outdated information both erodes trust and leads to ranking drops. Set up a quarterly review schedule for each page and keep a visible "Last updated" date on the page.

## How do you turn comparison page visitors into customers?

Visitors who land on a comparison page are in the decision stage. That's why your conversion setup should be different from your blog posts.

**CTA choice:** Offering an e-book or newsletter subscription here usually wastes the opportunity. Give visitors a direct way to experience the product. To decide whether to offer a free trial or a demo request based on how your product works, you can use the framework in our article [comparing free trials and demos](/blog/free-trial-vs-demo-b2b-saas).

**Competitor-specific offer:** Offers such as "20% off for the first 3 months for Competitor X users" or "Free data migration" lower switching costs and speed up the decision.

**Short form:** Don't wear out a decision-stage user with a long form. Name, work email, company and an optional "Which tool are you currently using?" field are enough in most cases. That last field gives your sales team valuable context before the call.

**Sales handoff:** Leads coming from comparison pages shouldn't be dumped into the general lead pool. These people have competitor knowledge and are close to a decision. Tag them in your CRM as "source: comparison page – Competitor X" and make sure the sales team follows up with them the same day. ## How do you measure performance?

Evaluating comparison pages on traffic alone is misleading. These pages have low traffic but high value. Here are the metrics you should track:

1. **Rankings for target queries:** Monitor 3-5 primary queries for each page on a weekly basis.
2. **Page-level conversion rate:** Measure what share of visitors start a demo or trial. Compare this rate with your blog average. Comparison pages are expected to convert significantly better. If they don't, the problem most likely lies in the CTA or the page structure.
3. **Pipeline contribution:** Track the total value and win rate of opportunities originating from these pages in your CRM.
4. **Sales cycle length:** Examine whether leads who visited a comparison page close faster.
5. **Sales team usage:** Are sales reps sharing these pages in post-call follow-up emails? This is a qualitative metric, but it reveals the content's true value.

Don't make decisions based on first-month results in your reporting. A new page usually takes a few months to settle into the rankings.

## How much does it cost and when will you see results?

The cost of a comparison page largely depends on whether it's produced in-house or by an agency. Account for the following items in your resource planning:

- **Research:** Reviewing the competitor's product with a trial account, reading documentation, talking to the sales team. Typically 4-8 hours per page.
- **Writing and editing:** 6-10 hours for a 1,500-2,500 word page.
- **Design:** 3-6 hours for the comparison table, screenshots and visuals.
- **Legal review:** An external advisory fee, especially for the first pages.
- **Maintenance:** A 1-2 hour update for each page every quarter.

Roughly speaking, a single well-crafted page represents a 15-25 hour workload. When deciding how to allocate these resources, take a look at our [guide to setting a B2B SaaS marketing budget](/blog/b2b-saas-marketing-budget-percentage-of-revenue).

The time to results depends on competition. For low-competition "alternatives" queries, reaching the first page within a few months is possible. For "vs" queries involving major global brands, it takes longer. In the meantime, you can also support the page with paid ads on competitor brand queries, but keep in mind that using a competitor's brand name in ad copy is limited by platform policies and trademark rights.

## What plan should you follow in the first 90 days?

**Days 1-30: Foundation**
- Add a "competitor" field to your CRM and map out competitors with the sales team.
- Select your first three competitors using the scoring table.
- Design the page template (sections, table rows, CTA) once.
- Write the first page, run it through legal review and publish it.

**Days 31-60: Scaling**
- Publish the second and third pages.
- Add a current-method comparison such as "Us vs Excel."
- Add internal links from blog posts and the pricing page.
- Give the sales team a short walkthrough of the pages and ask them to use them in follow-up emails.

**Days 61-90: Optimization**
- Review ranking and conversion data.
- Test the CTA, first-screen summary and table on low-converting pages.
- Add new objections from sales calls to the FAQ sections.
- Update the competitor list for the next quarter.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- **A table that wins every row:** Readers instantly recognize it as an ad.
- **Outdated information:** A page showing a competitor's pricing from a year ago will be spotted immediately by that competitor's users.
- **Weak CTA:** Offering a newsletter subscription to a decision-stage reader.
- **One page, every competitor:** Comparing ten competitors on a single page provides depth for no query at all.
- **Leaving the sales team out of the process:** The sales team knows the real objections best.
- **Language that badmouths competitors:** Satisfying in the short term, damaging to your reputation in the long term.
- **Not segmenting leads:** Dropping a lead from a comparison page into the general newsletter list.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should we write comparison pages if our brand isn't well known yet?

Yes, but you should start with the "Competitor alternatives" and "Us vs the current method" formats rather than "Us vs Competitor." These pages don't depend on people searching for your brand name. They directly target a competitor's users or companies still using Excel.

### Is it legal to state a competitor's pricing on a comparison page?

Sharing publicly available and accurate pricing information, along with its source and date, is generally considered objective comparison. Still, since Türkiye has regulations on comparative advertising, it's advisable to have a legal advisor review your first pages before publishing. Updating prices regularly also reduces the risk of misleading information.

### How many comparison pages are enough?

Priority matters more than quantity. For most B2B SaaS companies, pages for the three competitors encountered most often in sales plus one "current method" page are a good start. As these pages deliver measurable results, you can expand the list quarter by quarter.

### Do comparison pages replace blog posts?

No, the two content types complement each other. Blog posts attract readers in the awareness and learning stages, while comparison pages convert readers in the decision stage. The best results come when blog posts link naturally to comparison pages.

Comparison pages are one of the most effective ways to capture the search queries closest to the purchase decision in B2B SaaS. Even if traffic volume looks low, a few well-built pages can contribute far more directly to your pipeline than blog content.
