**Did You Hire a Person for Sales, or Build a System?**

CVs are reviewed, interviews are held, an offer is made. The person starts. The first week is introduction meetings, the second week is product training, the third week is getting used to the CRM. A month goes by and not a single meeting has been booked yet.

This slowness comes not from the person's shortcomings, but from the absence of a system.

**The hidden cost of a full-time sales rep**

The gross salary is calculated, but that's not where it ends.

The hiring process takes two to three months on average. Training is a separate process. During this time the person is paid but not yet productive. Then come the licenses for the tools they'll use, the CRM setup and the time spent writing sequences. And there's one more reality: if the sales rep leaves, the process resets and everything starts over.

On top of that, a single person cannot research, write messages, make calls and analyze data all at the same time. Expecting all of this from one person sets them up to fail.

**What difference does the SDR model make?**

The SDR structure relies not on one person, but on interconnected roles working together.

There is someone who researches potential customers. There is someone who writes and tests the messages. There is someone who reads and reports on the data. There is someone who manages the whole process and steps in when needed. When these four roles work together, what you get is not an employee, but a working machine.

And when this structure is brought in from outside, setup time is shorter and the company can keep focusing on its core business.

**Which one is more expensive?**

At first glance, a full-time employee looks cheaper. But once the overlooked items are added in, the picture changes.

When hiring costs, training time, tool licenses, the time it takes for performance to settle and a possible resignation scenario are taken into account, the real cost of a single sales rep climbs much higher. And when that person works alone, the productivity ceiling is low.

A well-designed SDR model, on the other hand, offers a scalable structure from the start. As targets grow, the system grows; when they shrink, it can be adjusted flexibly.

**So is the SDR model right for every company?**

No.

If the company is still testing product-market fit and wants to understand the sales process first-hand, an in-house salesperson may make more sense. Experiencing the learning process up close is valuable at this stage.

But if the product is clear, the target audience is defined and it's time to scale, trying to grow with a single person is like hitting the brakes.

**The real question is:** Are you trying to solve your sales problem by hiring a person, or by building a system?

These are two very different questions, and their answers lead to very different results.
